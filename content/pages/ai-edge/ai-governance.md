AI Governance for Healthcare Marketing Leaders
AI governance for healthcare marketing is not a policy binder. It is a working system of data boundaries, model selection, review checkpoints, and audit trails that you set up before deployment, because after an incident is too late. This page lays out that system the way an operator would build it.

The 2 a.m. Intake Call Is Now a Data Decision
Picture the moment governance actually protects. An admissions coordinator sits at a workstation at 2 a.m. A web form has arrived from someone researching treatment for a family member. The screen shows a name, a phone number, a preferred location, and a short note about what the family is trying to understand. The coordinator can follow the script in front of them. They cannot see the network calls leaving the browser.
Behind that form sits a stack of software decisions nobody in the room made that night. A chat widget may be logging message content to a vendor the coordinator has never met. An analytics tag may be passing the page URL, and sometimes form fields, to an ad platform. A lead-scoring model may be sending the inquiry text to a third-party API for enrichment. An auto-responder may be drafting a reply from a prompt that now contains the family's words. Each step is a data-handling decision. The person who opened the page disclosed something sensitive by seeking care information, often before they finished a sentence. Whether that disclosure stays inside the organization, or leaves for a vendor system nobody can audit, was decided by architecture choices the marketing team made months earlier. Those choices were rarely labeled as compliance decisions when they were made.
That is the argument of this page. AI governance in healthcare marketing is not a debate about whether to use models. Intake routing, ad bidding, analytics, call transcription, and content tools already use them. Governance is knowing, for every tool in the stack, three things: what data goes in, where the model runs, and who reviews what comes out. A memo the coordinator is supposed to remember at 2 a.m. is not a control. The control is a configuration that makes the unsafe path unavailable and puts the sanctioned path on the screen the coordinator already uses.
The same scene shows up in a hospital service-line team and in a multi-location behavioral health network. The stationery changes. The risk does not. Marketing Powered's founder is a former healthcare CMO who ran marketing for a treatment network that grew from 3 to 24 locations. Scale multiplies whatever the stack already does. Accidental model use scales as fast as intentional model use. The organizations that get hurt are rarely the ones that chose a model inside a reviewed workflow. They are the ones that inherited a model inside a pixel, a plugin, or an enrichment feature nobody inventoried.
A Policy Binder Does Not Stop a Pixel
Most healthcare organizations that say they have AI governance have a document. It tells staff not to paste patient information into public chatbots. It says model output should be reviewed. It says legal should be consulted before anything sensitive ships. All of that is directionally right. None of it stops a pixel from firing, strips identifiers from a prompt, or catches a clinical claim a model invented at 4:55 p.m. on a Friday.
A working system answers the same questions with configuration, not with trust.
* Which CRM fields may reach an external model API, and what removes the rest before the call is made?
* Which content types, including clinical claims, outcomes language, and service descriptions, cannot publish without a named human sign-off?
* Which pages carry which tags, and who approved each tag's data scope?
* When a model's output influenced a bid, a route, or a published sentence, where is that recorded, and for how long?
The distinction is commercial as well as legal. Hospital procurement teams, health-system privacy officers, and operators of multi-location provider groups ask marketing partners how models are used and where data flows. An answer backed by architecture is easier to defend than an answer backed by a PDF. Marketing Powered has been AI-native since 2022. Across that period, the useful pattern is that governance questions moved from the last slide of a vendor review to the first. A partner who cannot name data classes, deployment choices, and review owners is asking the buyer to carry risk the buyer will own.
Three failure modes show up more often than a missing policy paragraph. Shadow tools: a coordinator or copywriter using a public chatbot because it is faster than the sanctioned workflow. Inherited tags: a container copied from a consumer-brand template onto a condition page, still firing. Unlogged assistants: a drafting tool inside the CMS that rewrites service-line copy and leaves no record of the model, the prompt class, or the editor. A binder does not see any of the three. An inventory does.
The document still has a job. It states the standard. The system enforces the standard. When the two disagree, the system is what actually shipped.
The Regulatory Floor for Healthcare Marketing
Governance starts with constraints a marketing team does not get to negotiate away. Three of them shape almost every healthcare acquisition stack. Read them as design inputs, not as a legal opinion. Counsel owns the legal conclusion. Marketing owns the inventory counsel needs in order to reach it.
HIPAA and tracking technologies. The HHS Office for Civil Rights bulletin on online tracking technologies highlights the obligations of HIPAA covered entities and business associates under the Privacy, Security, and Breach Notification Rules when they use tracking technologies. The bulletin states that regulated entities are not permitted to use those technologies in a manner that would result in an impermissible disclosure of protected health information, and that disclosures of PHI to tracking technology vendors for marketing purposes, without individuals' HIPAA-compliant authorizations, are impermissible. [HHS guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html)
The same bulletin draws a line a marketing team can actually use. Tracking technologies on user-authenticated pages, such as a patient portal, generally have access to PHI. A vendor that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits PHI on behalf of a regulated entity is a business associate, and a business associate agreement is required. On unauthenticated pages, many tracking setups do not have access to PHI. A visit to a page about job postings or visiting hours, even with an IP address attached, is the bulletin's own example of activity that is not a disclosure of PHI. Some unauthenticated activity still can be. The bulletin points to appointment scheduling and symptom-entry tools, where the technology may collect an email address or a reason for seeking care together with identifying information. [HHS guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html)
A note at the top of the bulletin records a limit on how far that guidance goes. On June 20, 2024, the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas vacated the guidance to the extent it said HIPAA obligations are triggered merely by connecting an IP address with a visit to an unauthenticated public webpage addressing specific health conditions or healthcare providers. The bulletin states that HHS is evaluating its next steps in light of that order. [HHS guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html) For a marketing leader, the operating translation is narrower than a blanket fear of every pixel, and stricter than a hope that public pages sit outside the Rules. Form events, chat transcripts, call recordings, and authenticated portals are the high-risk surfaces. A general informational page is not automatically the problem the vacated theory described. The tag manager is still a compliance surface. Whatever the court narrowed, marketing disclosures of PHI without a valid authorization remain off limits. Every script on a health-services page deserves the same review as a vendor contract: vendor name, pages, fields collected, purpose, retention, and whether a business associate agreement is in place if PHI may be involved.
Platform certification. Google restricts the promotion of recovery-oriented drug and alcohol addiction services. Advertisers targeting certain locations may promote those services if certified by Google. Services unrelated to drug and alcohol addiction, such as impulse control disorders, behavioral addiction, or nicotine addiction, sit outside that specific restriction. [Google Ads policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031?hl=en) In the United States, advertising recovery-oriented drug and alcohol addiction services requires certification as an addiction services provider through the LegitScript Certification program, and it also requires Google certification. Not all drug and alcohol addiction services are eligible for LegitScript Certification. Those that are not eligible are not allowed to advertise in that category. [Google Ads policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031?hl=en) Read the policy against the actual service line. One certificate does not automatically cover a hospital's entire marketing program, and rules differ by country. Certification is not a one-time badge. Landing pages, claims, and intake practices have to keep matching the scope that was certified. A model that drafts outcomes language onto a certified landing page can put the certificate and the account at risk even when the media buyer never touched the copy.
Personalized advertising limits. Google treats health as a sensitive interest category in personalized advertising. The category includes physical or mental health conditions, and products, services, or procedures used to treat or manage them. Google's examples include counseling for depression, anxiety, and addiction. Advertisers promoting in that category can use predefined Google audiences. They cannot use advertiser-curated audiences, including Customer Match, your data segments, audience expansion, and lookalike segments, because those audiences may contain sensitive user signals. [Google Ads policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16701855?hl=en) Your data segments are the current name for what many teams still call remarketing lists. The practical build for health promotion is not a visitor-retargeting program.
Marketing Powered is LegitScript-aware and HIPAA-aware, and built for Google's sensitive healthcare vertical, where retargeting is not permitted. That constraint is a design input for [paid media](/services/paid-media/), not a cleanup item after launch. The founder managed $1.5 to $2M per month in Google Ads across 11+ behavioral and mental health accounts under these rules. The useful lesson from that volume is not a tactic list. It is that platform policy has to be in the brief before the first campaign is built, because the audiences other industries treat as standard are not available here.
Four Stages a Marketing Team Can Run
Here is the governance process as a sequence a marketing team can operate, left to right. Each stage has an owner and leaves an artifact. If a stage has no artifact, it did not happen.
Stage 1: Classify the data. Before any tool is evaluated, inventory what the marketing operation touches and sort it into tiers. A workable model uses three.
Public content covers service pages, educational articles, location hours, and other copy that does not identify a person seeking care. Operational data covers spend, impressions, creative performance, and conversion counts that have been aggregated so they do not identify a person. Protected or sensitive data covers form fields, call recordings, chat transcripts, CRM notes, insurance details, and page-visit patterns that can identify a person seeking care.
The tier determines everything downstream. Most governance failures start when sensitive data is labeled operational because it lives in a marketing tool. A free-text field on an admissions form is not campaign metadata. A call recording that names a person and a condition is not a quality-assurance file you can drop into a public transcription API. Write the tier on the inventory row, not in a separate policy no one opens.
Stage 2: Select the deployment. Match each tier to an allowed compute environment. Public content can flow through a commercial model API once the contract has been read. Operational data needs a review of training use, retention, logging, and subprocessors. Protected-tier data should touch only environments the organization controls. That is where owned, local infrastructure earns a place, covered in the next section. The artifact from this stage is an allowed-environment map: tool, data tier, where inference runs, and the date the terms were last read.
Stage 3: Review by humans, at named checkpoints. "A human looks at everything" collapses under volume, and then it collapses into nobody looking. Named checkpoints hold. Clinical claims and outcomes language require sign-off before publication. Changes to intake-routing logic require review before deployment. Model-drafted replies to inquiries require approval until the team knows the error pattern well enough to sample. Sampling before that pattern is known is hope. The artifact is a checkpoint list: content type, reviewer role, and what "approved" means. A checkbox with no reviewer name is not a checkpoint.
Stage 4: Audit and attribute. Log the model or endpoint, the version, the prompt class, the reviewer, the timestamp, and the surface the output reached: a published page, a sent reply, a bid change, a route. Keep that log in an environment appropriate to the data class. Do not copy protected inquiry text into a third-party observability tool in order to prove you were careful. Then connect outcomes. Marketing Powered tracks attribution to admission, not just lead, and the same discipline applies to governance. If you cannot trace an admission, a published claim, or a spend decision back through the system that influenced it, you cannot defend the system.
* Classify. The team decides which tier each field, tag, and tool touches. The proof is a data-tier inventory with an owner.
* Select deployment. The team decides which environment may process that tier. The proof is an allowed-environment map with a term-review date.
* Review. The team decides who signs off before the output ships. The proof is a named checkpoint list.
* Audit. The team decides how the chain is reconstructed later. The proof is a trace log tied to the surface reached.
Run in order. Skip classification and every later control is guessing. Skip review and the log only proves you published the error. Skip the log and review becomes a memory. Run weekly, these four stages turn a quarterly anxiety into a routine a marketing manager can chair without waiting for a crisis.
A single landing page is enough to test the sequence. Classify the page copy as public, the form fields and chat as protected, and the aggregate click count as operational. Map the ad tag to an environment that receives only the events counsel has cleared. Put outcomes language on the page behind a named review. Log the model version if a model drafted the hero or the auto-reply. If you cannot complete that pass on one page, you are not ready to roll the same stack across a health system.
Where the Model Runs Is the Control
Stage 2 deserves its own section because it is where most healthcare marketing teams are exposed. The default architecture of modern marketing tools is remote. Data leaves your environment, a vendor processes it, and output comes back. For a public-tier draft of a service description, that trade is often acceptable once the contract is understood. For a chat transcript that names a person and a condition, every remote call creates questions you have to answer. What does the vendor retain? For how long? Is the input used for training? Who are the subprocessors? What happens when the terms change after you signed?
There is a structural alternative. Marketing Powered operates owned, local AI infrastructure for HIPAA-aware inference, so sensitive workloads can run on hardware the firm controls rather than transiting a third-party API. Local models are not always the stronger model. Frontier commercial models are often better at raw drafting and reasoning, and plenty of marketing work belongs on them. The point is that deployment becomes a choice matched to data sensitivity, instead of a default inherited from whichever tool was easiest to turn on.
Be precise about what this does and does not mean. Owned infrastructure does not make a program HIPAA certified. No hardware or software purchase does that. HIPAA is a property of an organization's administrative, physical, and technical safeguards, not of a server. Owned infrastructure also does not mean every workload runs locally, and it does not change who owns client data. Client data ownership is separate from where a model happens to run. What owned infrastructure does is make one line enforceable with architecture rather than with policy language: identified sensitive marketing and intake text can be processed without leaving an environment the operator controls. That line is a concrete answer for a privacy officer, a board, or a regulator. The broader case for controlling where inference happens is on our [data sovereignty](/ai-edge/data-sovereignty/) page.
The commercial logic follows the risk logic. An impermissible disclosure is not a line item you can forecast in a media plan. Routing sensitive inference onto controlled infrastructure is a cost you can budget. Budget the control. Do not discover the boundary after a vendor's terms change.
Model Selection Starts With Risk
Most model-selection conversations in marketing teams compare capability: which model writes better, reasons better, or costs less per token. In healthcare marketing, that is the second conversation. The first is a risk conversation with four questions.
What are the data terms? Whether inputs are retained, logged, or used for training varies by vendor, by product tier, and over time. Terms that were acceptable at signing can change. Governance means someone owns a re-review date, and that the date is on a calendar rather than in a good intention. If the terms for the free tier differ from the terms for the enterprise tier, the inventory should say which tier you actually bought.
Can the model's behavior be held to a line? Healthcare copy has hard lines. No outcome promises. No diagnostic implications. Accurate, non-stigmatizing language about conditions and treatment. A model you can hold to system-level instructions, and check with automated screens for banned phrases and unsupported clinical claims, is safer than a marginally stronger model you cannot steer. Capability without a constraint is a faster way to publish a problem.
Is the output auditable? If a platform certifier, a privacy officer, or your own counsel asks why a page said what it said, you need the chain: prompt class, model version, output, editor, approval, and the surface it reached. Tools that cannot produce that chain fail this test regardless of writing quality. If the only record is a browser history on one employee's laptop, you do not have an audit trail. You have a person who might still work there.
Who is accountable for the error? Vendor terms assign responsibility somewhere. Read where. A large number of marketing tools disclaim the harms healthcare organizations care about most. If the disclaimer is the whole answer, the tool is a drafting aid, not a production system for patient-facing claims or intake decisions.
Marketing Powered's founder has served as a court-certified expert witness in advertising strategy. The discipline that role imposes is a useful build standard for this section: construct every model-assisted workflow as if you will one day have to explain it, under oath, to someone who is not inclined to be generous. Workflows built to that standard survive an audit. Workflows built only for speed survive until the first incident, and then the explanation is reconstructed from memory.
A Governed Admissions Marketing Room
Governance is easiest to judge when you can picture the room. From the hallway, a governed admissions marketing operation looks ordinary. Coordinators are on calls. A marketing manager is in a campaign dashboard. A content editor is working a publishing queue. The differences are in the plumbing and the habits, not in a poster on the wall.
The coordinator's screen shows inquiry details from the CRM. The assistant drafting the follow-up note runs in an environment the organization controls, so the inquiry text is not pasted into a public chatbot. Nobody is relying on the coordinator to remember a prohibition at the end of a long shift. The sanctioned tool is the one already open, and it is good enough that the public tool is not a temptation. That is the test. If the safe path is slower and worse, people will route around it, and your policy will describe a workflow that is not the one in use.
The marketing manager's dashboard shows spend next to admissions, not only leads, because attribution is tracked to admission and the team is judged on people who started care. That is a measurement choice. It is not a forecast of how many people will admit, and it is not a claim that a model caused a clinical result. It does change the conversation. A cheap lead that never reaches admission stops looking like a win once the dashboard refuses to stop at the form fill.
The content editor's queue flags treatment-outcome language and routes those drafts to clinical review before they can publish. The flag is a rule tied to phrases and claim types, not a vibe. Two articles can sit in the queue for an extra day. That delay is cheaper than a certified landing page that now implies a result the program cannot stand behind. The editor is not asked to be a clinician. The editor is asked to recognize the checkpoint and stop.
The weekly rhythm is equally plain. A standing review of the tag inventory against the approved list, including any tag a vendor added during a "quick install." A sample of model-drafted responses checked against tone, accuracy, and the ban on outcome promises. A check that certification scope still matches what the landing pages claim, especially after a model or a freelance writer touched the page. A log review confirming that model-touched outputs from the week still have a chain: version, reviewer, surface. None of this requires a new department. It requires a 45-minute meeting with the inventory open.
Hospitals often already have a privacy office that can own the classification standard and the escalation path. Multi-location networks often have the same tag copied across two dozen location pages and no named owner. The framework does not change. The first week of work does. In a hospital, the first meeting is usually with privacy, web, and the service-line marketer in the same room, arguing about one pixel. In a multi-location network, the first meeting is often the discovery that location pages were cloned from a template nobody has opened since the third site opened. Both meetings are useful. Both are Stage 1.
People move faster when the boundaries are explicit. Nobody is quietly wondering whether the tool in the browser is allowed. Teams that treat governance as friction get neither speed nor safety. Teams that treat it as infrastructure get a stack they can explain, a review path that does not depend on heroics, and a record they can hand to procurement without rewriting history the night before the questionnaire is due.

FAQ
Q: Do we need governance if we only use a model for drafting?
A: Yes, though the scope is lighter than a full intake automation program. Drafting still produces patient-facing claims, and that is where review checkpoints matter most. Clinical language, outcomes language, and service descriptions should not publish on model output alone. Most teams that believe they only use a model for drafting find, once they inventory the stack, that scoring, routing, bidding, call transcription, and analytics tools are also making model-driven decisions. Start with the inventory. Do not start with a belief about how narrow the stack is.
Q: Does owned or local infrastructure make us HIPAA compliant?
A: No. HIPAA compliance is a property of an organization's administrative, physical, and technical safeguards, not of any single tool or deployment choice. Owned, local infrastructure makes one control enforceable by architecture: sensitive data can be processed without leaving an environment you control. That strengthens a compliance posture. It does not replace policies, access control, vendor review, workforce training, or the business associate agreements the HIPAA Rules require when a vendor handles PHI. A privacy officer still has to look at the whole program.
Q: Can we retarget website visitors if we are careful about the audience?
A: Plan health promotion without advertiser-curated audiences. Google treats health as a sensitive interest category and does not allow Customer Match, your data segments, audience expansion, or lookalike segments when you are promoting in that category. Predefined Google audiences can still be used, because Google excludes sensitive user signals from them. [Google Ads policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16701855?hl=en) On top of platform policy, covered entities and business associates still have to analyze whether visit data sent to an ad vendor is PHI, and the HHS tracking bulletin treats marketing disclosures of PHI without a HIPAA-compliant authorization as impermissible. [HHS guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html) Strong healthcare acquisition programs are built on intent-based search, first-party conversion data, and landing page quality, not on a list of people who visited a condition page.
Q: Who should own governance: marketing, compliance, or IT?
A: Split the ownership by what each function can actually see. Marketing should own the tool inventory and the workflow checkpoints, because marketing knows where the tags, chat widgets, and drafting tools actually are. Compliance or privacy should own the data-classification standard and the escalation path. IT, or a technical partner, should own deployment architecture and logging. Governance fails when it is assigned wholly to any one of the three. Compliance cannot see the pixel a media buyer added on a Friday. Marketing cannot write the legal standard. IT cannot know which sentence on a landing page is an outcomes claim. A monthly meeting with the inventory on the table is the operating model. A named executive sponsor without that meeting is a title.
Q: How do we start if we have nothing in place today?
A: Start with Stage 1. Inventory every tool, tag, chat widget, transcription service, and integration that touches marketing or intake data, and classify what flows through each. The first pass usually surfaces exposures in tracking tags and chat tools, because those were installed to measure or to answer questions, not to handle PHI. Fix the exposures you can see, which often means removing a tag from form-confirmation and condition pages, or moving a chat log off a vendor that will not sign a business associate agreement. Then add review checkpoints and audit logging around the workflows that remain. Do not start by buying another model. You cannot govern a stack you have not listed.
Q: What should we ask a marketing partner before they touch the stack?
A: Ask for six things, in writing. Which data classes their tools will receive. Where inference runs for each class. Whether inputs are retained or used for training, and on what schedule those terms are re-read. Who reviews patient-facing output before it publishes. Whether a business associate agreement is in place if PHI may be involved. Whether they can produce the audit chain for a published sentence or a routing change.
