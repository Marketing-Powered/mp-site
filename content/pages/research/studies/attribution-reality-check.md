Attribution Reality Check: Greenfield Insights on Marketing Attribution
Most attribution reports answer a question nobody asked: which click was last. This marketing attribution study framework takes a greenfield approach instead, starting from verified business outcomes and working backward to test what your attribution model actually captures. The gap between the two is usually where the budget decisions go wrong.

Why Attribution Accuracy Decides Whether Leadership Trusts Marketing
Picture the quarterly budget meeting. The marketing lead presents a dashboard showing strong return on ad spend. The finance lead presents the ledger showing flat revenue. Both are looking at real numbers, and both believe they are right. The room has to decide which version of reality gets funded, and in most organizations that decision gets made on instinct, seniority, or fatigue rather than evidence.
This is the attribution problem in its practical form. Attribution models exist to connect spend to outcomes, and when they work, they show which channels generate qualified demand, which touchpoints move decisions forward, and where incremental dollars produce incremental customers. When they fail, they produce a confident, well-formatted distortion. Budget flows to the channels the model happens to credit, demand creation gets starved because it rarely wins the credit, and the pipeline erodes slowly enough that nobody connects the decline to the reporting that caused it.
The failure is rarely dramatic. It looks like a dashboard that stays green while admissions, signed engagements, or booked procedures drift down. It looks like branded search absorbing an ever-larger share of spend because it sits closest to the conversion. It looks like a CFO who stops asking questions about marketing because the answers never reconcile with the P&L. Attribution accuracy is not a reporting nicety. It is the thing that determines whether the marketing function keeps its seat in capital allocation conversations.
The rest of this page lays out what attribution models actually measure, why their defaults mislead in predictable directions, and a greenfield method any marketing or operations leader can run to test their own reporting against verified outcomes. It does not publish benchmark percentages, because attribution error is specific to your accounts, your sales cycle, and your tracking setup. A number measured on someone else's accounts is not evidence about yours. The method is what transfers.
What Attribution Models Actually Measure, and What They Assume
Every attribution model is a rule for distributing credit among observed touchpoints. That sentence contains the two failure points: the rule and the word observed.
Start with the rules. Last-click attribution gives all credit to the final tracked interaction before conversion. First-click gives it all to the earliest. Rules-based multi-touch models split credit by formula: evenly, by recency, or weighted toward the first and last touches. Each rule encodes an assumption about how buyers decide, and none of those assumptions was derived from your buyers. Google itself has retired most of these rules: the first click, linear, time decay, and position-based attribution models are no longer supported in Google Ads, and conversion actions that used them were upgraded to data-driven attribution ([Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6259715)). That consolidation tells you something important. The platform that popularized rules-based attribution concluded the rules were not worth maintaining.
Data-driven attribution is the better default because it distributes credit based on observed conversion path patterns rather than a fixed formula. But it is still bounded by the second failure point: it can only allocate credit among touchpoints it can see. It cannot see the referral from a discharged patient's family, the conference conversation, the physician's recommendation, the podcast mention, or the direct visit from someone who typed your name because a colleague said it out loud. Whatever share of your demand originates in places your tracking cannot reach, the model silently redistributes that credit to the touchpoints it can reach. The dashboard does not show a gap. It shows a confident allocation of 100 percent of the credit across a fraction of the journey.
This is the core reality check: an attribution model is a measurement of your tracking footprint, not a measurement of cause. Treating its output as ground truth means optimizing toward visibility rather than toward demand. Those are not the same thing, and in long-cycle or regulated markets the difference is large enough to change which channels look worth funding. If your reporting has never been tested against outcomes recorded outside the ad platforms, you do not yet know which side of that difference you are on. The Greenfield Method: Start From Outcomes, Not From Dashboards
Most attribution analysis starts inside the reporting tool: pick a model, pull the report, compare channels. A greenfield analysis inverts that. You start from a clean slate, as if no attribution model existed, and you begin with the outcomes your business actually records: admissions, closed engagements, completed procedures, signed contracts. Then you work backward and ask what the available evidence says about how each of those outcomes originated.
The inversion matters because it removes the model's framing before you evaluate the model. When you start from the dashboard, every question is shaped by what the dashboard chose to show. When you start from thirty verified customers and reconstruct their paths from CRM records, call recordings, intake notes, and the honest answer to "how did you hear about us," you build an independent reference point. Only then do you compare: what did the attribution model say about these same conversions, and where does its story diverge from the reconstructed one?
Three properties make this approach more defensible than another model comparison. First, it is anchored to outcomes, not to platform-reported conversions, so inflated or duplicated conversion counts cannot hide inside the baseline. Second, it is sized to be done by hand, which forces contact with the messy reality of individual journeys instead of averages. Third, it produces findings in the language leadership already trusts: these customers, this revenue, this origin. A finance team that will not accept a modeled ROAS figure will usually accept a reconciled list of its own closed business.
The honest limitation: a manual reconstruction is a sample, and samples carry noise. The point is not statistical precision. The point is direction. If your model credits one channel heavily and the reconstructed journeys rarely feature it, or if a channel your model reports as weak keeps appearing early in real journeys, you have found a distortion large enough to act on, and large enough to justify deeper measurement work.
How to Run Your Own Attribution Reality Check
This is the working process, as it actually runs. It takes a few focused working sessions, a CRM export, and one person with the patience to read call notes.
Step one: pull verified outcomes. Export a recent set of real outcomes from your system of record. Not leads, not form fills. Admissions, closed deals, booked revenue. Choose a window long enough to cover your full sales cycle. For a hospital service line or a treatment organization that may mean ninety days or more; for shorter cycles, thirty may suffice.
Step two: reconstruct each journey. For each outcome, gather every available signal: CRM touch history, call tracking records and recordings, intake or discovery notes, email history, and the stated answer to how the person found you. Write down the reconstructed path in plain language. Expect this to be slow for the first ten and fast after that, because patterns repeat.
Step three: pull the model's version. For the same conversions, record what your attribution setup claimed: which channel got credit, under which model, within which window. Where a conversion never appears in platform reporting at all, log that too. Invisible conversions are a finding, not a gap in the exercise.
Step four: reconcile and classify. Sort every case into one of three buckets: the model's story matches the reconstruction, the model credited the wrong origin, or the model never saw the journey. The distribution across those three buckets is your attribution reality. Most teams who run this exercise find the third bucket larger than expected, and that single discovery reframes every channel comparison they have been making.
What you do next depends on what you find. Wrong-origin cases usually point to tracking fixes: offline conversion imports so platforms learn from real outcomes rather than form fills, consistent call tracking, tighter CRM source capture. Invisible-journey cases point to structural limits of click-based measurement and argue for supplementing attribution with incrementality tests or aggregate modeling rather than tuning the model further. Where Attribution Breaks Hardest: Regulated and Long-Cycle Markets
Everything above applies to any business. In healthcare and other regulated markets, three additional forces widen the gap between attributed and actual.
First, personalization restrictions remove the connective tissue most attribution assumes. Google treats health content as a sensitive interest category and restricts how it can be used in personalized advertising, including the use of advertiser-curated audiences for health conditions ([Google Advertising Policies](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16701855)). In practical terms, the retargeting loop that lets consumer brands stitch a journey together across sessions is not available. Marketing Powered builds for Google's sensitive healthcare vertical, where retargeting is not permitted, and that constraint is a design input, not a footnote. Journeys in these markets are reconstructed from first-party data and conversion architecture, or they are not reconstructed at all.
Second, the conversion that matters happens far from the pixel. A form fill is not an admission. A call is not a scheduled procedure. When attribution stops at the lead, it rewards channels that produce contact volume rather than channels that produce patients or clients, and the two rankings often disagree. Marketing Powered tracks attribution to admission, not just lead, because the gap between those two reports is where regulated organizations quietly lose their budgets. The same principle applies to [hospitals and health systems](/who-we-help/hospitals-and-health-systems/) measuring service line campaigns, and to professional services firms measuring engagements rather than inquiries.
Third, scale exposes model behavior that small accounts never see. Marketing Powered's founder managed $1.5 to $2M per month in Google Ads across 11+ behavioral and mental health accounts, and earlier ran marketing as a healthcare CMO for a treatment network that grew from 3 to 24 locations. Operating at that spend level across many accounts makes one thing unmistakable: identically configured attribution behaves differently across accounts with different intake processes, call handling, and sales cycles. There is no universal correction factor. There is only validation against your own outcome data, which is the entire argument for the greenfield method. That operating history, including work as a court-certified expert witness in advertising strategy, is the vantage point this page is written from: what the founder observed, offered as method, not as a promise about your results.
What Better Measurement Looks Like Day to Day
Compare two versions of the same monthly review. In the first, the team projects a platform dashboard: channel ROAS, attributed conversions, cost per lead. Discussion orbits the numbers the platform chose to emphasize, and decisions follow the credit. In the second, the team puts two documents side by side: the platform's attributed view and a reconciled outcomes report built from the CRM, showing admissions or closed business by verified origin. The conversation changes immediately, because disagreements between the two documents are now visible, named, and assignable.
Reaching that second meeting takes infrastructure, and the components are well understood even if the assembly is work. Offline conversion feedback returns verified outcomes to the ad platforms so their optimization learns from reality instead of from form submissions. Durable first-party data capture, consistent source fields, and call tracking keep journeys reconstructable as browser-based signals continue to degrade. Periodic incrementality testing, holding back a region or segment and measuring what actually changes, supplies the causal evidence that no credit-allocation model can. Aggregate modeling can complement user-level attribution for organizations with longer cycles and larger budgets. None of these replaces attribution. Together they demote it from oracle to instrument, one input among several, each with known blind spots. Attribution Is a Discipline, Not a Dashboard Setting
The single insight to carry out of this page: a model's credit allocation is only as good as the verified outcomes it is reconciled against, and the reconciliation has to be repeated, because tracking decays, platforms change defaults, and intake processes drift. Teams that run an outcomes-first reality check on a regular cadence catch distortions while they are cheap. Teams that set a model once and trust it indefinitely find out about its failure from the revenue line, which is the most expensive possible report.
Perfect attribution is not achievable, and chasing it wastes the energy better spent on validation. Buyers research across devices, talk to people, and act on influences no tracking system records. The realistic goal is attribution that is accurate enough to rank your real options and honest enough to label its own blind spots. Questions, Answered
### What does a greenfield attribution analysis mean?
It means starting from verified business outcomes, such as admissions or closed deals, and reconstructing how each one originated before looking at what any attribution model claims. The model is then evaluated against that independent reconstruction instead of being trusted as the starting point. The approach removes the model's framing from the analysis and surfaces conversions the model never saw.
### Why not just publish the attribution error percentages?
Because attribution error is a property of a specific account: its tracking setup, sales cycle, intake process, and channel mix. A percentage measured elsewhere is not evidence about your accounts, and publishing one would invite exactly the false confidence this page argues against. The transferable asset is the validation method, which produces your number from your data.
### Which attribution model should we use in Google Ads?
Data-driven attribution is the practical default, and for most conversion actions it is now effectively the only multi-touch option, since Google no longer supports the first click, linear, time decay, and position-based models ([Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6259715)). The more important decision is what you do around the model: feeding it verified offline outcomes and checking its story against reconstructed journeys matters more than the model selection itself.
### How is attribution different for healthcare organizations?
Three ways. Health is a sensitive category in personalized advertising, so the retargeting loops other industries rely on are restricted ([Google Advertising Policies](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16701855)). The meaningful conversion, such as an admission, happens well after the tracked lead event, so lead-level attribution rewards the wrong behavior. And the data involved in reconciliation requires careful, privacy-aware handling, which shapes what tooling is appropriate.
### How often should we rerun the reality check?
Quarterly is a reasonable cadence for most organizations, with an additional run after any major change: a new tracking implementation, a platform attribution change, a new intake system, or a significant shift in channel mix. The exercise gets faster each time because the reconstruction process and source documents are already established.
