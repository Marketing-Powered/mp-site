Marketing Studies and Research
This hub collects Marketing Powered's published studies: documented examinations of how paid media, conversion systems, and attribution actually behave in regulated and multi-location markets. Every study states its scope, method, and limitations before it states a conclusion. If a number appears here, the page tells you where it came from and what it does not prove.

What this research hub is for
Most marketing research published by agencies is sales collateral wearing a lab coat. This hub exists for a different reader: the operator who has to defend a budget to a board, a CFO, or a compliance officer, and who needs to know whether a tactic holds up before committing spend to it.
The work behind these pages looks less like a brainstorm and more like an audit. Analysts sit with ad account change histories, call recordings, CRM admission records, and landing page test logs, and reconcile them against what the platforms reported. The gap between those two pictures is usually where the useful finding lives.
What counts as a study here
A study on this hub must clear a specific bar. It names its time window. It states the baseline before any change was made. It describes the intervention precisely enough that another practitioner could attempt it. It identifies the attribution method used and what that method can and cannot see. And it separates measured results from estimates, because those are different kinds of claims and deserve different levels of confidence.
Anything that cannot clear that bar is published as commentary or working notes, clearly labeled, not as a study. For applied examples of this standard, see [case studies](/results/case-studies/).
The evidence standard behind every number
Marketing Powered's evidence standard comes from experience that gets cross-examined, literally. The founder is a court-certified expert witness in advertising strategy, a setting where an unsupported claim is not a style problem but a credibility failure. That discipline carries into this hub: no invented statistics, no borrowed benchmarks presented as original findings, no outcomes attributed to a tactic when the data only shows correlation.
Where a study relies on platform policy or regulation, it links the primary source. Where data is incomplete, the study says so. A smaller, honest finding is worth more than a large, decorative one.
Research areas the studies cover
The hub concentrates on markets where the stakes and constraints are highest:
* Paid media in restricted verticals. The founder managed $1.5 to $2M per month in Google Ads across 11+ behavioral and mental health accounts, inside Google's sensitive healthcare vertical, where retargeting is not permitted. Google's policy on health in personalized advertising confirms that advertiser-curated audiences cannot be used for health condition targeting ([Google Ads policy help](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16701855)). Studies here examine what acquisition looks like when remarketing is off the table. Related service context lives at [paid media](/services/paid-media/).
* Attribution to real outcomes. Attribution tracked to admission, not just lead, is the measurement spine of this research. Studies test where lead-level reporting diverges from outcome-level truth.
* Multi-location growth. The founder ran marketing for a treatment network that grew from 3 to 24 locations, and the studies reflect multi-site questions: budget allocation, local intent capture, and intake capacity as a marketing constraint.
* Data control and owned compute. AI-native since 2022, with owned, local AI infrastructure for HIPAA-aware inference, Marketing Powered studies how data handling choices change what analysis is even possible. Background at [data sovereignty](/ai-edge/data-sovereignty/).
How a study gets built
Each study follows the same sequence. First, a question worth answering, usually surfaced by a recurring operational problem rather than a trending topic. Second, an evidence inventory: what data exists, who owns it, and what consent or policy constraints apply. Third, the analysis itself, with methods documented as they are used, not reconstructed afterward. Fourth, an internal claims review where every sentence with a number in it must point to its source. Only then does a draft become a published study.
How to read a study without being sold
Apply the same skepticism here that you would apply to any vendor's research. Picture two charts side by side: one shows a steep upward line with no axis labels, no dates, and no baseline; the other shows a modest improvement with its time window, method, and confounders stated. The second chart is the trustworthy one, even though the first one looks better in a pitch deck.
Check four things on any study, including these: the time window, the baseline, the attribution method, and the stated limitations. If a study on this hub ever fails that check, that is a defect worth reporting, and it will be corrected.

FAQ
