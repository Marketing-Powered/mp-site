Meta Ads Benchmarks 2026: Behavioral Health Study
Published Meta lead costs are real numbers from other campaigns, and they are the wrong planning number for a treatment organization. This study separates what those 2026 figures actually measure from the constraints Meta places on behavioral health advertising, then shows how to build an internal benchmark from a defined inquiry and an admission. It does not publish a pooled cost per lead or a cost per admission, because a single borrowed average would describe a different event than the one a census depends on.

Why a borrowed average fails here
Picture a Tuesday afternoon in a multi-site operator's conference room. A marketing lead, an intake supervisor, and a media buyer are looking at the same Meta screen on the wall. The account is "beating benchmark." Cost per result is well under a figure someone pasted in from a 2026 industry roundup. Form volume is up. The intake supervisor is not celebrating. Half the submissions never reach a clinical conversation. Several are from people asking about a level of care the organization does not offer in that state. A cluster came in overnight, sat until morning, and went cold. The media buyer did not fail a math test. The room is arguing about three different events while the screen shows one.
That scene is the reason a behavioral health Meta benchmark cannot start with someone else's cost per lead. Ads Manager will total whatever event the campaign is optimizing toward. Finance will hear "cost per lead" and treat it as a cost to acquire a patient. Intake will hear it and think of a person who can be scheduled. Those are not the same unit. When a public average is dropped on top of that confusion, the meeting gets a false referee. The account looks efficient because the event was shallow, or it looks expensive because the event was an admission the public study never counted.
A useful benchmark has to name the person, the setting, and the handoff. Who saw the ad. Which program and market the click was meant to serve. What the person had to do for the platform to record a result. What intake had to confirm before anyone called that person qualified. What "admitted" means in the census system, including whether a same-week transfer between locations counts once or twice. Without those definitions, a comparison across months, locations, or agencies is a comparison of labels.
The commercial problem is allocation, not curiosity. Behavioral health organizations buy Meta inventory to create qualified demand that intake can work, inside rules that limit what the ad can say and what the account can target. A benchmark that ignores those rules will recommend tactics the account is not allowed to run, or it will scold a campaign for missing a cost that belonged to retail, dentistry, or a lead-form average across unrelated local businesses. The rest of this page is a method for refusing that comparison and replacing it with one an operator can defend.
What Meta allows before any number
Eligibility comes before efficiency. If the advertiser cannot run the ad under Meta's standards, no cost benchmark applies, because there is no steady-state auction to measure.
For drug and alcohol addiction treatment ads aimed at people in the United States, Meta requires LegitScript certification and an application to Meta for permission to advertise. [[1]](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/drug-alcohol-addiction-treatment/) The policy states that advertisers can run those ads in the United States as long as they are certified with LegitScript and have written permission from Meta, and that eligible advertisers must show their activities are appropriately licensed. [[1]](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/drug-alcohol-addiction-treatment/) The covered services are not a vague "wellness" bucket. Meta's policy lists clinical addiction treatment services and sites that provide information about in-person treatment, online and in-person support groups, and crisis hotlines for people in recovery or people seeking information about addiction treatment. [[1]](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/drug-alcohol-addiction-treatment/) With certification and permission, the guidelines allow promotion of in-person addiction treatment, addiction hotlines, mutual support groups for addiction, and online addiction treatment. [[1]](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/drug-alcohol-addiction-treatment/)
That permission is the advertiser's to hold. An agency login does not substitute for it. Meta's application guidance says only certified addiction treatment centers may advertise to people in the United States, and that a business which is not eligible for LegitScript certification is not allowed to advertise drug and alcohol addiction services there. [[2]](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/787684018620830) The same guidance lists call centers that are not LegitScript-certified, online support groups that are not certified, lead generators, and sober living homes as not eligible to apply under that policy, because those categories are outside the certification program or lack the required certification. [[2]](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/787684018620830) A benchmark built from lead-seller traffic, or from a recovery residence that cannot clear that bar, is not a benchmark for a licensed treatment provider. It is a different business, often in a different risk category.
Meta also carves out ads that do not need that permission, including physical rehabilitation. [[1]](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/drug-alcohol-addiction-treatment/) A physical-therapy clinic and a residential addiction program should not share a "healthcare CPL" in a planning deck. The permission path, the creative limits, and the people the ads may address are not the same.
Creative limits change the auction even after permission is in place. Meta's personal attributes standard says ads must not assert or imply a person's race, ethnicity, religion, age, sexual orientation, gender identity, disability, physical or mental health including medical conditions, or several other personal attributes. [[3]](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/privacy-violations-personal-attributes) The standard's own examples allow "Depression counseling" and disallow "Depression getting you down? Get help now." [[3]](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/privacy-violations-personal-attributes) "You" and "your" are not banned. Tying them to a condition, or implying the advertiser knows the viewer's health, is the problem. Treatment creative that performs in a private review because it feels urgent can be the creative the policy rejects. A click-through rate from disapproved or borderline ads is not a baseline for next quarter.
A separate standard covers suicide, self-injury, and eating disorders. Ads must not encourage those harms, must not mock victims or survivors, and must not use vague depressive framing in a suicide or self-injury context. [[4]](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/suicide-selfinjury-eating-disorders) Emotional intensity is not a targeting strategy. It is a compliance review.
Audience construction changed in a way that breaks old comparisons. On November 9, 2021, Meta announced that starting January 19, 2022 it would remove Detailed Targeting options related to topics people may perceive as sensitive, including causes, organizations, or public figures related to health. The examples included "Lung cancer awareness," "World Diabetes Day," and "Chemotherapy." [[5]](https://www.facebook.com/business/news/removing-certain-ad-targeting-options-and-expanding-our-ad-controls) Any account history that depended on health-cause interests from before that removal is a different product. Treating 2021 interest-stack performance as the benchmark for a 2026 broad or custom-audience campaign mixes two delivery systems.
Do not confuse that with Special Ad Category self-identification. Meta's discriminatory practices policy requires advertisers running financial products and services, housing, or employment ads, in the United States or when targeting the United States, Canada, or certain parts of Europe, to self-identify in a Special Ad Category and use approved targeting options. [[6]](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/unacceptable-content/discriminatory-practices/) That paragraph does not describe behavioral health as the category being switched on. Health advertising is governed by the standards above, plus whatever data-source limits the account itself is showing. Those data limits are account-specific. This page will not generalize them into a single rule about retargeting, because Meta's in-account restrictions are not a number a blog can safely average.
One more policy is often pasted onto counseling ads by mistake. Meta's health and wellness standard requires ads that promote dietary, health, or weight-loss or weight-gain products and services, and ads for cosmetic products or procedures, to be targeted to people 18 or older, and it bans a listed set of claims, including claims to cure a specified set of incurable diseases. [[7]](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness/) That is a product and claims rule. It is not a substitute for the addiction-treatment permission process, and it is not a license to assume every therapy ad is reviewed under the weight-loss standard. Read the ad against the standard that matches what it promotes.
Marketing Powered's work in this vertical is built to be LegitScript-aware and HIPAA-aware. HIPAA-aware is a handling standard. It is not a certification, and it is not a promise that a workflow makes a campaign compliant. Certification stays with the advertiser. The benchmark question still starts in the same place: is this ad allowed to run, for this service, to people in this country, with this creative and this event?
Separate the event from the outcome
Most benchmark arguments fail on vocabulary. The words are shared. The events are not.
A cost per thousand impressions describes auction price for a placement and an audience under a given objective. A cost per click describes the price of a click, which may be a tap on a video, a profile, or a link. A cost per lead, in a published leads study, usually describes an on-platform lead event or a counted conversion inside a leads objective. A cost per qualified inquiry describes a judgment intake made after a conversation, a payer check, a level-of-care fit, and a geography check. A cost per admission describes a census event. None of these is a synonym for the others. Dividing one by another across different reports does not recover a hidden truth. It manufactures a fourth number that no system recorded.
The planning unit for a treatment organization is the outcome the organization is staffed to deliver. Marketing Powered tracks attribution to admission, not just to the lead, where the organization can connect acquisition to that outcome with data it is allowed to use. That is a design choice, not a published portfolio result, and it does not mean every click has a clean admission credit. It means a lead-only benchmark is the wrong stopping point.
The founder's own history is the practical reason to refuse a national average. As a former healthcare CMO, the founder ran marketing for a treatment network that grew from 3 to 24 locations, and managed $1.5 to $2M per month in Google Ads across 11+ behavioral and mental health accounts. That work sat in search, where the person often arrives with an active question, and it sat across many locations, where payer mix, capacity, and competition do not move together. It is not a Meta result, and it is not an agency client case study. It is evidence that scale makes a single cost figure less informative, not more. Eleven-plus accounts do not become one benchmark by being added together. Twenty-four locations do not share one admission cost because they share a logo.
Google and Meta also answer different demand. Marketing Powered is built for Google's sensitive healthcare vertical, where retargeting is not permitted. Search has to win on the query, the page, and the first response. Meta, when the advertiser is allowed to run, is an interruption in a feed, a story, or a reel. A person can be a high-intent searcher at noon and a low-intent scroller at night. Using a search cost per click as the "serious" benchmark and a Meta cost per click as the "cheap" one ignores that the clicks were purchased in different auctions for different jobs. The founder-managed Google Ads range is a reminder of budget scale in search. It is not a target CPM for Instagram.
Write the event dictionary before anyone opens a spreadsheet. A workable version has six lines, agreed by marketing, intake, and finance:
* Impression and click definitions stay inside the platform. They are diagnostics.
* The primary result is the event the campaign optimizes toward, named exactly as it appears in Ads Manager.
* The inquiry is the record intake can see, with a source and a timestamp.
* Qualified means a written rule: level of care offered, market served, minimum information present, and the exclusions intake already uses, such as out-of-area or a program the facility does not provide.
* Admission means the census definition, including how transfers, readmissions, and declined financial clearance are coded.
* The window is explicit. A seven-day click and a thirty-day inquiry-to-admission lag are different clocks. Reporting them as one ratio hides the lag.
Once those lines exist, external benchmarks have a job. They can show whether the platform event is in a familiar range for ordinary lead campaigns. They cannot set the admission budget.
What 2026 published lead costs measure
The most cited 2026 Meta figures in general marketing circles come from Facebook leads campaigns, not from addiction treatment and not from hospital service lines. WordStream's 2026 Facebook ads benchmarks put the average cost per click for leads campaigns across all industries at $1.80, down 6.25 percent from the prior year, and the average cost per lead at $27.39, down 0.98 percent. [[8]](https://www.wordstream.com/blog/facebook-ads-benchmarks-2026) In the same leads-campaign tables, health and fitness averaged a $1.84 cost per click and a $27.11 cost per lead, physicians and surgeons averaged $1.68 and $32.14, and dentists and dental services averaged $5.70 and $61.56. [[8]](https://www.wordstream.com/blog/facebook-ads-benchmarks-2026)
Conversion rate has to be read from the leads definition as well. WordStream's conversion-rate benchmarks put the average conversion rate across Facebook leads campaigns at 8.54 percent, with health and fitness at 7.98 percent, physicians and surgeons at 6.41 percent, and dentists and dental services at 6.07 percent. [[9]](https://www.wordstream.com/blog/conversion-rate-benchmarks) That conversion rate is a rate into the lead event those studies count. It is not a rate from landing-page view to admission, and it is not a show rate.

[[8]](https://www.wordstream.com/blog/facebook-ads-benchmarks-2026) [[9]](https://www.wordstream.com/blog/conversion-rate-benchmarks) These rows are adjacent labels, not a treatment-center cohort. Health and fitness is not residential care. Physicians and surgeons is not outpatient psychiatry by another name. Dental is on the table because it shows how far a regulated local service can sit from the all-industry lead cost inside the same study. If a planner needs a reminder that "healthcare" is not one auction, $61.56 against $27.39 is enough of a reminder, and it still is not a behavioral health figure. [[8]](https://www.wordstream.com/blog/facebook-ads-benchmarks-2026)
Year-over-year movement inside those health-adjacent rows is the second warning. WordStream reported that the largest decreases in cost per lead for Facebook lead ads included health and fitness, down 48.83 percent, and physicians and surgeons, down 32.29 percent, against an all-industry cost per lead that was nearly flat. [[8]](https://www.wordstream.com/blog/facebook-ads-benchmarks-2026) A team that updates only the all-industry average will miss a large move in the nearest public labels, and will still not have a number for detox, residential, intensive outpatient, or a hospital behavioral health service line. Last year's internal export can go stale for the same reason. A flat headline hides a category that moved.
Use these figures as a measurement check, not as a target. If an addiction-treatment account, properly permissioned, is producing on-platform lead costs that resemble the all-industry leads average, the first question is whether the event is a short form completion rather than a qualified inquiry. That question is a diagnostic. It is not an instruction to "get down to $27.39." Hitting a public lead cost by weakening the form is a way to look efficient while intake load rises. The dental row is useful for the opposite conversation. A higher cost per lead in a restricted local category is not, by itself, evidence of waste. It may be evidence that the category is competitive and the event is harder. You still cannot import $61.56 as the right cost for a treatment program. You can import the habit of reading the category before reading the average.
Do not back into a fake behavioral health cost by dividing a published cost per click by a published conversion rate. Those averages are reported separately. They will not reliably reproduce the published cost per lead, and they will not produce an admission cost no matter how carefully the division is done. Ecommerce benchmark reports that center on purchase return are a third mismatch. A checkout and a bed admission are different products. Pasting a retail cost per acquisition next to a treatment media plan compares a cart to a clinical decision.
Build the comparison on your own book
An internal benchmark is a table with rules, not a single bold number on a slide. The minimum useful cut is program by market by objective, over a window long enough to include intake lag and short enough that a policy or offer change does not hide inside it. Ninety days is a common operating window. It is not magic. If the admission cycle is longer than that, say so, and do not pretend a thirty-day platform attribution setting measured the census.
Columns that earn their place:
* Program, stated the way intake states it, not the way the ad set was nicknamed.
* Market, down to the level where capacity and competition actually differ. A state average conceals a city where three providers are buying the same families.
* Objective and optimization event, copied from the platform.
* Spend, results, and result rate, so delivery problems are visible before outcome problems.
* Inquiries that reached a human, not only platform results.
* Qualified inquiries, using the written rule.
* Admissions, using the census rule.
* Cost at each of those stages.
* A note on permission status, disapprovals, and any change in form, offer, or hours.
Leave cells blank when the data right is missing. A blank is more useful than a modeled number. This page will not fill those blanks with an industry substitute.
Qualification has to be written with intake, not reverse-engineered from whatever share of leads the media report wishes were real. A practical rule names the programs in market, the payer paths the facility can accept, the information required before a coordinator will schedule, and the exclusions that already end a call. If two locations use different exclusions, they do not share a qualified-inquiry benchmark. Averaging them creates a fictional location.
Multi-location operators feel this first. The same residential program in two states can have different travel patterns, different competitor density, and different evening coverage. The founder ran marketing across a network that grew from 3 to 24 locations. The lesson from that history is structural. Growth in location count increases the number of benchmarks you need. It does not create a national cost you can manage to. A dashboard that ranks locations on platform cost per result will punish the market with the harder clinical mix and reward the market with the easier form. Rank locations on the outcome the location is supposed to produce, and show the platform metrics beside that rank so media problems remain visible.
Hospitals and other healthcare organizations can use the same table with a different last column. A service line may care about a kept appointment, a referred case, or a scheduled procedure rather than a residential admission. Professional services firms often stop at a qualified consultation. The error to avoid is identical. Do not let the ad platform's result name become the business result because it was easier to export.
Finance should sign the definitions once, then stop renegotiating them inside each weekly meeting. If the definition changes, start a new row and keep the old row. A benchmark series that silently redefines "qualified" in March cannot be compared with January. That is a documentation problem, and it is the usual reason two agencies can both be "right" about the same account.
Attribution windows need the same discipline. Platform-reported results and CRM-reported admissions will not match. The gap is not automatically fraud, and it is not automatically proof that Meta "doesn't work." It is often lag, duplicate records, a call that started on a Meta click and was logged as direct, or a household in which one person clicked and another called. Reconcile a sample every month. Write down what you find. Over time that sample is a better benchmark adjustment than any public average, because it describes your phones, your forms, and your staff.
Read the limits before the rates
Delivery metrics still matter. They matter as diagnostics inside one account, not as trophies against a public table.
Cost per thousand impressions moves with season, placement, objective, and competition. A lower figure from Reels than from Feed, if you see one in your account, is not a reason to move the budget until you have followed those impressions to the inquiry definition you wrote down. Cheap attention that does not survive the form is a placement mix issue. Expensive attention that produces admissions in a market with open beds can be the correct buy. The comparison is internal and recent. It is not a 2024 screenshot.
Click-through rate is a creative and offer diagnostic under the personal attributes rule. An ad that says what the organization offers, where, and for whom, in the policy's allowed pattern, can be tested against another allowed ad. An ad that implies the viewer is in crisis, names the viewer's condition as if it were known, or leans on urgency around a family member's health is not a clever variant. It is a different risk, and Meta's examples already draw that line. [[3]](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/privacy-violations-personal-attributes) Keep rejected ads out of the benchmark set. They did not have a full chance to deliver, and including their early clicks flatters or punishes the account by accident.
Frequency is a saturation check, not a target this page will number. If frequency rises and qualified volume does not, the account is talking to a narrower set of people or the creative has stopped earning new attention. Investigate placement, geography, and whether the campaign is still allowed to find people outside a too-small custom list. Do not import a prospecting-frequency rule from an ecommerce cheat sheet. Those rules were written for catalogs and purchase cycles.
The landing page is the cost lever that does not require a looser audience. At any click price, the share of clicks that become a workable inquiry depends on load time, match between the ad's offer and the page, form length, and whether a person can call a staffed line. This is ordinary conversion work. It is also where behavioral health accounts lose the plot, because a page written to satisfy a keyword can still fail a frightened family member who needs a plain description of the next step. Describe the program, the levels of care, and what happens after the form. Do not imply an outcome, a recovery rate, or a bedside manner you have not been authorized to claim. Healthcare copy should stay accurate and non-stigmatizing. It should not diagnose the reader.
Measurement design is part of the benchmark, because a dirty event produces a dirty cost. Keep clinical detail out of event names, URL parameters, and ad-account custom conversions. "Residential admission, opioid use disorder, adolescent" is not a sensible pixel name. It is a health narrative sitting in a marketing tool. Use generic event names. Keep the clinical and identity detail in the system of record, under the organization's privacy policy and whatever business-associate arrangements actually exist. Marketing Powered is HIPAA-aware in how this work is framed. That sentence does not make a pixel, a form, or a call-recording vendor compliant. It means the benchmark should not be purchased by leaking the very information the organization is obligated to limit.
Where a workflow needs model help, such as sorting call dispositions or drafting a weekly variance note, the data boundary matters more than the model brand. Marketing Powered has been AI-native since 2022 and operates owned, local AI infrastructure for HIPAA-aware inference. That infrastructure is for inference the organization chooses to keep in its own environment. It does not mean every campaign runs on a local model, it does not mean client data is owned by the hardware, and it does not mean a model should invent a cost per admission when the CRM export is incomplete. A language model is a poor benchmark source. It will happily average the public leads figures with a half-remembered conference slide and present the blend as research. Refuse that output the same way you would refuse an unsourced agency deck.
Historical comparisons need a policy footnote. Campaigns from before the January 19, 2022 removal of health-related detailed targeting options are not a clean baseline for interest strategy. [[5]](https://www.facebook.com/business/news/removing-certain-ad-targeting-options-and-expanding-our-ad-controls) Campaigns from before the account received, lost, or renewed addiction-treatment permission are not a clean baseline for delivery. Campaigns that used a lead form with six fields are not a clean baseline for a form with sixteen. Write the footnote in the benchmark file. Future you will not remember it.
A review multi-location teams can run
The useful meeting is short and repetitive. It is a comparison of like with like, held by people who can change spend, creative, or intake coverage. It is not a tour of platform color scales.
Hold the review on a fixed cadence, with the same cuts every time. A workable hour looks like this.
Start with permission and delivery, not with cost. Confirm the ad account is still authorized for the services being promoted, that disapprovals are listed, and that no new ad is implying a personal health attribute. If that block is dirty, stop. A cost discussion on ineligible delivery is theater.
Move to the internal table for the current window against the prior window, same program, same market, same objective. Look at spend, platform results, inquiries, qualified inquiries, and admissions. Ask which stage moved. A rise in cost per result with a flat cost per admission can mean the event got stricter, which may be fine. A flat cost per result with a worse cost per admission usually means the loss is after the click: response time, payer mix, bed availability, or a definition drift in the CRM. Send that problem to the people who own it. Do not "fix" it with a broader audience.
Then, and only then, glance at an external reference such as the leads-campaign figures above. The question is narrow. Are we accidentally counting a shallow lead event and congratulating ourselves with a public average? If the answer is no, leave the public table. It has done its job.
Close with one decision. Shift budget toward a market and program where the admission math is holding and capacity exists. Hold budget where the creative test is unfinished. Pull budget where the event is shallow, the permission is unclear, or intake cannot work the volume. One decision is enough. A meeting that produces five new "tests" produces no benchmark at all, because next month's window will contain five confounds.
The chart worth putting on the wall is a category chart, not a false-precision league table. One column for published leads-campaign costs, labeled as other industries and other events. One column for this account's platform result. One column for qualified inquiries. One column for admissions or kept appointments. Empty cells stay empty. The point of the chart is to stop a $27 lead cost from being visually compared with a $1,800 story someone heard at a conference. This page does not supply that $1,800 story. If a prior deck used one, treat it as unverified unless your own census export produced it under a written definition.
The same review works for a hospital marketing team and for a professional services firm if the last column is renamed. A health system comparing orthopedic leads with behavioral health service-line inquiries will make the same mistake a treatment network makes when it averages detox and outpatient. The events differ, the policies differ, and the intake desks differ. Split the table. Hospitals also tend to have more than one phone number and more than one record system. Reconciliation, not a lower CPM, is the benchmark project.
What the review should refuse:
* Ranking agencies or coordinators on platform cost per result alone.
* Bonus plans that pay for beating a public CPL. People will buy the event the bonus counts.
* A blended "social CPL" that mixes Meta, other social platforms, and boosted posts.
* A year-over-year story that ignores the 2022 targeting removal, a permission change, or a form change.
* Any slide that presents ecommerce return on ad spend as the standard a clinical program failed to meet.
The founder is a court-certified expert witness in advertising strategy. The standard that role implies is the right standard for this meeting. If you would not want to explain the comparison under oath, do not put it in the forecast. Name the source, the objective, the dates, the event, and the exclusions. If a cell is an estimate, label it as an estimate. Measured facts and planning assumptions can live in the same workbook. They cannot live in the same cell without a label.
Use the benchmark as a planning input
A benchmark is an input to a budget conversation. It is not the conversation.
When the internal series is stable, the same program and market are clear, capacity is open, and cost per admission or cost per kept appointment sits inside what finance has already said the service can carry, that market has earned a test of more spend. The test should be large enough to read and small enough to stop. "More spend" is not a strategy by itself. Say what will change besides the budget: another allowed creative, a page fix, a change in hours covered, or a geographic split you have not measured cleanly.
When platform costs look fine and the qualified stage looks worse, the next dollar belongs to intake operations or to the offer, not to a new audience. Buying more of a broken handoff scales the break. When platform costs look poor and the few people who do arrive admit at a rate the census can support, the next dollar belongs to creative and page work under the policy, or to a decision that this market is simply expensive. Expensive is allowed. Unexplained is not.
When the account cannot show permission, cannot define qualified, or cannot join spend to an outcome, the next step is not a benchmark study from the open web. It is a measurement repair. Until that repair exists, any 2026 average will be used as a substitute for missing operations, and it will be wrong in a confident font.
This is also the point where owned infrastructure earns its keep, quietly. Variance notes, call-reason coding, and location-level rollups can be drafted with model assistance if the data path is one the organization accepts. Marketing Powered's local infrastructure for HIPAA-aware inference is relevant when the notes contain clinical detail and the organization does not want that detail sent to an arbitrary external endpoint. It is irrelevant as a slogan on a media plan. The benchmark still comes from the ads account and the census, joined by rules a person can read.
Do not ask this page, or any model, for "the" 2026 behavioral health Meta CPL. The honest answer is that the public record cited here measures other categories and a lead event, and that a treatment benchmark has to be built from the operator's own allowed campaigns. Anyone offering a single cost per admission for the vertical, without a dated method, a permission screen, and a census definition, is offering a slide.
Questions operators actually ask
### What is the average Meta cost per lead for behavioral health in 2026?
There is no single average this page will stand behind. WordStream's 2026 leads-campaign average across all industries is $27.39, with health and fitness at $27.11, physicians and surgeons at $32.14, and dentists and dental services at $61.56. [[8]](https://www.wordstream.com/blog/facebook-ads-benchmarks-2026) Those are not addiction-treatment or mental-health admission costs. They are a reference for a different event. Your planning number is the cost of the inquiry and the admission you defined, on campaigns you are allowed to run.
### Can an addiction treatment program advertise on Meta?
In the United States, yes, if the advertiser is LegitScript-certified and has written permission from Meta. [[1]](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/drug-alcohol-addiction-treatment/) Meta's policy covers in-person treatment, hotlines, mutual support, and online addiction treatment once that bar is cleared, and it does not treat every health advertiser as already cleared. ### Should we manage to the all-industry leads figure?
No. Use it only to ask whether your counted event is as shallow as a typical leads-campaign conversion. The all-industry conversion rate into that lead event is 8.54 percent in WordStream's leads-campaign benchmarks. [[9]](https://www.wordstream.com/blog/conversion-rate-benchmarks) An admission rate will not look like that, and it should not. Managing to the public lead cost tends to shorten forms and inflate volume that intake cannot use.
### Is behavioral health a Special Ad Category on Meta?
Meta's discriminatory practices policy requires Special Ad Category self-identification for financial products and services, housing, and employment ads in the regions it names. [[6]](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/unacceptable-content/discriminatory-practices/) Behavioral health is governed by other rules, including addiction-treatment permission, personal attributes, and the removal of sensitive detailed targeting options. [[1]](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/drug-alcohol-addiction-treatment/) [[3]](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/privacy-violations-personal-attributes) [[5]](https://www.facebook.com/business/news/removing-certain-ad-targeting-options-and-expanding-our-ad-controls) Do not assume a checkbox you do not see is the control that matters. Read the standards that match the ad, and read the data-source notices inside the account.
### Why does older account history no longer match?
Several breaks are documented, even before your own form changes. Health-related detailed targeting options were removed starting January 19, 2022. [[5]](https://www.facebook.com/business/news/removing-certain-ad-targeting-options-and-expanding-our-ad-controls) Permission, creative disapprovals, objective changes, and intake coverage changes each reset the series. Compare windows that share a policy status and an event definition. If they do not share those, report them side by side and do not average them.
### What should a multi-location team benchmark instead?
Program, market, objective, qualified inquiry, and admission or kept appointment, over a stated window, with blanks where the join is missing. Location count is a reason to split the benchmark. It is not a reason to publish one network-wide cost. The same pattern fits a hospital service line or a professional services practice if the final outcome column is renamed to the event that practice actually delivers.
### Does a lower CPM mean the acquisition program is healthier?
No. CPM describes inventory price. Healthier, in this vertical, means allowed delivery that produces inquiries intake can work and outcomes the census recognizes, at a cost finance has accepted for that service. A cheap thousand impressions can be the most expensive line in the plan if the people who arrive cannot be admitted and the staff time is real.
If the account's definitions are written down and the export still does not explain where spend becomes an admission, the useful next step is an acquisition review of that join, not another public average.
