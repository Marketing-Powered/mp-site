AI Ownership: Why We Chose to Own Our AI Stack
A marketing stack that lives inside other companies' chat windows is easy to start and hard to defend. Prompts, account notes, and reporting rules leave the building, and the model on the other end can change without a release your team approved. Ownership is the alternative: run inference on infrastructure you control, keep the workflows, and stop treating every useful task as another subscription.

Where rented marketing tools start to fail
A growth marketing manager opens a Monday review to a row of browser tabs and a renewal spreadsheet that does not match the work. One tab holds ad-copy variants for a hospital service line. Another holds call summaries a coordinator pasted in from the intake desk. A third clusters search terms exported from an ads account. A fourth is a slide that still needs a clean table before leadership meets. None of these products share a schema. The manager copies, pastes, and checks whether last month's seat is the one the team still uses.
That is a rented stack. Each license looked reasonable when someone bought it to clear one bottleneck. Together, the licenses produce a department that cannot describe its own process without naming vendors. Classification rules for high-intent queries live in a project the vendor hosts. Constraints for regulated language live in a prompt library nobody exported. Cancel the subscription in a budget review and the capability does not come home. The department keeps the invoice.
Metering changes behavior before it changes strategy. Teams run fewer variants near the end of a usage cycle. Analysts sample a query report instead of reading the full file. The second pass, the one that catches a mismatch between the ad, the page, and the call script, is the pass that gets skipped because it costs another increment. Ownership starts by refusing to let a billing meter decide how thorough the marketing system is allowed to be.
What owning the stack actually means
Owning the stack is a narrower claim than the phrase suggests, and the narrow version is the useful one. It does not mean a marketing organization pretrained a foundation model. It does not mean every draft, every research note, and every campaign runs on local hardware. It does not mean client records become the firm's property because the firm bought equipment. Those are separate questions. Mixing them up is how infrastructure talk becomes a compliance problem.
Four things are actually ownable.
The runtime. A named person can point to where a production job executes, who can open that environment, and what remains if a vendor account is closed. A username on a commercial chat product is access. It is not custody.
The weights you choose to keep. Open-weight models can be stored and called by version. Production can stay on a checkpoint already scored against your tasks while a separate environment tries a newer release. You are not required to absorb a silent model swap because a host retired a checkpoint to free capacity.
The orchestration. The layer that compounds is the sequence around the model: which fields are pulled, which fields are stripped, which rules the output must pass, and where the result is written. If that sequence is code and configuration your team can read, it survives a user-interface redesign. If it is a series of clicks inside someone else's product, it does not.
The boundary. Public research and non-sensitive drafting can sit on a commercial API when the contract matches the use. Account strategy, non-public performance data, and anything that could be protected health information need a different path. Ownership is the ability to enforce that split instead of discovering it after a paste.
Client data ownership stays with the client. Hardware ownership stays with whoever bought the hardware. Where a business associate agreement is required, that agreement still has to describe permitted uses. Buying a server does not rewrite the contract.
Model control when hosts change the rules
Hosts change models for capacity, safety, and product reasons that are rational for the host and disruptive for a workflow that assumed last quarter's behavior. A classification job that returned a stable set of labels can start returning prose. A filter can refuse a plain description of a service your ads are still allowed to run, provided the campaign itself follows platform rules. Even when a retirement schedule is published, the engineering work remains: retest every step that parsed a format.
An owned runtime does not freeze quality. Newer weights should be tested. The difference is who schedules the cutover. Pin the production checkpoint. Score a candidate on a fixed sample of work the team already does: grouping search themes, drafting location-page briefs from approved facts, checking whether a page still matches the query that paid for the visit. Promote the candidate if it wins on those tasks. Leave production in place if it does not.
The same discipline shows up in media, where platform rules already limit what a rented audience product can do. Google treats Health as a sensitive interest category. That category includes physical or mental health conditions, products, services, and procedures used to treat or manage chronic conditions, health issues tied to intimate body parts or functions, invasive procedures such as surgery or injections, and disabilities, including some content aimed at a primary caretaker. [[1]](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16701855?hl=en)
Advertisers promoting products and services in a sensitive interest category cannot use advertiser-curated audiences. Google's non-exhaustive list includes Customer Match, your data segments, audience expansion, and lookalike segments. Predefined Google audiences can still be used, because Google configures them without those sensitive user signals. [[2]](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/143465?hl=en)
Discovery and Demand Gen campaigns use advertiser-curated audiences by default and may be restricted from serving when the offer falls in a sensitive interest category. Custom segment audiences that use sensitive creative assets or point to sensitive landing pages are limited too: Google states they will only serve with Display campaigns to non-sensitive audiences or contextually, and that other campaign types using those custom segments with sensitive creative or landing pages will not be eligible to serve. [[2]](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/143465?hl=en)
That is the operating environment this firm is built for. Marketing Powered is LegitScript-aware and HIPAA-aware, and it is built for Google's sensitive healthcare vertical, where retargeting is not permitted. Attribution is tracked to admission, not just to the lead. The design comes from operator history. The founder, a former healthcare CMO, ran marketing for a treatment network that grew from 3 to 24 locations and managed $1.5 to $2M per month in Google Ads across 11+ behavioral and mental health accounts. The founder is also a court-certified expert witness in advertising strategy. A workflow that cannot survive a model change is a weak place to run accounts that already cannot lean on remarketing lists.
Rented tools beside an owned runtime
Set the two models side by side and the decision gets easier to brief. This is the comparison a leadership review should be able to walk through without a vendor in the room: a simple landscape of questions, not a score that pretends one column wins every row.
* Who changes the model; Rented interface: The host, on its schedule; Owned inference: Your team, after a test
* What the invoice measures; Rented interface: Seats, tokens, or both; Owned inference: Hardware, power, upkeep, and staff time
* Where a prompt goes; Rented interface: To the vendor's service; Owned inference: To the runtime you operate
* What remains if you cancel; Rented interface: Exports, if the vendor allows them; Owned inference: Weights you stored, code you wrote, logs you retained
* Fit for sensitive inputs; Rented interface: Depends on the contract and the product settings; Owned inference: Depends on the boundary you configured
The rented column wins on start time. A team can be drafting the same afternoon, and for public, non-sensitive work that speed is often the right trade. The owned column wins on repeatability and on the ability to refuse a path. It also carries costs the table should not hide. Someone has to patch the runtime, watch capacity, and keep the orchestration from becoming a private script only one person understands. A comparison that ignores maintenance is a sales sheet.
What the chart cannot show is judgment. A hospital marketing director comparing the two is not choosing a doctrine. The director is choosing which jobs may depend on a host, and which jobs have to keep working if that host changes price, filters, or terms. Professional-services firms face the same split when the input is a proposal, a fee discussion, or a non-public pipeline note rather than a clinical record.
How the cost moves once hardware is in
Once hardware is in service, the invoice shape changes. You are no longer buying each additional pass as a token charge. You are paying for equipment, power, upkeep, and the people who run the jobs. Those costs are real. Calling the next inference run free would be sloppy. Electricity, review time, and failed jobs still cost money. The change that matters is the incentive. A team can run the full query file, then run it again after a landing-page fix, without watching a usage bar and treating the second run as optional.
That matters in multi-location programs, where the useful cut is by facility, service line, and query, not by one blended account average. It matters in [paid media](/services/paid-media/) when the question is which themes still deserve budget after the click, not how many drafts a seat can produce. Infrastructure does not, by itself, produce a better acquisition cost. Offer, page, intake, and follow-up still decide whether demand becomes revenue. What changes is one reason those later steps get a thinner brief.
Rented APIs remain rational for burst work and for tasks where a hosted model is clearly stronger and the input is appropriate to send. The economic mistake is using that API as the system of record for every repetitive job, then acting surprised when the monthly bill punishes thoroughness.
Data boundaries in regulated markets
Regulated markets make the boundary concrete. Under HHS guidance, a business associate is generally a person or organization that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits protected health information on behalf of a covered entity, or that provides certain services involving the disclosure of that information. A subcontractor that does the same on behalf of a business associate is a business associate as well. [[3]](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/business-associates/index.html)
HHS includes, among its examples, a cloud service provider engaged to create, receive, maintain, or transmit electronic PHI on behalf of a covered entity or business associate. [[3]](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/business-associates/index.html) A separate HHS FAQ states that a covered entity or business associate may use a cloud service to store or process electronic PHI if it enters a business associate agreement with that provider and otherwise complies with the HIPAA Rules. [[4]](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/may-a-hipaa-covered-entity-or-business-associate-use-cloud-service-to-store-or-process-ephi/index.html)
Read those points together. A cloud model is not automatically off limits, and a local model is not automatically acceptable. The agreement has to cover the features in actual use. The risk analysis has to include the system. Staff still have to keep identifiers out of a prompt that does not need them. A consumer chat account with no agreement is the wrong default for material that could be PHI, whether the interface feels private or not.
Marketing Powered operates owned, local AI infrastructure for HIPAA-aware inference. That is a design choice, not a certification, and not a statement that every workload stays on that infrastructure. Some jobs use commercial models when the data and the contract support it. Local inference exists so sensitive internal work is not forced through a general web tool. It does not replace a covered entity's own obligations, and it does not transfer ownership of client data to the firm that owns the hardware.
The same boundary applies outside clinical records. Hospitals, health systems, and professional-services firms hold strategy, referral patterns, and non-public performance data that should not wander through a shared prompt history. First-party data is useful only if the path from acquisition to outcome is defined. In treatment marketing, the outcome this work is built to track is admission, not a form fill that nobody can tie to a decision. Where LegitScript certification applies to a treatment advertiser, that requirement is a separate track from model hosting. It still belongs in the same operating plan. A workflow that ignores the ad platform's certification rules is not a system.
How an operator runs a local job
The concrete version of this work is unglamorous, which is the point. An operator sits with a job definition, not a blank chat box. The job names the source file, the fields that must be dropped before the model sees the row, the model version, the checks the output has to pass, and the table the result is written to. The operator runs it, reads the failures, and changes the rule or the input. There is a log. There is a person who can explain the log.
A typical internal job follows a short sequence.
Separate the inputs before anyone writes a prompt. Public pages and published policies are one bucket. Account exports are another. Anything that might identify a patient or a caller is a third, and it does not enter a general tool.
Remove what the task does not need. A search-term classification job needs the query, the campaign, and the outcome fields the team is allowed to use. It does not need a name, a phone number, or a free-text intake note.
Pin the weights in the job, not in someone's memory. If the output format changes, you can see whether the model version changed.
Put a check a person can read between the model and the system of record. A label outside the allowed set fails. A number that does not match the source file fails. The failure is a row, not an impression.
Write the result where analysts already work. A transcript trapped in a chat window is not an operating record.
Marketing Powered has been AI-native since 2022 in this practical sense: models are part of how the work is produced, under human governance, in service of paid media, conversion, multi-location programs, and measurement. A new interface is not a strategy. When a hosted model is the better instrument and the data may go there, it is used on purpose. When it is not, the job stays on infrastructure the firm operates.
Questions before you commit to ownership
### Does ownership mean training a foundation model?
No. Training a foundation model is a different capital project. Ownership, as this page uses the word, means control of the runtime, the checkpoints you rely on, the orchestration, and the data boundary. Most marketing departments will never train a base model. They do not need to in order to stop renting every repetitive job.
### Does a local model make a healthcare workflow compliant?
No. HHS describes when a vendor that handles protected health information is a business associate and when an agreement is required, including for a cloud service that stores or processes electronic PHI. [[4]](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/faq/may-a-hipaa-covered-entity-or-business-associate-use-cloud-service-to-store-or-process-ephi/index.html) A model on equipment you control can reduce the number of outside parties that see a given job. It does not complete a risk analysis, set access controls, or authorize a use the Privacy Rule does not allow. If a vendor still creates, receives, maintains, or transmits that information for you, the agreement question remains.
### Can a health advertiser use any Google audiences?
For promotion in the Health sensitive interest category, advertiser-curated audiences are not available. That includes Customer Match, your data segments, audience expansion, and lookalike segments. Predefined Google audiences can be used. Discovery and Demand Gen need extra care because they use advertiser-curated audiences by default. [[2]](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/143465?hl=en) Policy text changes. The live Google Ads policy pages are the reference before a campaign is rebuilt.
### Should every task leave commercial APIs?
No. Move the jobs that are repetitive, that need a pinned result, or that should not leave your boundary. Keep a commercial API when the input is appropriate and the hosted model is the better tool. A blanket migration is how teams buy hardware and then recreate the same ungoverned paste habit on a local screen.
### What should leadership ask before funding this?
Ask where the prompt executes, who can read logs, which model version production uses, what is retained if a vendor account closes, and which tasks are forbidden in a general chat tool. Ask how outputs are checked before they reach a media account or a report. If the answers are product names rather than paths and controls, the stack is still rented.
