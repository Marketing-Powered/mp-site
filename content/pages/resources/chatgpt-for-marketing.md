ChatGPT for Marketing: Where It Works, Fails, and How to Govern It
Most teams evaluating ChatGPT for marketing are asking the wrong question. The question is not whether the model writes well; it is which decisions you are willing to let it touch, with what data, under whose review. This page maps the boundary: where a general-purpose model earns its keep, where it quietly fails, and the governance that keeps the second category from costing you.

What ChatGPT Does Well Inside a Marketing Team
Picture the actual workflow. A marketing manager at a twelve-location healthcare group opens Monday with forty ad variants to refresh, six location pages that need localized copy, a webinar transcript to cut into an email sequence, and a competitor's new pricing page to summarize for the Thursday leadership call. That is the terrain where ChatGPT is genuinely useful, because every one of those tasks shares three properties: the input is supplied by a human, the output is checked by a human, and a mediocre first draft still saves an hour.
In practice, the model performs reliably on:
* Volume drafting against a known structure. Ad variant expansion, meta descriptions, subject line batteries, FAQ phrasing. The strategy already exists; the model multiplies execution.
* Transformation, not creation. Turning a transcript into an outline, a long article into a social summary, a spec sheet into plain language. The facts come from your document, which sharply reduces invention risk.
* First-pass analysis of supplied material. Summarizing call notes, clustering survey verbatims, flagging tone problems in a draft. The model reads faster than any coordinator.
* Unblocking. A rough opening paragraph, five headline directions, a counterargument to stress-test. Blank-page cost is real, and this removes it.
Notice what unites these: ChatGPT is operating as a transformer of material you already own and trust. The marketer stays the author of record. Teams that frame it this way get consistent value. Teams that frame it as a replacement for the person who knows the market get the failures described next, usually without noticing until the damage compounds.
Where It Breaks: Strategy Is Not a Writing Task
The failure mode that costs the most money is not a factual error in a blog post. It is the strategic disconnect: fluent output that reads like a plan but encodes no actual knowledge of your market, your margins, your intake capacity, or your competitive position.
Ask ChatGPT for a marketing strategy and you will receive a confident, well-organized document recommending roughly what every organization in your category is told: post consistently, invest in SEO, nurture leads, test creative. None of it is wrong. None of it is a decision. Strategy is choosing what not to do given your specific unit economics, and the model has never seen your unit economics. It cannot know that your Tuesday admissions bottleneck makes lead volume on Mondays worthless, or that one service line carries triple the contribution margin of another, or that a competitor just exited a metro and the window to buy cheap impression share closes in weeks.
There is a second, subtler failure: averaging. Large language models produce the center of the distribution of their training data. Positioning built that way is, by construction, the same positioning available to every competitor with the same prompt. Differentiation generated from an average is a contradiction.
The founder of Marketing Powered ran marketing as a healthcare CMO for a treatment network that grew from 3 to 24 locations. The decisions that drove that growth were sequencing decisions: which market, which service line, which month, funded by which reallocation. Not one of them could have been outsourced to a text predictor, because each depended on operational facts that never appear in writing anywhere a model could learn them. Use the model to pressure-test a strategy you wrote. Do not ask it to supply one.
Accuracy Failures Cost More in Regulated Markets
Every organization pays something for model errors. Regulated organizations pay more, because the error surface includes legal exposure, not just embarrassment.
A fabricated statistic in a SaaS blog post is a correction. A fabricated clinical claim on a treatment page is a liability event. A hallucinated citation in a hospital's patient education content undermines the exact trust the content exists to build. An invented regulation quote in a professional services whitepaper can be cited back to you by a prospect's counsel. The cost of a wrong sentence scales with how regulated the reader's decision is.
The practical consequence: review intensity should be assigned by claim risk, not by content type. A useful three-tier rule:
* Low risk, light review. Social captions, internal summaries, structural edits. Spot-check.
* Medium risk, line review. Service pages, emails, ad copy. A human verifies every factual assertion against a source they can name.
* High risk, expert review. Anything with clinical, legal, financial, or outcome claims. A qualified reviewer signs off, and the draft's claims are traced to primary sources before publication.
Teams fail here in a predictable way: they calibrate review intensity in the first month, find the output "mostly fine," then quietly stop checking. The model's error rate did not drop; the vigilance did. The tenth hallucination ships because the first nine were caught and everyone relaxed. Governance that depends on sustained human alertness without a checklist is not governance. It is hope with extra steps, and in healthcare, finance, and law, hope is not a compliance posture.
What Happens to the Data You Paste In
Walk through a concrete scene, because this is where abstract policy becomes a real incident. An intake coordinator wants help drafting a follow-up email. The coordinator pastes the lead record into a free ChatGPT window: name, phone number, the service the person asked about, a note about their insurance. Thirty seconds of convenience. What actually happened to that data depends entirely on which product tier was in use, and almost no one on a marketing team can answer that question from memory.
The tiers are not interchangeable. OpenAI states that data from its consumer ChatGPT versions for individuals is used for model training by default, while business products such as ChatGPT Enterprise, ChatGPT Business, and the API platform are excluded from training by default ([OpenAI enterprise privacy](https://openai.com/enterprise-privacy/)). On the API side, OpenAI may retain inputs and outputs for up to 30 days for abuse monitoring, with zero data retention available for eligible use cases ([OpenAI enterprise privacy](https://openai.com/enterprise-privacy/)). For organizations handling protected health information, OpenAI offers Business Associate Agreements in support of HIPAA compliance for qualifying services, which must be requested and executed rather than assumed ([OpenAI BAA process](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/8660679-how-can-i-get-a-business-associate-agreement-baa-with-openai)).
Three operational rules follow:
* No PHI, PII, or client-confidential data in consumer-tier tools. Ever. This is the single highest-leverage policy a marketing leader can set this week.
* Procurement decides the tier; policy decides the inputs. An enterprise contract with training exclusions and, where needed, a BAA is a precondition for any workflow touching sensitive data, not an upgrade to consider later.
* Assume prompts are discoverable. Write every prompt as if it could be read back in an audit, because under retention windows and legal holds, it can be.
Platform Policy Does Not Care Who Wrote the Ad
A quiet assumption has crept into AI-assisted marketing: that generated copy somehow inherits safety because a sophisticated tool produced it. Ad platforms make no such allowance. The advertiser is responsible for compliance regardless of authorship, and in sensitive categories the rules constrain more than wording.
Google restricts personalized advertising for sensitive interest categories, including health: advertisers promoting in these categories cannot use advertiser-curated audiences such as their own data segments, which removes remarketing from the toolkit ([Google Ads personalized advertising policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/143465)). Google maintains a specific policy for health in personalized advertising covering how these restrictions are enforced and remediated ([Google health personalization policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16701855)).
This matters for ChatGPT governance in two ways. First, the model does not know these rules apply to you. Ask it for a healthcare remarketing sequence and it will happily produce one, complete with audience recommendations a healthcare advertiser cannot lawfully-within-policy run. The output is fluent, structured, and unusable, and a junior buyer may not know the difference. Second, generated ad copy can drift into claim territory that triggers disapproval or account-level review: superlatives about outcomes, implied diagnoses, before-and-after framing.
Marketing Powered builds for Google's sensitive healthcare vertical, where retargeting is not permitted, and the firm is LegitScript-aware and HIPAA-aware by design. The founder managed $1.5 to $2M per month in Google Ads across 11+ behavioral and mental health accounts, and the durable lesson from that scale is simple: platform policy is an input to strategy, not a post-production check. Any AI workflow that generates ads without encoding the category's restrictions is manufacturing rework. Rented Intelligence Versus Owned Infrastructure
Set two architectures side by side, because the comparison clarifies what you are actually buying.
Rented, multi-tenant: your prompts travel to a third party's servers, under terms the vendor can revise, with retention windows the vendor operates, at prices the vendor sets, with capabilities that change when the vendor swaps models. For drafting social copy, none of that matters much. The data is low-sensitivity and the task is disposable.
Owned, local: inference runs on hardware the organization controls, inside its own network boundary. Data does not transit a third party by default. Retention is whatever you configure. Model behavior changes when you decide to change it, not when a vendor ships an update that silently alters your outputs mid-campaign.
Neither column wins outright, and anyone selling a single answer is skipping the engineering. The honest decision rule is data sensitivity and process criticality. Public-facing draft copy belongs in whichever tool is fastest. Workflows that touch lead records, call transcripts, intake notes, or anything adjacent to PHI deserve infrastructure where the data path is short, inspectable, and contractually yours. This is why Marketing Powered, AI-native since 2022, operates owned, local AI infrastructure for HIPAA-aware inference: not because local models are universally better, but because for regulated data workflows, controlling the inference boundary is the difference between a policy you enforce and a promise you hope a vendor keeps.
There is also a continuity argument. A marketing operation that routes core workflows through a single rented model has concentrated vendor risk in its revenue engine. Owned capability, even partial, converts that from an existential dependency into a procurement preference.
A Governance Framework You Can Actually Enforce
Governance fails when it lives in a PDF nobody opens. Here is what it looks like when it lives in the workflow, with named owners and checkable steps.
1. A tool register with tiers. One page listing every approved AI tool, its contract tier, and its permitted data classes. The consumer ChatGPT app and the enterprise workspace are different entries with different rules. Owner: marketing operations. Review: quarterly.
2. Input rules by data class. Green: public information, published copy, general research. Yellow: internal strategy, unreleased campaigns, pricing, requiring an enterprise-tier tool with training exclusions. Red: PHI, PII, client confidences, permitted only in environments specifically contracted and architected for them, or nowhere. Every team member can recite the three colors; that is the test.
3. Review gates by claim risk. The three tiers from earlier, assigned at brief stage, not after drafting. The content calendar carries a review-tier column. A high-risk piece without a named expert reviewer does not enter production.
4. Disclosure and authorship standards. Decide once, in writing, how AI assistance is acknowledged internally, and who is accountable for each published piece. Accountability does not transfer to software.
5. An incident path. When someone pastes the wrong thing or publishes a hallucinated claim, there is a defined report-contain-correct sequence, treated as a process failure to fix rather than a career event to hide. Policies that punish disclosure get silence, not safety.
6. Measurement to the outcome, not the artifact. Count what the content produced, not how much content the model produced. The same discipline behind tracking attribution to admission, not just lead, applies here: AI that doubles output while qualified demand stays flat has doubled your noise.
