Google Ads Explained: What Decision Makers Actually Need to Know
Most executives evaluate Google Ads by the wrong signal. Clicks and raw lead counts are easy to report and nearly useless for judging return, because performance is decided by what happens after the click and by how the account is built. This is Google Ads explained as an evaluation framework for operators, not a platform tutorial.

How the Auction Decides Whose Ad Shows Up
Picture the moment a prospective customer types a search. In the milliseconds before the results page loads, Google runs an auction among every advertiser bidding on that query. The winner is not simply whoever bid the most. Google calculates a value called Ad Rank, which determines both whether your ad is eligible to show at all and where it appears relative to competitors, and it recalculates that value for every single search ([Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/1722122)).
Ad Rank is based on six factors: your bid, the quality of your ads and landing page, minimum thresholds an ad must clear to show, the competitiveness of the auction, the context of the person's search (their location, device, time of day, and the nature of the query), and the expected impact of ad assets like sitelinks and phone numbers ([Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/1722122)).
Two implications matter for anyone who controls budget. First, quality is a lever on price. Google states plainly that higher quality ads can often lead to lower costs per click ([Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/1722122)). An account with relevant ads and strong landing pages pays less for the same position than a sloppy one. Second, because the auction runs fresh every time, there is no permanent position to buy. You are renting attention one search at a time, and the rent is repriced constantly.
Google summarizes its assessment of your ad quality in a metric called Quality Score, visible inside the account. If your team or agency cannot show you Quality Score trends alongside spend, you are seeing half the economics.
Account Structure Is Where Budgets Quietly Leak
The auction decides the price of a click. Account structure decides whether that click was worth bidding on at all.
A Google Ads account is organized into campaigns, which hold budgets and settings, and ad groups, which hold tightly related keywords and the ads written for them. Three structural disciplines separate accounts that produce qualified pipeline from accounts that burn cash:
* Match type control. Keywords can be set to match searches broadly, by phrase, or exactly. Broad matching casts a wide net and hands Google discretion over which searches trigger your ads. Without oversight, a hospital bidding on cardiology terms can end up paying for searches with no commercial or clinical relevance to its service lines.
* Negative keywords. These are the searches you refuse to pay for: job seekers, students doing research, people looking for free alternatives, queries from outside your service area. A mature account has a negative keyword list that grew from actual search term data, not a template.
* Search term review. Google reports the real searches that triggered your ads. This report is the single fastest audit a decision maker can request. If nobody has reviewed it in ninety days, assume waste.
Ask your internal team or agency to walk you through the account tree, the match type logic, and the most recent search term review. The answers do not require technical fluency to evaluate. Vague answers are the finding.
Regulated and Sensitive Industries Play by Different Rules
Generic Google Ads advice tends to assume every advertiser can use every feature. In healthcare, legal, financial services, and addiction treatment, that assumption fails, and it fails in ways that change strategy, not just tactics.
Start with certification. Google restricts the promotion of recovery-oriented drug and alcohol addiction services, and advertisers targeting certain locations can only run these ads if certified by Google ([Google Ads policy help](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031)). In practice, that certification path runs through LegitScript, whose addiction treatment certification is recognized by Google, Microsoft, and Meta, and which is required for many forms of online addiction treatment advertising in the United States and Canada ([LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/certification/addiction-treatment-certification/)). LegitScript also explicitly disallows lead generators and call centers that refer patients to providers for compensation ([LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/certification/addiction-treatment-certification/)). Similar certification regimes apply to online pharmacies, telemedicine, and health insurance advertisers, with requirements that vary by country ([Google Ads policy help](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031)).
Then there is targeting. Google classifies health among its sensitive interest categories, and advertisers promoting products or services in those categories are not permitted to use advertiser-curated audiences, which include remarketing lists built from your site visitors, customer match uploads, and lookalike segments ([Google personalized advertising policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/143465)). The standard playbook of retargeting everyone who visited your site simply does not exist for these advertisers. Predefined Google audiences remain available because they are configured without sensitive user signals ([Google personalized advertising policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/143465)).
This is not theoretical for Marketing Powered. The founder managed $1.5 to $2M per month in Google Ads across 11+ behavioral and mental health accounts, operating inside Google's sensitive healthcare vertical where retargeting is not permitted. Accounts in that environment win or lose on search intent, structure, and conversion discipline, because there is no second-chance audience to fall back on. The operating posture is LegitScript-aware and HIPAA-aware by necessity, which shapes everything from tracking configuration to landing page content.
If a prospective vendor pitches you a retargeting-heavy plan and you operate in a sensitive category, that is a signal they have not read the policies governing your account.
The Click Is Not the Conversion
Here is the layer where most reporting quietly misleads: the definition of a conversion.
Many accounts count a form fill or a phone call as the conversion and stop there. But a form fill is not revenue. For a treatment provider, the outcome that matters is an admission. For a hospital service line, it is a scheduled and completed appointment. For a law or accounting firm, it is a signed engagement. Between the form and the outcome sits a qualification gap: wrong payer, wrong geography, wrong service need, no-shows, and unreachable contacts.
If your optimization signal is the form fill, Google's bidding algorithms will dutifully find you more form fills, including more of the unqualified kind, because that is what you told the system success looks like. The accounts that compound in efficiency are the ones that feed qualified outcomes back into the platform, so the machine learns what a valuable conversion actually is.
Marketing Powered builds attribution tracked to admission, not just lead, precisely because of this gap. The mechanics vary by business: CRM integration, call tracking with qualification dispositions, offline conversion imports. The principle does not vary. A decision maker should be able to ask one question of any report: can you show me the line from this spend to a qualified outcome? If the answer is a cost per lead with no downstream data, the account is being optimized toward the wrong target, and no amount of bid management fixes that.
Landing Pages Decide What the Click Is Worth
Walk through the experience as the searcher does. Someone searches for a specific service, sees an ad promising exactly that, clicks, and lands on a generic homepage with a navigation bar, a hero slider, and no obvious next step. The ad made a promise the page does not keep. Most of those visitors leave, and the click is already paid for.
Message match is the discipline of making the landing page continue the conversation the ad started. The headline should reflect the search intent. The proof should be relevant to the service promised. The conversion action should be obvious and low-friction. This is not cosmetic. Google's own quality assessment considers landing page relevance and the expectations users form from the ad they clicked ([Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/1722122)), which means weak pages raise your cost per click at the same time they lower your conversion rate. You pay twice for the same mistake.
For multi-location organizations, this compounds. A searcher looking for care in one city should land on a page for that location, with its address, providers, and intake path, not a corporate overview. Building and maintaining that page inventory is unglamorous work, and it is routinely the highest-leverage fix in an underperforming account.
A practical test: take your top three spending keywords, click your own ads' destination URLs, and ask whether a stranger would know within five seconds that they are in the right place and what to do next.
Budget, Bidding, and the Metrics That Actually Matter
Cost per click and cost per lead are the metrics most often quoted in reports and the least useful for decisions, because both are inputs. A high CPC in a competitive vertical can still produce excellent economics if the clicks convert to qualified outcomes at a strong rate. A low CPL can be a disaster if the leads never qualify.
The metric hierarchy a decision maker should insist on, in order:
* Cost per qualified opportunity. Admission, booked consult, signed engagement, completed appointment. This is the number that connects to revenue.
* Qualification rate by campaign. Which campaigns produce leads that become customers or patients, and which produce volume that dies in intake.
* Cost per lead, as a diagnostic between the first two, not as a headline.
* CPC and Quality Score, as efficiency indicators on the input side.
Bidding strategy should follow from this hierarchy. Google's automated bidding can be effective, but it optimizes toward whatever conversion signal it is given. Feed it form fills and it buys form fills. Feed it qualified outcomes, imported back into the platform, and it starts buying those instead. Budget decisions work the same way: shifting spend toward the campaigns with the best cost per qualified opportunity is a monthly governance habit, not a one-time setup.
Be skeptical of industry benchmark numbers quoted without a source. Averages blend wildly different markets, and the only benchmark that matters is whether your cost per qualified outcome supports your unit economics.
Questions to Ask Before You Hire or Renew a Partner
Imagine the review meeting. Your prospective or incumbent partner is across the table with a deck. These are the questions that separate operators from reporters, and every one of them has a verifiable answer:
* Who owns the account? You should hold admin access to your own Google Ads account, with the agency working inside it. If the account lives in the agency's property and leaves when they do, your history, data, and learnings leave with it.
* Show me the account structure and the negative keyword list. You are listening for a logic you can follow, not jargon.
* Show me the last search term review and what changed because of it. Governance leaves a paper trail.
* What exactly counts as a conversion, and how is it verified? Ask whether qualified outcomes flow back into the platform, or whether optimization stops at the form fill.
* If we operate in a regulated category, which policies and certifications apply to us? A competent partner in healthcare should speak fluently about certification requirements and the targeting restrictions on sensitive categories without looking them up ([Google Ads policy help](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031)).
* What is the reporting cadence, and what decision does each report drive? Reports that only describe the past are overhead. Reports that allocate next month's budget are management.
* What would make you recommend spending less? The answer reveals whether their incentives are aligned with your outcomes or your spend.
A partner who welcomes these questions is showing you how they will behave after the contract is signed.

FAQ
Q: Is Google Ads worth it for a multi-location healthcare organization?
A: It can be, with two conditions. First, the account must be structured by location and service line, with landing pages to match, so budget maps to the markets and capacity you actually have. Second, conversion tracking must reach a qualified outcome, such as a completed appointment or admission, because location-level decisions made on raw lead counts routinely move budget to the wrong markets. Certification and targeting restrictions may also apply depending on your services ([Google Ads policy help](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031)).
Q: How long before Google Ads produces results?
A: Clicks arrive the day campaigns launch. Reliable judgment takes longer, because automated bidding needs conversion data to learn from, and qualified-outcome data lags lead data by days or weeks depending on your sales or intake cycle. Judge early weeks on structural signals, search term quality, and message match, and judge the following months on cost per qualified opportunity. Distrust anyone who promises a specific result by a specific date; the auction does not work that way.
Q: Should we run Google Ads in-house or hire an agency?
A: In-house works when you have someone with real platform depth, time for weekly governance, and access to your conversion data. An agency makes sense when you lack that capacity or operate in a regulated category where policy mistakes are expensive, including account suspension for serious violations ([Google Ads policy help](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031)). Either way, the evaluation framework in this guide applies identically. The structure, the tracking, and the governance questions do not change based on who holds the keyboard.
