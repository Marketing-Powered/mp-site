Evaluating AI Marketing Agency Capabilities: A Guide for Treatment Centers
Every agency pitching treatment centers now claims to be AI-powered, and almost none of them can show you what that means in operational terms. This guide gives marketing leaders and executives a concrete framework for separating vendors with real capability from vendors with a new adjective. The test is not what the agency says about AI; it is what the agency can demonstrate about data handling, compliance, and attribution.

Why "AI-Powered" Tells You Almost Nothing
Picture the pitch meeting. Two agencies present to your leadership team in the same week. Both decks mention AI in the first three slides. One shows screenshots of a chat interface and promises faster content. The other walks you through where patient-adjacent data flows when their systems touch your website, which platform policies restrict your category, and how a phone call becomes an admission record you can audit. Those are not two versions of the same capability. They are different businesses wearing the same label.
The label is cheap because the underlying tools are cheap. Any agency can open an account with a frontier model provider and call itself AI-native. What cannot be improvised is the operating knowledge underneath: how behavioral health advertising is policed, what regulators expect from tracking technology, and how to connect spend to admissions rather than form fills. A vendor evaluation that starts with "what AI do you use" is asking the wrong question. Start instead with "show me what your systems do with our data, our ads, and our intake pipeline," then judge the specificity of the answers.
Marketing Powered has been AI-native since 2022, and the honest lesson from that period is that the model matters far less than the system around it. The sections below cover the four areas where real differences show up.
Start With Data Handling, Not Model Names
Treatment centers sit inside one of the most regulated data environments in marketing. The Office for Civil Rights at HHS has published guidance stating that HIPAA-regulated entities "are not permitted to use tracking technologies in a manner that would result in impermissible disclosures" of protected health information to tracking vendors ([HHS OCR](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html)). The same bulletin notes that a website cookie banner does not constitute a valid HIPAA authorization, and that tracking vendors who meet the definition of a business associate require a signed business associate agreement ([HHS OCR](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html)). A 2024 federal court order vacated a portion of this guidance concerning unauthenticated public webpages, and OCR has said it is prioritizing Security Rule compliance in its tracking investigations, so the enforcement landscape is active and still moving ([HHS OCR](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html)).
Now add AI to that picture. When an agency runs your intake transcripts, call recordings, or website analytics through third-party AI tools, you need to know exactly which vendors see that data, under what agreements, and with what retention terms. Questions worth asking any AI-forward agency:
* Which AI vendors and subprocessors touch data from our website, calls, or CRM?
* Do you have business associate agreements in place where the data flow requires them?
* Can any inference run in an environment we can audit, rather than a consumer AI tool?
* What is your written policy on pasting patient-adjacent information into general-purpose chatbots?
Marketing Powered operates owned, local AI infrastructure for HIPAA-aware inference, which exists precisely because some workloads should not travel to a third-party API by default. That is an architectural choice, not a compliance certification, and any vendor who presents infrastructure as a substitute for a compliance program should lose points for that answer alone.
Compliance Posture Is Testable in One Conversation
You do not need a lawyer in the room to pressure-test an agency's compliance fluency. Two subjects will reveal it in minutes.
First, certification. LegitScript certification is required to run addiction treatment ads on platforms including Google, Meta, Microsoft Ads, and Nextdoor, and the program evaluates applicants against 16 certification standards covering licensure, clinical staff qualifications, website disclosures, and advertising practices ([LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/certification/addiction-treatment-certification/)). The standards also prohibit co-ownership of or informal affiliation with lead generators ([LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/certification/addiction-treatment-certification/)). An agency that cannot explain how certification interacts with your ad accounts, or that proposes lead-buying arrangements that would jeopardize it, is a liability regardless of its technology.
Second, targeting restrictions. Google treats health as a sensitive interest category in personalized advertising, covering physical and mental health conditions and treatments for them, and advertiser-curated audiences such as remarketing lists, Customer Match, and your-data segments cannot be used when promoting in these categories ([Google Ads policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16701855?hl=en)). This is the single most consequential constraint in treatment marketing: the retargeting playbook that works in e-commerce is simply not available. Any agency whose proposal assumes remarketing audiences in your category has not read the policy that governs your spend.
Marketing Powered is LegitScript-aware and HIPAA-aware, and builds for Google's sensitive healthcare vertical, where retargeting is not permitted. The founder managed $1.5 to $2M per month in Google Ads across 11+ behavioral and mental health accounts, which is the kind of background that makes these constraints operating assumptions rather than surprises discovered after a policy disapproval.
Ask to See the Attribution Trail, End to End
Here is the process a capable vendor should be able to draw on a whiteboard without hesitation. A family member searches at night and clicks an ad. The landing page loads with call tracking in place, so the inbound call carries its source with it. Admissions answers, and the call outcome is logged against the campaign that produced it. Verification of benefits happens, the admission happens or does not, and that final status flows back so the marketing team can see which keywords and campaigns produced admissions, not just inquiries.
Most agencies stop the measurement at the lead. The call happened, the form was submitted, the cost per lead looks fine, and the report ships. But a treatment center does not run on inquiries; it runs on admissions, payer mix, and census. Two campaigns with identical lead costs can have wildly different admission economics, and you cannot see that difference unless the tracking reaches the end of the intake pipeline.
Marketing Powered tracks attribution to admission, not just lead. When you evaluate any vendor, AI-branded or not, make this the demonstration you insist on: show me, in a live account or an anonymized walkthrough, how a click becomes an admission record. Ask how they handle calls that convert weeks later, how they attribute readmissions or transfers between levels of care, and what happens to the data when a call tracking vendor or CRM sits in the middle. The quality of these answers predicts the quality of everything else.
Where AI Actually Helps a Treatment Center Grow
Once the compliance and measurement foundations are real, AI earns its place in specific, unglamorous jobs:
* Search query and call analysis at scale. Classifying thousands of queries and call outcomes to find wasted spend and missed intents faster than a human analyst working alone.
* Budget and bid decisions informed by admission data. When attribution reaches admissions, optimization can favor what fills beds rather than what fills spreadsheets.
* Content production with clinical review. Drafting service line and location pages faster, with licensed humans reviewing anything that touches clinical claims.
* Intake follow-up workflows. Structured, consent-aware follow-up on inquiries that did not convert on the first call, built with clear limits on what automated systems may say.
* Multi-location consistency. Keeping listings, landing pages, and tracking uniform across facilities, which matters more as a network grows.
Notice what is absent: nothing on this list replaces judgment about a vulnerable population, and nothing should run without a named human owner. The founder of Marketing Powered previously ran marketing as a healthcare CMO for a treatment network that grew from 3 to 24 locations, and the operational lesson from that kind of scaling is that automation amplifies whatever process it touches. Automating a broken intake process produces faster failures. The right evaluation question is not "what can your AI do" but "what does a human check before your AI's output reaches a patient-facing surface."
Infrastructure Questions That Separate Operators From Resellers
Most AI-branded agencies are thin wrappers around the same handful of third-party APIs. That is not automatically disqualifying, but you should know which kind of vendor you are hiring, because it determines who controls your data, your costs, and your continuity if a provider changes terms.
Useful questions:
* Where does inference physically run for workloads involving our data, and who controls that environment?
* What happens to our prompts, documents, and outputs after processing? What are the retention terms?
* If your primary AI provider changed its pricing or policies tomorrow, what breaks?
* Who owns the process documentation, prompt libraries, and automations built for our account if we leave?
Marketing Powered owns and operates local AI infrastructure for HIPAA-aware inference, which means sensitive workloads can run in an environment the firm controls rather than defaulting to external APIs. Be skeptical of any vendor, including this one, that cannot explain the boundary: which workloads run locally, which run on third-party services, and why. A vendor who claims everything stays offline is usually overclaiming. A vendor who has never thought about the question is underbuilt.
A Working Scorecard for Your Next Vendor Meeting
Bring this to the room. Picture your CFO, your compliance officer, and your marketing lead on one side of the table, the agency on the other, and score each answer as demonstrated, described, or dodged.
* Data handling: Name every AI vendor and subprocessor that touches our data. Produce the agreements that govern those flows.
* Tracking compliance: Explain the current OCR position on tracking technologies and how your stack avoids impermissible disclosures ([HHS OCR](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html)).
* Platform policy: Explain what Google's health personalization policy prohibits in our category and show a plan that works within it ([Google Ads policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16701855?hl=en)).
* Certification: Walk through how LegitScript certification affects our accounts and your advertising practices ([LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/certification/addiction-treatment-certification/)).
* Attribution: Demonstrate a click-to-admission trail, including call tracking and CRM integration.
* Governance: Identify the named human who reviews AI output before it reaches a patient-facing surface.
* Portability: State in writing what we keep if we terminate: accounts, data, documentation, automations.
* Experience: Show operating history in regulated healthcare marketing, attributed accurately to the people who did the work.
A vendor who demonstrates five or more of these is worth a second meeting. A vendor who dodges the first four is a risk no amount of AI branding offsets. FAQ
Q: Does an agency need to be LegitScript-certified itself?
A: Certification applies to the treatment provider advertising its services; LegitScript certifies addiction treatment businesses so they can advertise on platforms that require it ([LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/certification/addiction-treatment-certification/)). Your agency needs to understand the program well enough to keep your accounts and advertising practices aligned with it.
Q: Is retargeting completely off the table for treatment centers?
A: On Google, advertiser-curated audiences, including remarketing lists and Customer Match, cannot be used when promoting in sensitive health categories ([Google Ads policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16701855?hl=en)). Growth plans should be built on search intent, contextual reach, and strong conversion systems rather than audience retargeting.
Q: Can AI tools make our marketing HIPAA-compliant?
A: No tool makes an organization compliant. Compliance is a program of agreements, safeguards, and processes; OCR's guidance makes clear that privacy policies and cookie banners alone do not authorize disclosures of protected health information to tracking vendors ([HHS OCR](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html)). Evaluate vendors on how their systems fit inside your compliance program, not on compliance language in their marketing.
