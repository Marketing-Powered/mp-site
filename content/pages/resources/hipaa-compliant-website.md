Building a HIPAA compliant website: requirements for forms, hosting, and tracking
A HIPAA compliant website is not a plugin checkbox, a badge in the footer, or a generic SSL certificate. Healthcare organizations that collect appointment requests, clinical inquiries, or patient intake details online have to engineer coordinated safeguards and Business Associate Agreements across hosting, forms, and analytics. This guide outlines the technical architecture, policy boundaries, and vendor governance required to protect patient data while preserving accountable marketing attribution.

What makes a website HIPAA compliant across infrastructure and code
A website becomes HIPAA-aware when every technical layer that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits Protected Health Information (PHI) operates under documented security safeguards and executed Business Associate Agreements (BAAs). No single tool confers compliance across an entire domain. Instead, healthcare operators, compliance officers, and web engineers must evaluate the public front end, intake forms, hosting environment, and analytics pipeline as one connected system.
Inside a typical hospital system, specialty practice, or multi-location treatment center, compliance failures rarely happen because a web developer ignored security entirely. They happen when departments work in silos. A marketing director adds a third-party scheduling widget or conversion tag to a landing page, an outside agency installs a contact form plugin, and IT assumes the public website holds no patient data because the clinical portal sits on a separate subdomain.
### How web activity becomes Protected Health Information
Under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), individually identifiable health information (IIHI) transmitted or maintained in electronic form by a covered entity or its business associate is electronic Protected Health Information (ePHI). When a website visitor pairs identifying details, such as a name, phone number, or email address, with details about a medical condition, past care, or a request for clinical treatment, that submission creates PHI.
The [HHS OCR tracking technologies guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html) explains that disclosing PHI to third-party tracking vendors without patient authorization or an applicable HIPAA permission is an impermissible disclosure. That conclusion still holds for PHI on authenticated pages, scheduling flows, and intake forms. It does not rest on the vacated theory that an IP address combined with a visit to an unauthenticated public condition page is automatically PHI.
### Why Business Associate Agreements define the legal perimeter
Technical encryption alone does not satisfy HIPAA if an external vendor can access, store, or transmit ePHI without a written contract. Under [45 CFR 164.502](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164/subpart-E/section-164.502) and 45 CFR 164.504(e), a covered entity may permit a business associate to create, receive, maintain, or transmit PHI on its behalf only after obtaining satisfactory assurances documented in a written Business Associate Agreement.
Standard commercial web vendors, low-cost shared hosting providers, and mainstream consumer analytics platforms routinely refuse to sign BAAs for marketing tools. Sending patient intake payloads through any uncontracted vendor breaks the compliance chain immediately, even if the hosting server itself is locked down.
### Why single-layer security breaks down in production
A healthcare website operates as an active software environment exchanging payloads across visitor browsers, content delivery networks, form processors, CRM platforms, and analytics endpoints. Hardening one layer leaves the organization exposed if an adjacent layer leaks data.
Consider a health system that pays for enterprise cloud hosting and encrypted storage volumes for its intake database. If a browser-side tag manager fires an unvetted marketing script on the appointment confirmation page and captures URL query parameters containing the service line or form fields, the database encryption cannot stop that browser disclosure. Engineering a compliant website requires closing every path where PHI enters, moves, or rests.
Core HIPAA Security Rule requirements for healthcare websites
The HIPAA Security Rule at [45 CFR 164.312](https://www.ecfr.gov/on/2026-08-04/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164/subpart-C/section-164.312) defines the technical safeguards governing systems that handle ePHI: access controls, audit controls, integrity protections, person or entity authentication, and transmission security. Translating those federal standards into production web engineering means distinguishing between required specifications, addressable specifications, and operational engineering baselines.
In the Security Rule, "addressable" does not mean optional. A covered entity must assess whether an addressable specification is reasonable and appropriate for its environment based on a documented risk analysis. If it is, the organization implements it; if not, it documents the rationale and deploys an equivalent alternative measure.
### Encryption in transit and at rest
The Security Rule treats encryption of ePHI at rest under 45 CFR 164.312(a)(2)(iv) and encryption in transit under 45 CFR 164.312(e)(2)(ii) as addressable implementation specifications within [45 CFR 164.312](https://www.ecfr.gov/on/2026-08-04/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164/subpart-C/section-164.312). The regulation does not name specific cipher suites or protocol versions as statutory mandates.
In practice, modern risk analyses almost always treat encryption as reasonable and appropriate for internet-facing healthcare systems. A sound engineering baseline is TLS 1.2 or higher for all browser-to-server and server-to-API traffic, paired with strong encryption such as AES-256 for any database volume, file store, log archive, or backup snapshot that contains ePHI.
### Role-based access controls and authentication
Administrative dashboards, cloud hosting consoles, database management tools, and CMS backends must restrict access to authorized personnel only. Under 45 CFR 164.312(a)(2)(i), systems handling ePHI require unique user identification so every action ties back to a specific individual.
Shared credentials such as a generic "admin" or "marketing" login break accountability and undermine incident investigation. Operations teams should enforce multi-factor authentication (MFA), apply least-privilege roles so content editors cannot export database tables or alter server scripts, and deprovision vendor or agency accounts the day an engagement ends.
### Audit controls and activity review
Under 45 CFR 164.312(b), covered entities must implement hardware, software, and procedural mechanisms that record and examine activity in information systems that contain or use ePHI. For web infrastructure, this includes server access logs, CMS administrative login histories, file-change records, and API transmission logs.
Immutability (such as write-once log storage) is a stronger operational safeguard rather than a named Security Rule specification. What matters legally and operationally is that logs capture relevant access and activity, stay protected from casual tampering, and undergo scheduled review defined by the organization's risk analysis.
### Data integrity and ephemeral web storage
Integrity standards under 45 CFR 164.312(c) protect ePHI from improper alteration or destruction. On a public marketing website, the safest way to preserve integrity and limit breach exposure is to keep patient records off the web server altogether.
Rather than treating a CMS database as a holding tank for clinical leads, web teams should design intake routes that validate submissions, transmit them directly into a BAA-covered electronic health record (EHR) or HIPAA-aligned CRM, and leave no persistent copy inside the website database or automated site backups.
How to secure HIPAA compliant website forms and patient intake
Website intake forms are the highest-risk contact point on almost every healthcare domain because prospective patients voluntarily enter direct identifiers alongside clinical details. Building HIPAA compliant website forms requires eliminating accidental storage inside public CMS tables, stopping unencrypted email relays of patient inquiries, and routing submissions through a BAA-covered endpoint.
### Where default CMS form plugins create exposure
Standard WordPress and CMS form tools were built for general lead generation, not regulated clinical intake. Teams frequently misunderstand how different plugins handle submission payloads after a visitor clicks submit.
Default installations of database-backed plugins such as Gravity Forms write submitted field values directly into local CMS database tables without field-level encryption by default. If a site backup is stored on an uncontracted cloud bucket, or if a compromised plugin exposes the database, every historical inquiry sits in readable form.
By contrast, Contact Form 7 emails submissions by default and does not store entries in the local WordPress database unless a storage add-on such as Flamingo is installed. Even without local database storage, sending form contents over plain SMTP introduces a separate risk.
Unencrypted email containing patient names, phone numbers, and clinical details can expose ePHI across external mail relays and staff inboxes. Because transmission security encryption is addressable under [45 CFR 164.312](https://www.ecfr.gov/on/2026-08-04/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164/subpart-C/section-164.312), plain SMTP is not a flat statutory violation in every theoretical case. When an organization's risk analysis treats encryption as reasonable and appropriate for patient intake data, however, emailing unencrypted form submissions fails that safeguard.
### Four-step architecture for HIPAA-aware web forms
Engineering and marketing teams can protect patient intake without adding friction that suppresses conversion rates by following a four-step intake architecture:
* Collect only the minimum necessary fields on public pages. Ask for name, preferred callback method, location or general service interest, and timing. Remove open-ended comment boxes where visitors often type diagnoses, medication lists, insurance member IDs, or family crisis details before entering an authenticated clinical workflow.
* Enforce encrypted transport and strict input validation. Serve the form and its submission endpoint exclusively over TLS 1.2 or higher, validate field formats to block script or SQL injection attempts, and prevent browser-side marketing scripts from reading input values or form-abandonment events.
* Process submissions ephemerally into a BAA-covered destination. Use a dedicated HIPAA-aligned form vendor willing to sign a BAA, or build a custom serverless API inside a BAA-covered cloud account that forwards validated payloads straight into the EHR or HIPAA-aligned CRM. Confirm that zero patient records persist in the CMS database, application cache, or debug logs.
* Replace detailed email alerts with de-identified operational notifications. Instead of emailing a patient's name, phone number, and treatment inquiry to an admissions inbox, trigger a generic notification (such as a timestamped record ID) that prompts staff to view the inquiry inside the authenticated CRM or EHR.
### Comparing default CMS forms against HIPAA-aware intake
* Payload storage; Default CMS form setup: Saved in local CMS database tables (for database-backed plugins) or left in mail servers; HIPAA-aware intake architecture: Routed ephemerally into an encrypted EHR or BAA-covered CRM; zero CMS storage
* Staff notifications; Default CMS form setup: Full form fields sent via standard SMTP email; HIPAA-aware intake architecture: De-identified ping or record ID linking to an authenticated clinical workspace
* Vendor legal coverage; Default CMS form setup: Plugin developers and shared web hosts rarely sign BAAs; HIPAA-aware intake architecture: Executed BAA with the form processor, cloud API host, and downstream CRM or EHR
* Field design; Default CMS form setup: Open-ended message boxes inviting detailed health disclosures; HIPAA-aware intake architecture: Minimum necessary contact and routing fields with clear privacy notices
* Third-party script exposure; Default CMS form setup: Marketing tags and session replay tools may load on form pages; HIPAA-aware intake architecture: Tracking scripts blocked or isolated from form inputs and confirmation states
### Extending form governance to chat widgets and call tracking
Forms are rarely the only conversion mechanism on a healthcare site. Live chat widgets, AI chatbots, embedded self-assessments, click-to-schedule calendars, and dynamic call-tracking scripts capture the exact same combination of caller identity and clinical intent.
Every interactive intake channel requires the same two checks: verify whether the vendor will execute a BAA covering the specific product tier you purchase, and verify where transcripts, recordings, and caller logs live once the conversation ends. If a vendor cannot sign a BAA, remove that widget from clinical intake flows rather than relying on a disclaimer above the chat box.
Managing website tracking, ad policies, and attribution boundaries
Healthcare marketing teams need accurate attribution to allocate media budgets across locations and service lines, yet standard browser pixels routinely create legal and ad-account exposure. Managing tracking responsibly requires separating federal privacy boundaries, non-HIPAA consumer protection enforcement, and advertising platform audience restrictions.
### How federal guidance and court rulings define tracking boundaries
When standard JavaScript snippets from major advertising platforms run inside a visitor's browser, those scripts automatically read page URLs, referrer paths, device attributes, and click behavior. If those tags fire inside a patient portal, on an appointment scheduling workflow, or alongside an intake form, they can transmit PHI directly to third-party advertising companies that do not hold BAAs with the healthcare provider.
Federal oversight of online tracking shifted significantly in 2024. On June 20, 2024, the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas issued an order in American Hospital Association v. Becerra vacating the portion of OCR's March 18, 2024 tracking bulletin that treated an IP address combined with a visit to an unauthenticated public webpage on a specific health condition or provider as IIHI by itself, as noted on the [HHS OCR tracking technologies guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html) page.
Operators should read that ruling carefully rather than treating it as a green light to restore aggressive client-side tracking. The court order did not vacate the rest of the bulletin, nor did it remove HIPAA requirements for authenticated patient portals, appointment booking screens, intake forms, or interactive symptom and eligibility checkers. State consumer health privacy laws and wiretapping lawsuits also continue to target unconsented tracking on public health websites regardless of HIPAA status.
### Why FTC enforcement reaches beyond covered entities
Healthcare organizations that fall outside traditional HIPAA covered-entity definitions, including many cash-pay clinics, wellness platforms, and digital health apps, still face federal privacy enforcement. The [FTC GoodRx enforcement release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/02/ftc-enforcement-action-bar-goodrx-sharing-consumers-sensitive-health-info-advertising) on February 1, 2023 alleged that GoodRx shared users' prescription medications and health conditions with advertising platforms, including Facebook and Google, and used shared data to target ads contrary to its privacy promises, while also failing to notify consumers under the Health Breach Notification Rule.
The operational takeaway is direct: privacy notices on your website are binding commitments. If your privacy policy tells patients you do not share health inquiries with advertisers, any unvetted marketing script firing on conversion pages creates immediate regulatory exposure under federal and state consumer protection laws.
### Separating Google Ads personalized advertising rules from healthcare certification
Healthcare marketers frequently conflate two distinct Google Ads policies when auditing their websites and campaigns. Getting the distinction right prevents both wasted compliance effort and sudden account suspensions.
Under Google's [Health in personalized advertising policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16701855?hl=en), ads about physical or mental health conditions, or about products and services that treat them, cannot use advertiser-curated audiences. That restriction covers your data segments (remarketing lists), Customer Match, lookalike segments, and audience expansion. It is a targeting restriction on personalized advertising, not a blanket ban on every site pixel, and it is not a rule located inside the Healthcare and medicines policy.
Separately, Google's [Healthcare and medicines policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031?hl=en) governs advertiser certification requirements, prescription-drug services, addiction treatment services (including LegitScript verification requirements where applicable), and restricted clinical claims in ad copy and landing pages.
Teams running paid search in regulated categories such as [mental health and behavioral health care](/who-we-help/mental-health/) cannot rely on retargeting lists to rescue weak landing pages. Instead, high-performing acquisition programs focus on high-intent search capture, strict landing page alignment, and [fixing broken marketing attribution](/problems/fix-attribution/) so bidding algorithms learn from verified downstream outcomes rather than noisy top-of-funnel clicks.
How server-side analytics protect attribution without exposing PHI
Standard client-side Google Analytics cannot be used to collect or process PHI because Google states plainly in its [Google Analytics HIPAA documentation](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/13297105) that it does not offer a Business Associate Agreement for Google Analytics and that HIPAA-regulated customers must not expose PHI to that product. Healthcare organizations solve this measurement gap by moving data collection out of the visitor's browser and into a first-party, server-side analytics pipeline.
### How a server-side privacy proxy works step by step
In a client-side tracking setup, the visitor's browser opens direct network connections to external analytics and advertising servers, giving the healthcare organization almost no technical control over what headers, cookies, or URL strings leave the page. A server-side pipeline inserts a controlled server layer owned by the healthcare organization between the browser and every downstream tool:
* First-party event capture on the provider domain. When a visitor loads a public page or initiates an action, a lightweight script sends minimal event data to a first-party endpoint (such as a subdomain controlled by the healthcare organization) rather than directly to an external ad network.
* Server-side ingestion inside a BAA-covered environment. The organization's private server container receives the payload inside an enterprise cloud environment governed by an executed BAA.
* Deterministic PII and PHI scrubbing. Before any event leaves the server, automated rules strip or truncate the visitor's IP address, remove query parameters, redact sensitive URL slugs that identify clinical conditions or treatment programs, and replace persistent browser cookies with ephemeral, rotated server tokens.
* Controlled server-to-server dispatch. Only de-identified, non-PHI aggregate metrics or clean conversion pings are forwarded to downstream analytics platforms or advertising conversion APIs.
### Linking spend to admissions and conversion value per cost
Privacy-safe tracking should improve commercial decision-making, not blind the marketing team. When healthcare organizations measure PPC campaigns purely on raw form fills or inbound calls, automated bidding engines often chase low-intent inquiries, wrong-number calls, or out-of-network traffic that admissions teams cannot serve.
A disciplined measurement model connects non-PHI click identifiers captured at the first landing page visit to downstream milestones stored inside the organization's HIPAA-aligned CRM. Once an inquiry qualifies or converts into a scheduled appointment or admission, the CRM sends an offline, de-identified conversion signal back to the ad platform with a weighted value attached.
Marketing Powered builds healthcare measurement around tracking attribution to admission rather than stopping at lead counts, allowing media teams to evaluate PPC against conversion value per cost, where higher is better. That structure keeps patient names, diagnoses, and medical details strictly inside the clinical system while giving bidding algorithms accurate economic feedback.
### Landing page routing and owned AI governance
Server-side event pipelines also support privacy-safe experimentation. Instead of building invasive user profiles across clinical browsing sessions, marketing engineers can run multi variant optimization on landing page layouts, intake step order, and call-to-action placement using anonymous session-level conversion data and aggregate traffic attributes such as channel, geography, and device type.
The same data-sovereignty discipline applies when healthcare teams introduce AI into marketing operations, call-quality scoring, or content workflows. Pasting intake transcripts or CRM notes into consumer AI tools creates the exact same BAA failure as an uncontracted form plugin. Organizations handling regulated inquiries should keep sensitive inference isolated on contracted enterprise infrastructure or on owned, local AI infrastructure for HIPAA-aware inference, matching the governance model covered in our [healthcare data and analytics engineering](/services/data-agency/) practice.
Selecting HIPAA compliant website hosting and infrastructure providers
Clean form code and scrubbed analytics cannot fix an uncontracted hosting layer. Deploying a HIPAA compliant website requires infrastructure backed by an executed BAA for every service that touches ePHI, combined with network isolation, encrypted storage, disciplined patching, and verified disaster recovery.
### Why standard shared and managed WordPress hosting fails
Shared hosting accounts, entry-level virtual private servers, and many popular managed WordPress plans explicitly state in their terms of service that customers may not store, process, or transmit PHI on the platform. Even when a host offers free SSL certificates and automated nightly backups, three structural blockers remain:
* No executed BAA. Without a signed Business Associate Agreement covering the hosting environment, any ePHI traversing the server triggers a contractual gap under [45 CFR 164.502](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164/subpart-E/section-164.502).
* Multi-tenant exposure and support access. Shared servers place multiple organizations on overlapping operating-system resources, and standard hosting support desks rarely operate under healthcare-specific access logging or workforce training rules.
* Uncontrolled backup and staging copies. Automated staging environments, caching layers, and offsite backup buckets often clone entire production databases without encryption keys managed under the covered entity's risk analysis.
### Two viable hosting architectures for healthcare websites
Healthcare organizations do not always need to host their entire public marketing site inside an expensive, locked-down clinical enclave. Depending on site complexity and engineering capacity, two production architectures work reliably.
### Option 1: Full BAA-covered cloud or managed healthcare hosting
In this model, the entire web application, database, cache, firewall, and backup storage reside inside a dedicated virtual private cloud (VPC) or specialized healthcare-managed hosting environment covered by a BAA. This approach fits health systems running complex dynamic applications, authenticated patient directories, or custom server-rendered intake portals where web servers directly process ePHI.
### Option 2: Decoupled public front end with an isolated BAA intake pipeline
In a decoupled architecture, the public informational website (service descriptions, provider bios, location pages, and educational resources) serves static or cached content that never stores, logs, or processes PHI. All intake forms, scheduling modules, and chat interfaces are isolated components that post directly from the visitor's browser to a separate, BAA-covered API, CRM, or EHR backend.
Many multi-location medical groups and behavioral health operators prefer the decoupled model because it shrinks the compliance boundary dramatically. Content teams can publish educational resources and location updates rapidly without putting every CMS template edit inside the ePHI audit perimeter.
### Comparing healthcare website hosting models
* BAA availability; Standard shared or managed CMS host: Rarely offered; PHI often prohibited by TOS; Full BAA-covered cloud hosting: Available for eligible covered services; Decoupled front end + isolated BAA intake: Required for the intake API, form processor, and CRM/EHR; public static layer holds no PHI
* Isolation and network security; Standard shared or managed CMS host: Multi-tenant; limited firewall customization; Full BAA-covered cloud hosting: Dedicated VPC, private subnets, WAF, and isolated databases; Decoupled front end + isolated BAA intake: Public content isolated from sensitive endpoints entirely
* Patching and maintenance burden; Standard shared or managed CMS host: Managed by host, but outside HIPAA governance; Full BAA-covered cloud hosting: High internal or managed-DevOps responsibility; Decoupled front end + isolated BAA intake: Moderate; CMS updates stay separated from clinical data pipelines
* Best operational fit; Standard shared or managed CMS host: Non-healthcare brochures with zero intake; Full BAA-covered cloud hosting: Custom web apps and authenticated patient workflows; Decoupled front end + isolated BAA intake: Multi-location providers focused on fast SEO, paid media landing pages, and clean intake
### Understanding the shared responsibility model
Signing a BAA with an enterprise cloud provider does not make a website HIPAA compliant automatically. Cloud providers secure the physical data centers, hypervisors, and underlying managed services listed in their BAA eligibility scope.
The healthcare organization and its engineering partner remain responsible for configuring encryption keys, restricting database ports, enforcing MFA, updating CMS plugins, maintaining audit log reviews, and verifying that developers never enable non-eligible cloud features on servers handling ePHI.
Auditing your existing website, vendors, and third-party scripts
Most healthcare organizations are not building a website from scratch; they are inheriting a live domain accumulated over years of agency handoffs, marketing experiments, and clinic acquisitions. Before scaling paid media spend or redesigning landing pages, a joint team of marketing leadership, compliance officers, web developers, and IT security should audit the live site across four concrete surfaces.
### Step 1: Inspect live network traffic for hidden tags and replay scripts
Open the live website in a clean browser session with developer network tools enabled and walk through four page types: the homepage, a condition or treatment page, a location page, and an appointment or intake form. Record every external domain receiving network requests.
Pay particular attention to session-replay tools, heatmap scripts, embedded third-party calendars, video players, and tag managers. Session-replay scripts often record DOM interactions and keystrokes; if one loads on an intake page without strict field-masking and a BAA, it can transmit patient entries directly to an analytics vendor before the visitor even hits submit.
### Step 2: Trace every form, chat, and call path with synthetic test data
Submit test inquiries through every active form, popup modal, footer widget, and chat box using clearly labeled dummy information (never real patient details). Then follow the payload end to end:
* Check the CMS database to confirm no entry tables stored the submission.
* Check staff email inboxes and mail-relay logs to verify whether plain-text notification emails fired.
* Verify that the payload arrived intact inside the HIPAA-aligned CRM or EHR with source-attribution parameters preserved cleanly.
* Place a test call to dynamic tracking numbers and confirm where call recordings, caller IDs, and transcripts are stored and whether the call-tracking vendor has an executed BAA on file.
### Step 3: Reconcile your vendor stack against executed BAAs
Create a single inventory spreadsheet listing every vendor connected to the website, its technical role, whether it can touch ePHI, and the date of the signed BAA.
* Hosting and cloud infrastructure; Can it touch ePHI?: Yes, if forms or server logs handle ePHI; BAA required?: Yes (unless decoupled layer holds zero PHI); Practical risk control: Restrict workloads to BAA-eligible services; disable unencrypted debug logs
* Form processor or intake API; Can it touch ePHI?: Yes; BAA required?: Yes; Practical risk control: Route ephemerally to CRM/EHR; purge temporary server copies immediately
* CRM and EHR platforms; Can it touch ePHI?: Yes; BAA required?: Yes; Practical risk control: Enforce role-based access, MFA, and encrypted API handoffs
* Call tracking and chat vendors; Can it touch ePHI?: Yes; BAA required?: Yes; Practical risk control: Confirm BAA covers recordings and transcripts; disable SMS PHI replies
* Web analytics and ad platforms; Can it touch ePHI?: Only if misconfigured; BAA required?: Generally unavailable; isolate from PHI; Practical risk control: Deploy server-side proxy to strip IP addresses, URL paths, and identifiers
* CDN, DNS, and WAF; Can it touch ePHI?: Potentially, if terminating TLS over form traffic; BAA required?: Evaluate per architecture and vendor scope; Practical risk control: Keep TLS encryption end to end; exclude intake APIs from uncontracted edge caching
### Step 4: Enforce CMS governance and change control
A clean website drifts out of compliance the moment an unmanaged plugin or marketing pixel goes live without review. Establish a lightweight release gate that requires compliance and IT sign-off before any new tag-manager container rule, form field, chat widget, or third-party script deploys to production.
Remove dormant accounts belonging to former employees or past agencies, schedule quarterly plugin and tag audits, and pair every audit with your annual HIPAA risk analysis so leadership maintains a verifiable compliance record.

FAQ
Q: Is there an official HIPAA certification for websites?
A: No, the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services does not issue, endorse, or recognize an official HIPAA certification for websites, hosting providers, or software plugins. Any vendor badge claiming certified HIPAA website status reflects a private self-attestation or third-party assessment, not government approval. Compliance depends on your organization's documented risk analysis, executed BAAs, and verified technical and administrative safeguards.
Q: Does a website need HIPAA compliance if it has no patient portal?
A: Yes, a public healthcare website without a login portal still falls under HIPAA requirements whenever contact forms, appointment requests, insurance verification forms, live chat tools, or call-tracking workflows collect identifying details alongside health or treatment inquiries. If a website contains purely informational brochures and directs every inquiry solely to a phone number or an externally hosted, BAA-covered portal without collecting visitor data, the website's own compliance scope shrinks substantially.
Q: Can healthcare websites use Google Analytics 4?
A: Healthcare organizations cannot send PHI to Google Analytics 4 because Google does not offer a Business Associate Agreement for Google Analytics and explicitly instructs HIPAA-regulated customers not to expose PHI to the service, per [Google Analytics HIPAA documentation](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/13297105). Organizations that want aggregate site performance data without violating that boundary either adopt a BAA-capable analytics alternative or route events through a first-party, server-side proxy that removes IP addresses, sensitive URL paths, and user identifiers before any data reaches Google.
Q: Are WordPress websites allowed under HIPAA?
A: WordPress can power the public marketing front end of a HIPAA-aware healthcare website when configured under strict architectural boundaries. Standard WordPress installations are not compliant out of the box, so teams must either run the site on BAA-covered infrastructure with hardened access controls or decouple intake completely so zero PHI enters WordPress databases, plugin tables, caches, or SMTP email notifications.
Q: Did American Hospital Association v. Becerra eliminate HIPAA tracking restrictions?
A: No, the June 20, 2024 order in American Hospital Association v. Becerra vacated only the portion of OCR's March 18, 2024 tracking bulletin that treated an IP address combined with a visit to an unauthenticated public webpage on a specific condition or provider as IIHI by itself, as documented in the [HHS OCR tracking technologies guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html). Authenticated portals, scheduling workflows, intake forms, and interactive clinical tools remain subject to HIPAA privacy and security rules, and state privacy laws continue to apply separately.
Q: Can healthcare advertisers run retargeting campaigns on Google Ads?
A: No, ads promoting physical or mental health conditions or treatments cannot use remarketing lists on Google Ads. Under Google's [Health in personalized advertising policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16701855?hl=en), sensitive health promotions are barred from using advertiser-curated audiences, including your data segments (remarketing lists), Customer Match, lookalike segments, and audience expansion. Regulated healthcare teams instead build growth around high-intent search acquisition, strong landing page architecture, and server-side offline conversion tracking tied to verified admissions.
