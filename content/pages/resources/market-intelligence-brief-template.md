A Market Intelligence Brief Template That Leads to a Decision
Most research disappointments begin before any research happens. A request like "look at the competition" leaves the analyst to invent the question, and the answer arrives as a deck of industry facts nobody can act on. A market research brief template fixes that by converting a vague ask into a decision specification with named deliverables, source rules and acceptance criteria.

Name the decision, the decision maker and the deadline
Picture the meeting where this research will actually be used. Three people around a table or on a video call: an owner or general manager who controls the budget, a marketing lead who will have to execute whichever path is chosen, and a finance or operations person who will be asked whether the numbers hold. Someone says "we should look into that market" and the group nods. Nobody writes down what will be decided, who signs, or when. That missing sentence is the single biggest cause of research that lands with a thud.
Start the brief by completing one sentence in plain language, like this one.
By March 14, the managing partner will decide between launching the new service line, acquiring a boutique competitor that already offers it, or deferring for twelve months.
Swap in your own date, your own decision owner and your own options. If you cannot complete that sentence with a real name, a real date and real alternatives, the brief is not ready to send to an analyst or an agency, and extra research hours will not rescue it.
Three details make the sentence work.
Real alternatives, not yes or no. "Should we expand into Phoenix?" is weaker than "Phoenix, Tucson, or deepen share in our current metro." Alternatives tell the analyst what to compare. A binary question invites a one-sided argument.
The decision type, because it changes the method. Opening a location in a new metro needs demand estimates, competitor density, staffing supply and referral structure. Choosing between two positioning statements needs buyer language, win and loss patterns, and message testing. Setting a price floor needs competitor pricing behavior and willingness-to-pay signals. Research firms treat these as separate services rather than one blended study, separating market sizing and opportunity, competitive intelligence, industry trend analysis, win/loss analysis and market entry research ([Teknicks market research and analysis](https://www.teknicks.com/services/research/market-research/)). Bundling them into "a market study" is how cost and timeline inflate without improving the decision.
The consequence of delay. Write what happens if the decision slips by sixty days. A lease expiring, a hiring window, a payer contract cycle or a competitor's announced opening all create genuine timing pressure. If nothing happens when the date slides, say so. An honest soft deadline lets the analyst use slower, stronger methods such as primary interviews instead of rushing to desk research.
Finally, name the decision owner by role and list the people who must be consulted before sign-off. Research that satisfies the marketing lead but never passes the owner's test is research you pay for twice.
Define the questions that matter and the ones that do not
With the decision fixed, write the questions the research must answer. Keep the list to three to five, rank them, and state what each one unlocks. Ranking matters more than people expect. When budget or time runs short, the analyst needs to know which question survives intact and which gets a thinner treatment.
A useful question is answerable with evidence, bounded in scope, and tied to the decision. Compare these pairs:
* Weak: "What is the market size?" Strong: "How many households in the three target zip code clusters fall inside our service and payer profile, and what annual volume could we realistically capture at current share assumptions?"
* Weak: "Who are our competitors?" Strong: "Which five providers appear most often in the paid and organic results our buyers see, what do they promise on their landing pages, and where do they leave gaps we can occupy?"
* Weak: "Is demand growing?" Strong: "Has search and referral demand for this service line grown, flattened or declined over the last twenty-four months in our metro, and what explains the direction?"
Exclusions are the half most briefs skip, and they are where budget leaks. Write a short "not in scope" list: adjacent service lines, national market commentary, brand perception studies, technology vendor evaluations, anything the team already knows and accepts. Exclusions protect the vendor as well as the buyer. An analyst with written permission to ignore a topic can spend those hours on the questions you ranked first instead of padding an appendix.
State the assumptions you are prepared to accept without testing. If leadership has already settled on a service model, say the brief treats it as fixed. If you want the research to challenge it, say that instead. Ambiguity here produces a report that quietly relitigates a settled question while the ranked questions go half-answered.
This exclusion discipline is familiar to anyone who has run a formal vendor search. The [Rehab Marketing Agency RFP Template and Vendor Guide](/resources/rehab-marketing-rfp) applies the same logic to selecting a healthcare marketing partner: define what is being evaluated, then rule out everything else so responses stay comparable. The jobs are different. An RFP selects a supplier. A research agency brief specifies a body of work and the market research project scope that work must stay inside. Both fail the same way when the requester will not say what is out of bounds.
Close the section with one line on how the answers will be used. "These answers will support a go or no-go on a second location by the March board meeting" keeps everyone honest about why the project exists.
Set evidence rules, sourcing ethics and research boundaries
Two analysts can answer the same question with very different evidence. One pulls public filings, industry associations and government datasets. Another runs twelve buyer interviews. A third buys a syndicated database subscription. The cost, defensibility and shelf life of those paths are not the same, so the brief should state which sources count.
Specify four boundaries.
Source types. List what is acceptable and what is required: public company filings, regulatory and licensing registries, government statistical programs, trade association reports, licensed panels and databases, customer and prospect interviews, mystery shopping of publicly advertised services, and your own first-party data. If a finding has to withstand review by a lender, a board or a court, say so in the brief, because that raises the bar on attribution and repeatability.
Geography and time period. Name the metros, counties, states or service radii, and set the lookback window. "Last twenty-four months" and "last five years" tell different stories in a market that moved through a regulatory change or a wave of new entrants.
Permissions and access. Note what the vendor may and may not touch: CRM exports, call recordings, ad platform accounts, client or patient records. In regulated environments this is not a formality. Protected health information and other sensitive records require a documented basis for access, and research convenience is not one. Marketing Powered is HIPAA-aware and LegitScript-aware and runs owned, local AI infrastructure for HIPAA-aware inference, which shapes how source material is handled. Private infrastructure by itself does not establish compliance, so the brief should record hosting, retention, access, subprocessors and approved data flows for anything you share, per engagement and in writing.
Methods that are off limits. Legitimate competitive intelligence relies on public and consented sources. SCIP, the professional body for competitive and market intelligence, publishes a code of ethics that it expects members and the third parties those members hire to follow, including accurate disclosure of identity and organization before interviews ([SCIP code of ethics](https://scip.world/ethics/)). Its ethics guidance also states that obtaining a password to a competitor's site without authorization is illegal and therefore violates the code, that posing as a customer or student may be illegal depending on jurisdiction, and that an employee who previously worked for a competitor should be told not to share confidential information, with any such information left unused if it surfaces ([SCIP code of ethics](https://scip.world/ethics/)). Write those limits into the brief in your own words, and add your own requirement that every finding records its method, its source and its collection date.
The [Hospital Marketing Agency RFP: Guide and Evaluation Matrix](/resources/hospital-marketing-agency-rfp) sets boundaries the same way for partner selection inside a complex health system. The parallel is deliberate. Evaluation rules agreed up front cost far less than disputes after delivery.
Choose deliverables that match the decision and require stated confidence
Here is where a brief earns or wastes its budget. Picture two versions of the same project, commissioned by the same company in the same week. Both are illustrative, not measured outcomes.
Version one asks for "a presentation on the market." The analyst produces forty slides: industry growth commentary, a competitor logo grid, a SWOT, three charts from secondary sources, and a closing slide of recommendations. It reads well. The owner cannot change a single assumption inside it, cannot see which numbers are observed and which are inferred, and cannot hand it to a finance lead for modeling.
Version two asks for a six-page decision memo with the recommendation on page one, a spreadsheet model with editable assumptions, a sourced competitor table with the date each observation was collected, and a one-page summary for the board. Same question, same budget range, entirely different usefulness. The second version survives the first "what if volume is twenty percent lower" question. The first does not.
The table below is an illustrative guide for matching format to decision, not a fixed package.
* Choosing between named options. Primary deliverable: Decision memo. What the brief should require: Recommendation first, evidence behind it, a dissenting read, stated limits.
* Volume, price, capacity or payback. Primary deliverable: Model or spreadsheet. What the brief should require: Editable assumption cells, ranges rather than single points, no hard-coded values buried in formulas.
* Work that will be rerun or extended. Primary deliverable: Dataset or source register. What the brief should require: URLs, collection dates, method notes, consistent field definitions.
* Communicating a decision already made. Primary deliverable: Short summary deck. What the brief should require: Built on top of a memo, model or dataset, never the only deliverable.
Then require disclosed confidence. Every number should carry a label: measured, modeled or estimated. Measured means observed in a named source. Modeled means derived from stated assumptions. Estimated means informed judgment. Ask for ranges on anything modeled, and for a short "what would change this conclusion" section at the end. Market research is commonly sold as work that reveals competitive dynamics, buyer preferences and untapped opportunities ([Teknicks market research and analysis](https://www.teknicks.com/services/research/market-research/)), and all three of those outputs contain inference. A competitive intelligence brief template that does not force inference to be labeled will get inference presented as fact.

Agree scope, dependencies and acceptance before work begins
Scope disputes are rarely about effort. They are about expectations nobody wrote down. Five fields prevent most of them.
Dependencies. List what the vendor needs from you and when: analytics and ad account access, CRM exports, intake or sales call availability, a named internal contact, introductions for customer interviews. Interview-dependent projects slip for one reason more than any other, which is that the client could not get people on calendars. Put your own response time in writing, for example two business days on data requests.
Checkpoints. Schedule a kickoff alignment, a midpoint readout where early findings can redirect remaining hours, and a draft review before final delivery. The midpoint readout earns its place because it is the last moment to change direction cheaply.
Budget assumptions. You do not have to publish a number, but you should state the shape of the engagement. A narrowly scoped competitive review of ten public competitors is a different purchase from a market-entry feasibility study with primary interviews and a capacity model. Say which one you are buying, state whether licensed data purchases sit inside or outside the fee, and ask for the change-order process before kickoff rather than during delivery.
Ownership and rights. Name who owns the deliverable, the underlying data and the model file. Specify retention and deletion expectations for anything sensitive you share, and whether subprocessors are permitted.
Acceptance criteria. Write the test for "done" before work starts: every ranked question has a direct answer or a documented reason it could not be answered, every number is labeled measured, modeled or estimated, every external claim carries a source and a collection date, the model recalculates with editable assumptions, and the memo states the limits of its conclusion.

A completed example brief, labeled fictional
The following example is fictional and illustrative. It does not describe a client, an engagement or a real company, and the figures in it are invented for teaching purposes.
* Organization. Entry: Northbank Advisory, a hypothetical thirty-person accounting and advisory firm with two offices in a Midwest metro.
* Decision. Entry: By March 14, the managing partner will decide between launching an outsourced CFO service line, acquiring a two-person boutique that already offers it, or deferring twelve months.
* Consequence of delay. Entry: A senior hire with the right background is interviewing elsewhere, so a deferral means rebuilding the hiring pipeline.
* Question 1. Entry: What is realistic annual billable demand for outsourced CFO work among metro businesses with five to fifty million in revenue, and what share could a new entrant plausibly win in year one and year two?.
* Question 2. Entry: Which five firms currently serve that segment locally, and how do they package, position and price the service publicly?.
* Question 3. Entry: What do buyers in that segment say drove their choice of provider, from interviews with eight to twelve finance decision makers?.
* Question 4. Entry: What staffing model and utilization rate would the service line require to reach contribution margin?.
* Out of scope. Entry: Tax season capacity planning, wealth management adjacency, national market commentary, brand perception research, software vendor selection.
* Evidence rules. Entry: Public filings, state business registries, association salary data, licensed market data if cost is pre-approved, competitor public pricing pages, primary interviews with consented participants, no contact with competitor staff under false pretenses, no confidential material, documented sources with collection dates.
* Deliverables. Entry: Six-page decision memo with recommendation first, an editable demand and staffing model, a sourced competitor table, an interview findings summary with verbatim themes and no participant identifiers.
* Confidence rules. Entry: Demand figures labeled modeled, with ranges and stated assumptions. Competitor pricing labeled measured, with source URLs and collection dates.
* Acceptance. Entry: All four questions answered or formally declared unanswerable, model opens and recalculates, every external claim sourced, limits section included.
Now notice how single fields change the work. Remove the deadline and the analyst defaults to a slower secondary-research path. Remove question three and the project becomes a desk study with no buyer voice, which is cheaper and much weaker on positioning. Remove the exclusions and the deck grows a wealth management section nobody asked for. Add "must withstand lender review" and the interview sampling and attribution tighten considerably. Each field is a lever on cost, method and defensibility, which is why a completed brief shortens the scoping call instead of replacing it.

Questions buyers ask when commissioning research
### What should a research brief include before asking for a quote?
At minimum: the decision and its owner, the deadline, the real alternatives, three to five ranked questions, explicit exclusions, acceptable source types, geography and time period, deliverable formats, confidence labeling requirements, dependencies and acceptance criteria. Those fields let a vendor price the work instead of pricing the risk of guessing what you want. Separating the research type also helps, because sizing, competitive intelligence, trend analysis, win/loss and market entry are distinct services with distinct methods ([Teknicks market research and analysis](https://www.teknicks.com/services/research/market-research/)). If you send only a topic, expect wide quote ranges and a long scoping call before any number appears.
### How much background information should we provide?
More than feels necessary on context, less than feels necessary on conclusions. Share the business situation, prior internal analysis, the hypotheses leadership already holds, and any constraint that cannot change, such as a fixed service model or a capped capital budget. Share your first-party data where permissions allow, because an analyst who can see your own conversion and revenue patterns will produce sharper work than one modeling from public data alone. Withhold your preferred answer. Telling a vendor what you hope the research will show invites confirmation, and the point of the exercise is to test the assumption rather than decorate it.
### How do we judge whether the final research answered the brief?
Read the deliverable against the acceptance checklist you agreed before kickoff, question by question. Each ranked question should have a direct answer or a documented explanation of why the evidence could not support one, which is a legitimate finding rather than a failure. Check that measured, modeled and estimated items are distinguished, that each external claim carries a source and a collection date, and that the model recalculates when you change an assumption. The strongest test is practical: can the decision owner sit down with the memo and choose between the stated alternatives without commissioning another round of analysis?
### How should a small team adapt this approach?
Keep the structure and shrink the scope. A one-page brief with two ranked questions, a hard exclusion list and a named deadline will still outperform an open-ended request. Be realistic about method cost as well. Eight well-chosen customer interviews often produce more decision value than a purchased market report, and they cost time rather than license fees. If budget is tight, commission the question that unlocks the decision and explicitly defer the rest. Writing "phase two, not funded" into the brief produces a better outcome than thin coverage of five topics, and it gives you a ready scope for the next round.
### What evidence would change the recommended approach?
Three conditions. If the decision is genuinely reversible and cheap to undo, a lighter brief and a fast test in market will beat a formal study. If the question is already answered by first-party data you have not analyzed, internal analysis is the right first step and external research can wait. If the decision depends on a regulated or platform constraint, verify the constraint before commissioning demand research, because it may remove an option outright. Google treats health as a sensitive interest category and does not allow advertiser-curated audiences such as your own data segments and Customer Match for promotion within it, while predefined Google audiences remain available ([Google Ads policy on health in personalized advertising](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16701855?hl=en-AU)). A demand study that assumes a remarketing-led funnel in that category would model a channel the advertiser cannot run. The founder's own history includes managing $1.5 to $2M per month in Google Ads across 11+ behavioral and mental health accounts. Marketing Powered tracks attribution to admission, not just lead. Constraints first, then the research.
