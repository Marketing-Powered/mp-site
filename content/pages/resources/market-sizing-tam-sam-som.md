How to Build a Defensible Market Size With TAM, SAM and SOM
Market-size slides often struggle with one follow-up question: where did the one percent come from? Useful TAM SAM SOM market sizing treats the three figures as three separate calculations resting on three different kinds of evidence, not one guess sliced into progressively smaller fractions. This guide sets out that method and a worksheet structure you can rebuild in a spreadsheet before your next board or investor conversation.

Define the buyer and the unit of demand first
Before anyone counts anything, the model needs a unit. A unit of demand is the single thing you are counting and valuing: an account, a person, a transaction, an episode of care, a contract year, or a unit of delivered capacity such as a chair, a bay, a bed, or a billable hour. Pick the wrong unit and every number downstream inherits the error, no matter how carefully you source the inputs.
Picture the room where this goes wrong. A founder, a CFO and a head of growth sit around a conference table building a market-entry case for a regional outpatient clinic group. The founder counts eligible adults in the service area. The CFO counts annual treatment episodes, because that is what the general ledger recognizes as revenue. The growth lead counts intakes, because that is what the CRM records. All three units are defensible, and they produce three different market sizes for the same service. The gap between them is not rounding, it is definitional: one adult may generate no episodes in a year, or two, and one episode may involve several intakes. Until the group agrees on the unit, the meeting ends with three decks instead of one model.
Choose the unit by asking what the business actually sells and bills. If revenue is recognized per completed episode, count episodes. If revenue is a monthly subscription per account, count accounts and treat seats as a value multiplier. If delivery is capacity constrained, count capacity directly, because a market you cannot staff is not a market you can serve. Write the definition in one sentence at the top of the model, including the time period: "one completed outpatient episode, per calendar year, within the licensed service area."
Then define the buyer separately from the user. In healthcare, professional services and most regulated categories, the person who experiences the service is often not the person who authorizes the purchase. A hospital service line buys through a committee. A multi-location operator buys at the corporate level and consumes locally. If the model counts users but the sales process has to convert authorizers, the serviceable layer will be far narrower than the total suggests.
Record the exclusions at the same time: out-of-area prospects, ineligible payers, segments you are not licensed to serve, buyers below a minimum contract size. Exclusions are easier to defend than inclusions, and a reviewer who sees them stated up front spends less time probing the headline number.
Calculate a total market from named sources
Total addressable market is a population and value question. The bottom-up market sizing form is simple arithmetic: population or establishment count, multiplied by an eligibility or incidence rate, multiplied by average annual value per unit. The discipline is not in the formula, it is in the sourcing. Every multiplier gets a named source and a retrieval date, or it is labeled an assumption. A model with four inputs and four citations survives scrutiny. A model with four inputs and one citation gets re-litigated line by line.
For U.S. buyers, public data covers the counting layer reasonably well. The Census Bureau points businesses to tools including data.census.gov and the Census Business Builder, describing them as a way to understand the local economies a business wants to serve, and notes that Census data can help answer where people matching an ideal customer live, how many competitors are already in an area, and how much revenue each employee earns for similar businesses nearby ([U.S. Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/topics/business-economy/small-business/about/powerful-data.html)). For industry counts, the same program family includes County Business Patterns and the NAICS classification system used to identify an industry before searching.
Know the limits of that data before leaning on it. Public counts carry a vintage, they are organized by statistical geography rather than by your service area, and they describe population and establishment structure rather than demand for your specific offer. The Census Business Builder, for example, publishes data down to the census tract level, and the Bureau directs users to the American Community Survey or a decennial census for anything smaller ([U.S. Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/topics/business-economy/small-business/about/powerful-data.html)). An establishment count tells you how many firms of a given type exist in a county. It does not tell you how many have a budget, a trigger event or a reason to switch this year. Treat public datasets as the denominator and your own evidence as the qualifier. If you cannot confirm a table or vintage while building the model, describe the dataset type in your notes rather than citing a table you have not opened.
Value per unit is where totals inflate. List price is not average realized value. Use net revenue per unit after discounting, payer mix, cancellations and refunds, and separate first-year value from lifetime value so the annual market is not quietly multiplied by a retention assumption. If the model needs a contribution view rather than a revenue view, build the unit economics explicitly rather than applying a blended margin at the end. Our guide to [marketing investment economics](/resources/marketing-economics) covers how to construct value-per-unit assumptions that hold up when finance reviews them.
One more sourcing rule, and it is ours rather than a borrowed one: no imported totals without provenance. A figure lifted from a press release, a competitor deck or a summary article is not a source. Sizing specialists apply the same standard to their own work. Douglas Insights frames market sizing around decisions on new market entry, fundraising, annual strategic planning, capacity and supply planning, and product line extensions, and says every figure in its models ships with a numbered source ([Douglas Insights](https://www.douglasinsights.com/consulting/market-sizing/)). The total is the easiest number to inflate and the one reviewers tend to challenge first.

Apply serviceable constraints to reach a credible SAM
Serviceable available market is a constraint question, not a percentage. You get there by subtracting the parts of the total you cannot legally, operationally or economically serve, and by writing down the reason for each subtraction. Reviewers rarely argue with a constraint that has a cause attached to it.
Start with the hard constraints. Licensure and regulation often decide the geography outright: a clinical service cannot be delivered into a state where the organization holds no license, and a professional services firm cannot sign work it is not credentialed or insured to perform. Payer and contract status is a second hard filter, because an in-network relationship determines access for a large share of healthcare demand. Accreditation, certification and platform eligibility rules can also limit which channels you are allowed to use, which matters for regulated categories where some ad formats and targeting options are restricted. Each of these converts a slice of the total into a documented exclusion.
Then apply the soft constraints, which are judgment calls that still deserve evidence. Offer fit excludes buyers whose needs fall outside the service configuration, such as acuity levels you do not treat or engagement sizes below your minimum. Channel reach excludes demand you cannot practically address, such as a segment that only transacts through a procurement network you are not on. Operational reality excludes what you cannot deliver on current staffing, hours, facilities or coverage area.
Geography deserves particular care for multi-location organizations. Service areas are drive-time and referral-pattern realities rather than county lines, and a model built on county data will overstate reach for any site whose catchment stops at a river, a toll road or a competing provider three miles closer. Build the service area from the way patients and clients actually travel, then map it to whatever statistical geography your population data uses, and note the mismatch in the assumption log.
Economics close the loop. A segment you can legally serve but cannot acquire profitably belongs outside the serviceable market, or at minimum in a separate tier with its own assumptions. That requires a clear view of acquisition cost by segment and channel. Our reference on [customer acquisition cost definitions](/resources/customer-acquisition-cost) sets out the cost definitions that make this filter consistent across segments rather than argued case by case.
Document each constraint as a line item showing the volume it removes and the reason it applies. Three filters with named causes are more persuasive than one blended realistic-adjustment factor, and each can be revisited on its own when the business adds a license, a payer contract or a location.
Estimate reach from capacity, not from a round share
Serviceable obtainable market is a capacity and distribution question. This is where most models break, because the author reaches for a round percentage. One percent of a large number feels modest, which is exactly why it persuades nobody on the other side of the table. A share figure is a conclusion, not an input. If the only support for it is that it sounds small, the model has no bottom. Douglas Insights makes the same criticism of top-down estimates, noting that percent-of-a-big-number figures from free sources collapse the first time a board member asks where a figure came from ([Douglas Insights](https://www.douglasinsights.com/consulting/market-sizing/)).
Build the obtainable figure from the operating system that actually produces revenue. Four inputs carry most of the weight.
Capacity. How many units can the organization deliver in the period at planned staffing? Clinicians, chairs, consultants, project slots and onboarding throughput all cap the answer regardless of demand. If projected demand exceeds what the schedule can absorb, the obtainable market is the capacity figure until a hiring plan with dates changes it.
Pipeline mechanics. Take the current or planned number of sellers or intake staff, their realistic qualified opportunity volume per month, the historical conversion rate from qualified opportunity to closed business, and the average sales cycle length. Multiply forward across the period, and remember that a cycle longer than the forecast window pushes revenue into the following year. For organizations with a long authorization or procurement step, this alone can move a meaningful share of revenue into the next period, so model it explicitly.
Distribution evidence. Count what exists: current lead flow by channel, signed referral or partner agreements, waitlists, contracted payer relationships, existing customer expansion rights. Planned channels are assumptions and belong in the scenario layer, not the base case.
Ramp. New locations, new sellers and new channels do not start at steady state. Model the ramp with a start month and a productivity curve rather than applying an annual average that assumes day-one maturity.
Each of those inputs depends on first-party data you already own: CRM stage history, call tracking, scheduling and admissions records, and attribution that follows a lead through to a closed customer or a completed admission rather than stopping at the form fill. A sizing model is only as good as the measurement system feeding it, which is why attribution work and market sizing tend to improve together. Where you need sourced external evidence for an input, use work you can cite and date. Our [published research hub](/research) shows how sourced research is documented rather than asserted.
Keep the units consistent. If capacity is counted in episodes but the pipeline is counted in closed customers, the two halves of the model never meet, which is the same definitional error the first section warns about.
Table 2. Illustrative obtainable-market structure. Letters are placeholders for your own measured inputs, not findings.
* 1. Input: Qualified opportunities per month, all channels. Placeholder: E. Evidence type: Measured lead flow, first-party.
* 2. Input: Qualified-to-closed rate. Placeholder: F. Evidence type: CRM history.
* 3. Input: Months of selling in the period, net of cycle length. Placeholder: G. Evidence type: CRM history and forecast window.
* 4. Input: Units delivered per closed customer. Placeholder: H. Evidence type: Operating history.
* 5. Input: Demand-side obtainable units. Placeholder: E x F x G x H. Evidence type: Calculated.
* 6. Input: Delivery capacity in the period, same unit. Placeholder: I. Evidence type: Staffing and facilities plan.
* 7. Input: Serviceable obtainable market. Placeholder: Lower of row 5 and row 6. Evidence type: Calculated.
The output is a number you can trace to a person, a shift or a contract. When the obtainable figure lands far below the serviceable market, that is not a weakness in the model, it is the signal the model exists to produce: the binding constraint is capacity or distribution, and the investment case should address that constraint rather than the size of the universe. Douglas Insights lists capacity and supply planning alongside market entry among the situations where sizing applies ([Douglas Insights](https://www.douglasinsights.com/consulting/market-sizing/)).
Reconcile bottom-up and top-down views honestly
A bottom-up model built from units, rates and values should be checked against a top-down figure built from an industry or trade estimate scaled to your geography and segment. The point of the second view is not a second opinion to choose from. It is a diagnostic, and the discipline is common practice among sizing teams: Douglas Insights describes modeling bottom-up by volume and price, then cross-checking against reported revenues and capacity so the model closes ([Douglas Insights](https://www.douglasinsights.com/consulting/market-sizing/)).
Build the top-down view by taking a national or regional category total, allocating it to your footprint with a defensible allocator such as population share, establishment share or insured-lives share, then filtering to the segment you serve. Record the allocator and its source, because the allocator is usually where the error lives. Population-share allocation assumes demand distributes evenly with people, which is rarely true for specialized services concentrated around referral centers, employers or payer networks.
Then compare. Small divergence is normal and not worth resolving. Large divergence is information. When the top-down figure is much larger, the usual causes are a category definition that includes adjacent services you do not sell, double counting across the value chain, or a national total that includes institutional segments closed to you. When the bottom-up figure is larger, the usual causes are an eligibility rate applied too generously, a value per unit based on list price, or a service area drawn wider than real travel patterns.
Investigate in a fixed order. Check definitions first, because most gaps are definitional. Check the denominator second: are both views counting the same geography and the same time period? Check value third: gross against net, annual against lifetime, revenue against contribution. Only after those three should you conclude that one view is simply wrong.
What you should not do is pick the flattering number and delete the other one. Present both, show the gap, and state which view you are using for planning and why. A model that names the bottom-up view as the planning basis because it rests on measured conversion and capacity data, while explaining that the top-down view sits higher because the category definition includes services you do not offer, is more credible than a single confident figure.
Be clear as well about which research discipline you are buying when you commission outside help. Teknicks separates market sizing and opportunity work from competitive intelligence, industry trend analysis, win/loss analysis and market entry research as distinct components of a research service ([Teknicks](https://www.teknicks.com/services/research/market-research/)). They answer different questions, and a sizing study will not tell you why you lose deals. Our overview of [research methods and resources](/resources/research) explains how these methods fit together in an evidence plan.
Present uncertainty with ranges and dated inputs
A single-point market size signals false precision. A range signals that you know which inputs are soft. Build the model so every assumption can be flexed, then publish low, mid and high scenarios with the specific input changes that produce each one.
Keep the scenario logic honest. The low case should not be the mid case minus an arbitrary haircut. It should be the mid case with named inputs set to their pessimistic end: eligibility at the lower bound of the published range, close rate at last year's actual rather than the plan, hiring delayed by a quarter. The high case does the same in reverse, and it should still respect capacity, because no amount of demand optimism adds clinicians who were never hired.
Table 3. Illustrative sensitivity structure. Each cell holds your own input at its low, mid or high setting.
* Low. Eligibility rate setting: Lower bound of sourced range. Close rate setting: Trailing actual. Capacity setting: Current staffed capacity. Obtainable value: Calculated from these inputs.
* Mid. Eligibility rate setting: Central sourced estimate. Close rate setting: Trailing actual adjusted for mix. Capacity setting: Planned capacity, ramped. Obtainable value: Planning basis.
* High. Eligibility rate setting: Upper bound of sourced range. Close rate setting: Best sustained performance. Capacity setting: Planned capacity, fully hired. Obtainable value: Capped by capacity.
Every row in the worksheet carries four metadata fields: the value, the source, the date it was sourced in YYYY-MM-DD form, and a confidence label of measured, estimated or assumed. Measured means it came from your own systems with a defined period. Estimated means it came from an external dataset with a vintage. Assumed means someone decided it, and the name of that someone belongs in the cell comment. This single habit does more for defensibility than any additional analysis, because it lets a reviewer see exactly where the model is soft without reverse engineering it.
Set a revisit rule as well. Note when each input should be refreshed: public population data on its publication cycle, internal conversion rates quarterly, capacity whenever the staffing plan changes, payer and licensure status whenever a contract moves. A stale input that nobody has revisited for two years is the fastest way to lose credibility in a diligence conversation.

Questions founders and executives ask about sizing
### Why is assuming one percent market share usually weak reasoning?
Because it reverses the direction of the analysis. Share is an output of capacity, distribution and competitive position, so presenting it as an input hides every assumption a reviewer wants to test. Build the figure from throughput instead, as described in the section on estimating reach, and if the result happens to land near one percent you will have a reason for it.
### How should a service business calculate its market size?
Count delivery capacity and demand separately, then take the lower figure. Services are constrained by people and hours, so define the unit as an engagement, an episode or a billable period, value it at net realized revenue rather than list rate, and cap the obtainable layer at what current and planned staff can deliver. Geography is usually a travel-time and referral-pattern boundary rather than a statistical one. Public tools can establish the denominator, and Census guidance points to questions such as how many competitors operate in an area and how many people each existing business serves on average ([U.S. Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/topics/business-economy/small-business/about/powerful-data.html)).
### What should a market-size model show when evidence is sparse?
It should show the gap rather than fill it. Mark the input as assumed, state the range you believe is plausible, name what would resolve it, and show how much the answer moves across that range. If a small swing in an eligibility rate changes the total dramatically, the model is telling you where to spend research budget. Sparse evidence is a reason to widen the range and prioritize primary research, not to adopt a confident-looking figure from an unattributed source.
### How should a small team adapt this approach?
Keep the structure and shrink the scope. One service line, one geography, one unit of demand, and a worksheet short enough to read in a single screen. Use free public tools for the counting layer; the Census Business Builder filter tool lets you narrow a target market using up to three of its available variables, which is enough for an initial segment and footprint ([U.S. Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/topics/business-economy/small-business/about/powerful-data.html)). Pull conversion and capacity numbers from whatever operating records exist, even if that is a spreadsheet of last year's intakes. A small, fully sourced model beats a large, partially sourced one in every review that matters.
### What evidence would change the recommended approach?
Three things. First, a long or committee-driven buying process, which means the pipeline layer needs opportunity-stage data rather than lead counts. Second, a capacity-unconstrained model such as software, where the binding limit moves to distribution and payback rather than delivery. Third, strong primary data, such as a representative survey of the defined buyer population, which can replace a public eligibility proxy with a measured rate and materially narrow the range. If none of those apply, the sequence stays as written: unit, total, constraints, capacity, reconciliation, range.
If one assumption in your model is carrying more weight than the evidence supports, that is the conversation worth having.
