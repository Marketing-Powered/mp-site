Meta Health Event Restrictions for Peptide Marketers
Most teams searching for Meta health event restrictions peptides are looking at one blocked custom conversion, not a disabled ad account. Those are different decisions with different remediation paths. The fix almost always starts with what data reached Meta, not with what the event was called.

Identify which object Meta actually restricted
Before anyone touches an event, write down the object type, the object ID, the date, and the exact notice text. Meta's developer documentation states that beginning September 2, 2025 it will start rolling out more proactive restrictions on custom conversions that may suggest information not permitted under its terms, naming conditions such as arthritis and diabetes as examples, and that flagged conversions will be prevented from being used to run ad campaigns ([Meta Marketing API, Custom Conversions](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/marketing-api/reference/custom-conversion/)). [[1]](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/marketing-api/reference/custom-conversion/) That is an object-level classification, not a verdict on the advertiser.
The same page is specific about what the restriction does to delivery. You will not be able to use a flagged custom conversion when creating new campaigns. If an active campaign already uses one, the documentation says to create a new campaign or duplicate the campaign and use a non-impacted custom conversion, to avoid performance and optimization issues. Read that as a campaign-object problem. It is not, by itself, an account disablement.
For API-managed accounts, the same documentation says the is_unavailable field returns true when a custom conversion has been flagged, and that attempting to modify a flagged conversion returns an error whose user-facing title names the custom conversion as blocked ([Meta Marketing API, Custom Conversions](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/marketing-api/reference/custom-conversion/)). [[1]](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/marketing-api/reference/custom-conversion/) Read the field and the object ID rather than the sentence in the alert. The generic error text in that documented response refers to a custom audience, even when the title names the conversion.
Audiences are a separate object with a parallel rule. Meta's customer file custom audiences documentation describes the same September 2, 2025 rollout for custom audiences and lookalikes that suggest specific health conditions, again using arthritis and diabetes as examples, and says flagged audiences cannot be used when creating new campaigns ([Customer File Custom Audiences](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/marketing-api/audiences/guides/custom-audiences/)). [[2]](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/marketing-api/audiences/guides/custom-audiences/v25.0/) Individual ads and the ad account itself surface through separate notices and separate queues. Treating a flagged conversion as an account emergency sends the team into the wrong queue.
Inspect the meaning your payload carries
Custom conversions are rules, and rules read your site. Meta's own example defines a purchase conversion by matching URLs that contain thankyou.html, using a case-insensitive substring operator ([Meta Marketing API, Custom Conversions](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/marketing-api/reference/custom-conversion/)). [[1]](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/marketing-api/reference/custom-conversion/) If the matching URL is a path like /glp-1-weight-management/consult-booked, the conversion carries condition meaning even when the conversion itself is named something neutral. Capitalization and a neutral event name do not hide a substring the rule is built to match.
Build a sanitized map of every signal before you argue about any of them. For an illustrative peptide funnel, that map covers the conversion name, the full rule definition, the event name, content_name and content_category values, quiz or intake answers passed in query strings, the referring URL, and any product or protocol name embedded in the path. Check what the server sends as well as what the browser sends. A Conversions API call that still includes the old path, a protocol name, or an intake answer is the same inference, moved to a different pipe.
Health information is broader than most measurement specs assume. The FTC describes it as anything that conveys information or enables an inference about a consumer's health, including browsing or purchase data, and advises businesses to take a broad view ([FTC business guidance on consumer health information](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach)). [[3]](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach) A product page view can qualify. For the tracking-layer view of this problem, see [Meta Pixel HIPAA compliance](/resources/meta-pixel-hipaa/).
Evaluate permitted measurement under law and platform terms
Platform terms and legal obligation are two separate analyses, and clearing one does not clear the other. HIPAA rules apply to covered entities, meaning health plans, providers conducting standard transactions electronically, and clearinghouses, as well as to business associates ([FTC business guidance on consumer health information](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach)). [[3]](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach) Many peptide sellers and direct-to-consumer telehealth brands sit outside that definition and conclude they are unregulated. That conclusion is wrong.
The FTC Act's obligations apply to HIPAA-covered entities and business associates as well as to companies handling health information that are not required to comply with HIPAA. The agency points to its BetterHelp, GoodRx, and Premom matters as establishing that disclosing consumers' health information for advertising without affirmative express consent may be an unfair practice ([FTC business guidance on consumer health information](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach)). [[3]](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach) The same guidance warns against false or misleading claims that a business is "HIPAA Compliant," "HIPAA Secure," "HIPAA Certified," or the like.
Some businesses outside HIPAA may still have duties under the FTC Health Breach Notification Rule if they are vendors of personal health records, related entities, or service providers to those vendors. The FTC describes a breach under that rule as including an unauthorized disclosure by the company itself, not only an outside intrusion ([FTC business guidance on consumer health information](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach)). [[3]](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach) Whether that rule applies is a legal question for counsel, not a setting in Events Manager.
Keep four questions separate: whether the business operates lawfully, what the product's regulatory status is, whether the channel permits the advertising, and whether the account is in good standing. Measurement design follows all four. A platform review that restores an event does not answer the consent question, and a privacy review that limits what you send does not, by itself, restore delivery. Outcome-side measurement is covered in [healthcare marketing attribution through admissions](/services/analytics-and-attribution/).
Correct the data flow instead of the label
Renaming a flagged event while the same payload keeps flowing is not remediation. It leaves the inference intact, and it reads as concealment rather than correction. Removal is the defensible move. Events Manager also will not let you edit the rule. Meta's help documentation says you can edit the name, description, and conversion value of a custom conversion, and that you cannot edit the rules ([How to edit custom conversions in Meta Events Manager](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/297192884523482)). [[4]](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/297192884523482) A rename leaves the matching logic in place.
A workable sequence looks like this. Inventory every event and the fields it sends. Decide, at the source, which values should never leave your systems, including condition terms, protocol names, and intake answers. Restructure URLs so the path and query string no longer describe a condition, and strip residual parameters server side before the event is sent. Only then rebuild the conversion with a neutral definition that describes a commercial action, such as a completed booking or a completed purchase, without naming a condition, a protocol, or an intake result.
Meta's documentation offers three resolution paths for flagged conversions: create a new custom conversion, select a different existing one, or duplicate a running campaign and select a non-flagged conversion before publishing. It notes that once the campaign is published you cannot remove or change the custom conversion ([Meta Marketing API, Custom Conversions](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/marketing-api/reference/custom-conversion/)). [[1]](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/marketing-api/reference/custom-conversion/) Duplicate first, confirm the new object is the one selected, and only then publish. Editing the live campaign after publish is not one of the documented paths.
The same reference caps custom conversions at 100 per ad account, so retiring dead objects is part of the cleanup, not housekeeping you defer. Archive or delete conversions you no longer use before you create the replacement, and record the old object ID so a later report does not treat the gap as a tracking outage. Related channel rules are summarized in [Facebook ads for healthcare](/resources/facebook-ads-for-healthcare/).
Use the documented review route correctly
Review exists for disputed classification, not for pushing a sensitive payload through a second time. Meta's documentation states that if you believe a custom conversion was flagged in error and does not include non-permitted information, you can request a review in Ads Manager under the campaigns table, or in Events Manager under the custom conversions page ([Meta Marketing API, Custom Conversions](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/marketing-api/reference/custom-conversion/)). [[1]](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/marketing-api/reference/custom-conversion/) Use that route only after the rule, the URL, and the parameters are clean.
Before filing, assemble the record: the exact rule definition, a sanitized example of a matching URL, the full parameter list, the date the payload changed, and who approved the change. If any of that still carries condition meaning, fix the flow first and create a clean object instead. Resubmitting an unchanged signal asks the platform to approve a case you have not corrected, and it can draw further scrutiny of the same data source.
Document the decision either way. A short internal log of what was removed, when, and on whose authority is what makes the next conversation with a platform, a partner, or counsel short. Keep the log free of patient or customer identifiers. Store the object ID, the rule text, the approver, and the date. A broader operational list sits in the [healthcare marketing compliance checklist](/resources/healthcare-marketing-compliance-checklist/).
Explain what measurement you keep and what you lose
Be explicit with stakeholders, because a clean fix still costs signal. What usually survives is a commercial action described without a condition: a completed inquiry, a scheduled consult, or a purchase, reported in aggregate. Consented and lawful contact matching, geo or holdout testing, and CRM-side reporting that follows an inquiry through to a closed outcome can still carry the business question. What does not survive is condition-level conversion granularity, audiences built from sensitive inference, and any optimization that depended on the removed field.
Set expectations on timing too. Rebuilt conversions start with no history, so delivery systems relearn, and week-over-week comparisons across the change are not like for like. Plan a measurement window rather than reading the first few days as damage. Tell finance which reports will break, which object IDs were retired, and which downstream CRM stage is now the source of truth for a closed outcome.
This pattern is not new to regulated advertisers. Google treats personal health content as a sensitive interest category and does not allow advertiser-curated audiences for that content, while predefined Google audiences remain available because sensitive signals are excluded from them ([Health in personalized advertising](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16701855?hl=en)). [[5]](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/16701855?hl=en) Teams working in that vertical carry the measurement load in first-party systems and track attribution to admission rather than to lead.
Marketing Powered is LegitScript-aware and HIPAA-aware, and built for Google's sensitive healthcare vertical, where retargeting is not permitted. More resource topics
### Diagnosing a peptide advertising restriction
A restriction notice arrives with a policy name, a timestamp, and very little else, and the instinct is to edit something immediately. Start with the object ID and the notice text before you change a rule, a URL, or an appeal.
### Peptide ad appeal evidence packet
An appeal is not a persuasive letter with attachments bolted on. The useful packet is the rule definition, a sanitized matching URL, the parameter list, and the date the payload changed.
### FDA approval, registration, and certification in peptide marketing
Peptide businesses accumulate official-looking records: an NDC number, a facility registration, a certificate of analysis, a merchant certification. Those records answer a product-status question. They do not answer whether Meta will accept a conversion rule, or whether a disclosure to an ad platform is lawful.
