Multi-Location Healthcare Reporting by Market and Site
Multi-location healthcare marketing reporting is a standard, site-level view of spend, qualified demand and verified admissions built on definitions that every location shares. The comparison is only as trustworthy as those definitions. If your CEO asks how 24 locations compare and gets three different answers, the cause is usually that marketing, intake and admissions are each counting something different, not that the dashboard is broken.

What a multi-location report should show
A useful report answers one question at each level of the organization: where did acquisition spend go, what did it produce, and how confident are we in that number? The structure matters more than the tool you use to build it.
Build the view on four dimensions, applied consistently across every site:
* Site: the physical or virtual location that delivers care, whether a treatment center, hospital campus or clinic group.
* Market: the geography or region that groups sites, which matters when several locations share a service area and compete for the same searchers.
* Service line: the type of care being sought, such as inpatient residential, intensive outpatient, outpatient therapy, psychiatric services or a hospital specialty like cardiology or orthopedics.
* Stage: where in the chain the number sits, from spend to lead to qualified call to verified admission.
Each row should carry its denominator and a confidence label. A cost-per-admission figure built on a handful of admissions is not the same kind of number as one built on a full quarter of volume, and the report should say so rather than letting leadership assume they are comparable. Platform dashboards will not give you this view by themselves; they report by campaign and account, and local and national spend often overlap in ways that obscure site-level performance. The multi-location report is something you assemble from ad platforms, call tracking, intake records and admission outcomes, with the joins documented so anyone reading it can trace where each number came from.
When this applies to behavioral and mental health organizations
Behavioral and mental health providers feel this problem early because the chain between a search query and an admission is short, human-dependent and capacity-constrained. Inquiries often arrive by phone, intake handling varies by site and by shift, payer verification determines whether a call can become an admission, and bed or clinician availability changes week to week. A call can be perfectly qualified on every measurable criterion and still not convert because the site had no space that week.
Hospitals and health systems face the same structural problem with different details. Demand is split across multiple facilities and service lines, scheduling and intake friction sit between the campaign and the appointment or admission, and leadership wants facility-level measurement without losing sight of system-wide performance. The method described here applies to both contexts. Behavioral health is the sharpest example because the volume per site is lower and capacity constraints are more visible, but a hospital group running 40 service lines across six campuses has an identical definitional problem at larger scale.
If you operate a single site with one intake team and one budget owner, most of this framework is unnecessary overhead. The method pays for itself when you have several sites, shared or overlapping budgets, more than one intake team, or leadership that asks cross-site comparison questions on a regular cadence.
Fix the definitions before you compare sites
The report is a contract between marketing, intake and finance. Write that contract before you build any dashboard. The table below defines what each stage means, who owns it, where the data comes from, and how confident the number should be treated as being. This is not a theoretical exercise; it is the document you pull out when two departments disagree about whether a call was "qualified."
* Spend. Definition to agree in writing: What is included: media fees, agency costs, call tracking, shared overhead and how they are allocated across sites. Typical owner: Finance with marketing. Data source: Ad platforms, accounting ledger. Confidence label: Measured.
* Lead. Definition to agree in writing: Which inbound actions count: calls above a duration threshold, form submissions with required fields, chat sessions with intent signals. Typical owner: Marketing operations. Data source: Call tracking, form records, CRM. Confidence label: Measured.
* Qualified call; Definition to agree in writing: The intake criteria a call must meet: service fit, geographic eligibility, payer confirmation, clinical appropriateness; Typical owner: Intake or call center lead; Data source: Call tracking plus logged disposition codes; Confidence label: Measured where dispositions are consistently logged; estimated otherwise
* Verified admission. Definition to agree in writing: A confirmed admission or first appointment tied back to the originating lead through a shared identifier. Typical owner: Admissions or operations. Data source: CRM record or de-identified EHR extract. Confidence label: Measured only where the data join exists and has been validated.
The "qualified call" row is where most disputes start. Marketing may consider a lead qualified because it matched a high-intent keyword and stayed on the line for four minutes. Intake may consider that same call unqualified because payer verification failed or the patient was outside the service area. Neither definition is wrong, but the report cannot mix them without producing misleading comparisons. Pick one definition per stage, name the owner who can change it, log the date of any change, and make sure every site applies the same criteria regardless of which intake team answered the phone.
### Location CPA and what it hides
Location cost per acquisition is the number most leadership teams use to rank sites because it is a single ratio that looks comparable at a glance. It often is not comparable, because both the numerator and the denominator are shaped by factors outside marketing's control:
* Intake handling: response speed, disposition practices and after-hours coverage differ by site and by team composition.
* Payer mix: a site in a market with narrower commercial payer coverage will lose more qualified calls during verification than one in a broader-payer region.
* Capacity: a site at full occupancy turns away calls that a site with open beds would admit, creating an artificial gap in conversion rates that has nothing to do with marketing quality.
* Lead definition drift: if one site counts any inbound call and another counts only calls above two minutes with a stated service need, the CPAs describe fundamentally different populations.
Consider this illustrative scenario, not client data: two sites show identical location CPA for the quarter. Site A is generating many calls that are out of area or off-service but still being counted as leads in the numerator. Site B is generating qualified calls consistently but has a three-week waitlist and cannot admit them. Moving budget from B to A based on CPA alone would reward volume generation over actual admission production. The report needs a capacity status flag and a qualified-call conversion rate beside the CPA so the reader can see that two identical ratios are describing different operational realities.
How to compare acquisition across 24 locations
For an executive asking how 24 locations compare, the answer is not a single ranked list. It is a normalized view with context columns. Here is the process:
* Compare like with like. Group sites by service line and market type before any cross-site comparison. A rural outpatient site serving insurance-restricted populations should not share a leaderboard with an urban inpatient site with broader payer coverage.
* Show the full chain, not one number. Present spend, qualified calls, verified admissions and the cost at each transition stage side by side so the reader can see where the drop-off happens.
* Flag low-volume sites. Agree with finance on a minimum admission or call volume below which a site's per-unit costs are shown as directional rather than ranked. A site with four admissions in a quarter produces a cost figure that is statistically fragile, and presenting it alongside a site with 80 admissions implies false precision.
* Show capacity status. Add an open, near-capacity or full flag per site so the reader can separate demand generation problems from throughput constraints.
* Label confidence visibly. Measured, estimated and unknown should use different visual treatment in the report so a decision-maker does not weight them equally.
### Local versus national spend allocation
Shared campaigns are the hardest part of location-level reporting because they serve multiple sites simultaneously. A regional search campaign may target three locations, while a local brand campaign targets one site but attracts callers from neighboring markets. Write the allocation rule down before the quarter starts: allocate by call origin phone number, by service-area geography, or by a fixed split agreed with finance. Apply that rule consistently and report shared spend as its own line item, then show the allocated view beside it so nobody mistakes an allocation model for a measurement. Check for overlap where two campaigns serve the same searcher in the same area to avoid double-crediting a single conversion to multiple sites.
Measuring against qualified calls and admissions
Admission attribution is the step that converts a marketing report into an acquisition accountability tool. It requires joining the originating lead, usually a tracked call or form submission, to the downstream record of an admission or first appointment. This join does not happen automatically; it requires a shared identifier carried through every system touchpoint.
A workable method: assign a persistent identifier at the lead stage, such as a call tracking number or form ID, and ensure that identifier is captured in the intake record. Have intake log a disposition code for every call using the agreed qualified definition. Have admissions or operations confirm which leads became verified admissions and return that confirmation to the analytics owner on a defined cadence. Report by lead cohort, grouped by the month the lead arrived rather than the month the admission occurred, because recent cohorts are still maturing and their outcomes have not yet materialized. Set a lag window per service line: an outpatient appointment may close within days while a residential or inpatient admission can take weeks.
Whether and how outcome data moves between systems depends on consent, contractual terms and de-identification requirements specific to each organization. A report built on aggregated, de-identified admission counts is often sufficient for budget allocation decisions without requiring granular patient-level data to flow into marketing tools. Service line metrics belong in the same structure: add the service line dimension to every row so a hospital can see demand and cost for each line at each facility, and a behavioral health group can separate inpatient from outpatient performance rather than blending them into an average that obscures both.
Implementation steps and owner roles
* Name an executive sponsor (CEO, COO or CMO) who has authority to settle definition disputes between departments when marketing and intake cannot agree on what "qualified" means. Owner: executive sponsor.
* Write the definition contract using the table above, with each department signing off on their stage's criteria. Owner: marketing operations lead, with intake and finance sign-off.
* Map the data sources for spend, calls, intake dispositions and admissions, and document who can export each dataset in what format. Owner: analytics or data lead.
* Set allocation rules for shared campaigns and local versus national overlap, documented in writing before the next budget cycle. Owner: finance with marketing input.
* Build or validate the identifier join between lead ID and admission outcome, with privacy or compliance review on which fields move across systems. Owner: analytics lead with privacy or compliance review.
* Build the scorecard layout with site, market, service line, stage costs, capacity flag, sample-size flag and confidence label as columns. Owner: analytics lead.
* Set a joint review cadence where marketing, intake and admissions review the same report page in one meeting, so budget changes follow the documented definitions rather than competing narratives. Owner: executive sponsor.
Regional campaign controls and multi-site budget governance sit on top of this structure. Decide who can shift budget between sites, what evidence level is required to trigger a reallocation, and how frequently those decisions are made. Mistakes, constraints and what the data cannot prove
Common errors that undermine a multi-location report:
* Ranking on location CPA alone. It hides capacity, payer mix and lead definition differences, producing false signals for budget reallocation.
* Letting each department keep its own definition of qualified. The report then displays organizational disagreement as if it were performance variance.
* Reporting by admission month instead of lead cohort month. Recent cohorts look weak because their outcomes have not yet arrived, creating an artificial downward trend at the tail of every quarter.
* Treating allocation as measurement. A fixed split of shared spend is a governance rule, not a measured result, and presenting it without that label misleads readers into thinking the platform attributed those conversions.
* Over-interpreting small-sample sites. Three or four admissions can swing a per-unit cost figure by 50 percent or more, making the number unreliable for decision-making.
What the data cannot prove, even when the report is well built:
* Call-to-admission gaps where dispositions are not logged or admissions cannot be matched to leads. Those stages remain estimated, and the report must say so explicitly rather than presenting them as measured.
* Multi-touch causality. A person may encounter a search ad, read reviews, receive a referral from a physician and then call. A site-level report shows which tracked source received the final interaction, not the full causal chain.
* Platform measurement limitations in healthcare advertising. Major platforms impose restrictions on sensitive-category audiences, retargeting and conversion tracking for regulated verticals, which can limit the precision of attribution models. These rules change over time; treat them as a current constraint rather than a permanent one.
* Clinical outcomes or care quality. Acquisition reporting measures whether someone arrived; it says nothing about the effectiveness, safety or experience of the treatment they received and should never be used to imply it.
