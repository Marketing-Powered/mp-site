Can Peptide Businesses Advertise? An Eligibility Framework
The question "can peptide companies advertise" usually gets asked too late, after a budget is approved and a landing page is already in staging. Legality, certification and platform permission are three different answers, and a fourth question sits ahead of all of them: what the business actually does. This page walks those four checks in the order that protects spend.

Classify the actual offer before anything else
Advertising policy is written around business models, not around molecules. Before anyone debates whether a peptide is lawful, write down which of these the company actually operates:
Licensed clinical care and prescribing. A clinic or telehealth group that evaluates patients and prescribes, with a pharmacy partner preparing the product.
Pharmacy dispensing and compounding. The entity preparing the drug product itself, under a specific regulatory pathway.
Laboratory and research supply. Sale of materials to commercial labs and institutions, with no patient relationship and no human-use positioning.
Lawful consumer products. Supplements and cosmetic or topical goods sold directly to consumers.
The classification is not cosmetic. Google tells applicants to identify the specific healthcare certification type required by the services or products advertised, lists Online Pharmacy, Addiction Services, Telemedicine, Health Insurance and Pharmaceutical manufacturer as examples, and states plainly that you should not apply for a category that does not match your business model. It also notes that some certifications require third-party accreditation first, for example from LegitScript, NABP or G2 ([Google Ads pharmaceutical manufacturers policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15597836?hl=en)).
Run this step as a working session, not an email thread. The people who need to be in the room are the executive who owns the P&L, the medical or clinical lead, whoever manages the pharmacy or supplier relationship, compliance or counsel, and the person who will actually build the campaigns. The artifacts they should put on the table are the prescribing workflow, the pharmacy or supply agreement, the product label, the certificate of analysis, and the current website copy. If two people in that room describe the business differently, the eligibility question cannot be answered yet.
Establish legal status and intended use with qualified review
Legal to sell is necessary. It is not sufficient, and it is not the same as approved.
On October 28, 2026, HHS filed a Federal Register notice announcing the availability of its "Enforcement Discretion Policy Regarding 503A Compounding Using Certain Peptide Bulk Drug Substances," covering six peptides and describing conditions under which FDA intends to refrain from taking specified enforcement action against qualifying compounders ([LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/healthcare/hhs-peptide-compounding-policy-what-healthcare-businesses-need-to-know/)). The same source is explicit that enforcement discretion does not approve the compounded products, add the substances to the final 503A bulks list, or remove other applicable legal requirements, and that the policy does not mean these peptides are FDA-approved or that all formulations, uses or compounding arrangements are permitted.
The detail matters more than the headline. The policy identifies specific forms and permitted routes of administration for each covered peptide, applies to qualifying 503A pharmacies and physician compounders, and does not cover 503B outsourcing facilities. Its ten conditions include a single active pharmaceutical ingredient, sourcing from a facility registered under section 510 of the FD&C Act with a valid certificate of analysis, required labeling reading "This is a compounded drug product. Not for resale," documentation of oral bioavailability for oral preparations, and a limit on co-packaged delivery devices to an FDA 510(k)-cleared syringe.
Intended use is the second half of this check. Human therapeutic use, research use and topical or cosmetic use pull in different rule sets, and the same compound can sit in different categories depending on how it is sourced, labeled and described. This page is an operational framework, not a legal or regulatory determination. Product-specific status, state requirements and clinical positioning need qualified counsel and clinical review before anything is published. Check what your certification actually covers
Certification is scoped. It attaches to an entity, a domain and a defined set of activities, and it says nothing about activities outside that scope.
LegitScript updated its certification review approach effective November 2, 2026 to reflect the enforcement discretion policy's conditional allowances, and its stated position is that offering one of the covered peptides should not by itself prevent a merchant from achieving certification when the offering meets the policy's conditions, all other applicable Certification Standards, and the laws and regulations governing compounding. Offering other peptides or formulations that fall outside the policy, and that are not otherwise eligible for compounding under Section 503A or 503B, remains a barrier to certification ([LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/healthcare/hhs-peptide-compounding-policy-what-healthcare-businesses-need-to-know/)).
Two consequences follow for anyone building a media plan.
First, the review is granular. LegitScript states that it will consider the specific ingredient, formulation, route of administration, compounding facility, supply chain compliance and other relevant requirements. A change to the pharmacy partner or the formulation is a change to the thing that was reviewed.
Second, certification is not channel permission. LegitScript says directly that it does not control the policies of advertising platforms, payment processors, acquirers or card brands, that its review updates do not change those independent requirements, and that certification does not secure access to their services. It also warns that where a platform intends to update its policies, implementation may take time and may not align with LegitScript's timeline, so businesses should confirm each platform's current requirements and the effective date of any change.
Treat certification as one passed gate on a list, recorded with its scope and date. Our [healthcare advertising compliance guide](/resources/compliance-ad-policy) covers how that record fits the wider review process.
Assess each advertising channel on its own policy
Platforms do not inherit anyone else's conclusion. They publish their own rules, apply their own certification process, and enforce against the account.
Google states that some healthcare content cannot be advertised at all, while other content can only be advertised in certain locations by advertisers who have applied and been approved, and that ads and destinations are expected to follow appropriate laws and industry standards. Its unapproved substances rules prohibit promotion of specified products regardless of any claims of legality, including items on its unapproved pharmaceuticals and supplements list, herbal and dietary supplements with active pharmaceutical or dangerous ingredients, products that imply they are as effective as prescription drugs or controlled substances, and non-government-approved products marketed as safe or effective for preventing, curing or treating a disease or ailment. Promotion of speculative or experimental medical treatments is also not allowed, and promotion of services related to online prescribing, dispensing and sale of prescription drugs is restricted to advertisers who apply ([Google Ads healthcare and medicines policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031)).
For the supply side, Google permits bulk drug manufacturers, medical professional suppliers, and suppliers of antibodies, peptides and compounds for commercial labs to advertise in Canada and the United States only, and requires pharmaceutical manufacturers to be certified by Google to serve ads. That page also notes that violations will not lead to immediate suspension without prior warning, with a warning issued at least seven days beforehand ([Google Ads pharmaceutical manufacturers policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15597836?hl=en)).
Meta and every other channel on the plan need the same treatment: open the current policy, read the version in force today, and record what it permits for your exact offer and geography. Build a simple source ledger with policy name, URL, retrieval date, reviewer, permitted activity and geographic limit, one row per channel. Illustrative scenarios, not eligibility rulings
The following are hypothetical business types used to show how the four layers interact. None of them is a statement that any real product, company or campaign is eligible.
Scenario A: a telehealth group prescribing a compounded peptide. The layers run offer classification, then whether the specific ingredient form, route and compounding arrangement sit inside the enforcement discretion policy's 503A scope and conditions, then whether certification covers that offering, then whether each platform's prescription drug services or telemedicine category permits it in the target geography. Failure at any layer stops the launch.
Scenario B: a supplier selling research material to commercial labs. Classification is straightforward, but geography is a hard constraint under Google's supplier allowance, and any drift of site copy toward human use moves the offer into categories where promotion is prohibited regardless of claimed legality.
Scenario C: a cosmetic brand marketing a peptide-containing topical. The product may be an ordinary consumer good until the copy implies drug-like effect, at which point disease-treatment and unapproved-substance rules become the operative policy.
Scenario D: a supplement brand adding peptide positioning. The ingredient profile and the claims decide the outcome, not the word on the label.
The pattern across all four: wording does not change what the business does, and the platform reviews the destination as well as the ad. For what certification itself involves, see our guide to [LegitScript certification](/resources/legitscript-certification).
Document the decision, the reviewer and the recheck date
A peptide eligibility decision that lives in someone's memory is not a decision. Record it as a dated matrix, one row per offer and channel, with these fields: offer description and classification, the legal and intended-use evidence reviewed with the name of the qualified reviewer and the date, certification status and its exact scope, the platform policy consulted with its URL and retrieval date, the geographic limits that apply, the decision taken (launch, limited launch or hold), the responsible approver, and the recheck date.
Set the recheck date deliberately. This area moved twice inside one week in late 2026, and LegitScript has cautioned that platform implementation may lag and may differ from its own timeline. A sensible operating cadence is a fixed review window plus a confirmation pass immediately before any new campaign goes live, with the ledger updated rather than replaced so the history stays visible.
This is documentation discipline and risk reduction, not compliance certification. What it buys is a defensible record of who decided what, on what evidence, and when it expires.

Questions executives ask before approving peptide spend
### Does legal sale automatically mean an ad is permitted?
No. Google's healthcare rules prohibit promotion of specified products regardless of any claims of legality, and restrict other categories to approved advertisers in listed locations ([Google Ads healthcare and medicines policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031)). Lawful operation and channel permission are separate determinations with separate evidence.
### Does LegitScript certification mean platform approval?
No. LegitScript states that it does not control the policies of advertising platforms, payment processors, acquirers or card brands, that its review updates do not change those independent requirements, and that certification does not secure access to their services ([LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/healthcare/hhs-peptide-compounding-policy-what-healthcare-businesses-need-to-know/)). Certification is one gate. The platform is another, and so is your payment stack.
### Are all research-use-only products treated the same?
No. Google permits suppliers of antibodies, peptides and compounds for commercial labs to advertise in Canada and the United States only ([Google Ads pharmaceutical manufacturers policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15597836?hl=en)), which is a narrow allowance tied to the customer and the geography. A research-use label does not travel with the product into human-use marketing, and site content, imagery and keywords are part of what gets reviewed.
### Can supplement or cosmetic wording change a product classification?
Wording changes how a review reads the offer, and it can make an otherwise ordinary product reviewable under healthcare rules. It does not change the underlying activity, the ingredient or the regulatory status. Google's rules reach products that imply they are as effective as prescription drugs and non-government-approved products marketed as safe or effective for treating a disease or ailment ([Google Ads healthcare and medicines policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031)). Rewriting copy to clear a review while the offer stays the same is the fastest route to an account action.
### Who should sign off on eligibility?
One named approver, usually the executive accountable for the P&L, signing on evidence supplied by qualified legal or regulatory review for product status, by the clinical or pharmacy lead for formulation and workflow, and by the media owner for the dated platform policy record. Agencies and consultants can assemble the evidence. They are not a substitute for the regulatory review or for the signature.
Four layers, in order: what the business does, what the product is and how it is intended to be used, what the certification actually covers, and what each channel permits today. If any layer is unresolved, the honest answer is not yet, and the cheapest place to find that out is before the media plan is funded.
