A Peptide Claims Knowledge Base With Human Approval
A peptide claims knowledge base stores what a source says, where it came from and when it was last checked. It cannot decide what is safe to publish, because that judgment belongs to a named human reviewer applying a substantiation standard. The job of the system is to make that boundary structural rather than aspirational.

Define the knowledge object before you build the retrieval layer
Most teams start this work by pointing a drafting tool at a folder. That produces a search box, not a claims system. The useful unit is a structured record with two linked but separate objects: the claim, and the evidence that someone believes supports it. Keeping them separate is what prevents a source update from silently rewriting approved copy, and what lets a reviewer answer the only question that matters at publish time: who decided this sentence was supportable, on what basis, and when.
The people involved should be visible in the schema. In practice that means an editorial owner who maintains the record, a regulatory or clinical reviewer who holds approval authority, and a requester who proposed the claim for a specific campaign. If a field cannot name a person, the accountability is theoretical.
Here is an illustrative field set for a single record in a peptide claims evidence library. The values are synthetic and used only to show shape.
* claim_id. Purpose: Stable identifier referenced by drafts and published pages. Illustrative value: CLM-0412.
* claim_text. Purpose: Exact wording cleared for use, not a paraphrase. Illustrative value: "Compound A has been studied in adults for X; results are preliminary"
* claim_type. Purpose: Descriptive, mechanistic, safety, outcome or regulatory-status. Illustrative value: Mechanistic.
* permitted_context. Purpose: Product category, audience, channel, jurisdiction. Illustrative value: Clinician-facing page, U.S., no paid social.
* prohibited_context. Purpose: Known disallowed uses of the wording. Illustrative value: Consumer ads, any disease framing.
* source_ids. Purpose: Linked evidence records, many to one. Illustrative value: SRC-0088, SRC-0121.
* source_limitations. Purpose: Why the evidence is narrower than the claim. Illustrative value: Animal model only; different route than marketed form.
* status. Purpose: draft, sourced, approved, suspended, retired. Illustrative value: Sourced.
* approver. Purpose: Named reviewer who holds publish authority. Illustrative value: Regulatory reviewer of record.
* approved_at. Purpose: Timestamp of the approval decision. Illustrative value: Populated only on approval.
* review_trigger. Purpose: Condition that forces re-review. Illustrative value: Source page update, new product category, 90-day cycle.
* version. Purpose: Immutable history of prior wording. Illustrative value: v3, with v1 and v2 retained.
Evidence records carry their own fields: source type, publisher, publication date, retrieval date, document location, storage rights, and a plain-language summary of what the source does and does not establish. The retrieval date matters as much as the publication date. Regulatory pages change without notice, and a record that cannot say when it last matched the live source is an assumption wearing the costume of a fact.
Separate evidence from approval, because they are different decisions
A retrieved document is an input. Authorization is an act. Conflating the two is the design flaw behind most "AI compliance" pitches, and it is expensive in health-adjacent categories because the legal standard sits before publication, not after. FTC staff guidance states that advertisers must have adequate substantiation for objective product claims conveyed expressly or by implication before an ad is disseminated, and that health benefit and safety claims generally require competent and reliable scientific evidence ([FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)). A retrieval system can surface a study in two seconds. It cannot hold that obligation.
Three failure patterns show why evidence alone is not authorization.
The source does not match the product. FTC guidance asks advertisers to compare dosage, formulation, route of administration, outcome measures and study population against the marketed product and the advertised benefit, and to evaluate whether extrapolation is appropriate when research conditions differ from real-life use ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)). A retrieval tool scoring documents by semantic similarity will happily return a study on a different compound, a different delivery form or a different population, because the language is close. Closeness is not relevance.
The evidence is the wrong kind. The same guidance treats anecdotal reports, consumer satisfaction surveys and a practitioner's observations about patients as insufficient to substantiate health product claims, and treats animal and in vitro work as supporting background rather than proof without human clinical confirmation ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)). Those documents still belong in the repository, labeled for what they are. They simply cannot carry an approval.
Hedging does not rescue an overreach. FTC guidance warns that vague qualifiers such as "may" or "helps," and framing like "promising" or "preliminary," are unlikely to convey the real limits of the science, and that a disclaimer cannot contradict the claim it sits next to ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)). Teams that rely on softened verbs as a compliance control are usually substituting tone for substantiation.
So approval is modeled as its own event with its own record: reviewer identity, decision, date, scope of permitted use, and the specific evidence relied on. Control ingestion and access to permitted documents only
Ingestion is a policy decision disguised as a technical one. Everything you load becomes something the system can paraphrase with confidence, so the inventory of authorized documents is the real perimeter. Start with a written list of what may enter, who may query it, and who may change status.
An illustrative authorized document inventory for a peptide approved content repository:
* Federal regulatory status pages. Ingest: Yes, with retrieval date. Access tier: All drafters, read. Refresh trigger: Source page change.
* Advertising platform policy pages. Ingest: Yes, with retrieval date. Access tier: All drafters, read. Refresh trigger: Source page change.
* Licensed or lawfully stored study copies. Ingest: Yes, with rights note. Access tier: Reviewers and senior editors. Refresh trigger: New publication in same area.
* Internal legal-reviewed boilerplate. Ingest: Yes. Access tier: All drafters, read. Refresh trigger: Legal re-review cycle.
* Approved product descriptions. Ingest: Yes. Access tier: All drafters, read. Refresh trigger: Formulation or label change.
* Previously published approved copy. Ingest: Yes, with status flag. Access tier: All drafters, read. Refresh trigger: Linked claim retired.
* Pharmacy or supplier attestations. Ingest: Metadata only. Access tier: Reviewers. Refresh trigger: Contract or supplier change.
* Patient records, intake notes, CRM exports. Ingest: No. Access tier: Not applicable. Refresh trigger: Not applicable.
* Practitioner anecdotes and testimonials. Ingest: Reference only, never as substantiation. Access tier: Reviewers. Refresh trigger: Not applicable.
* Competitor website copy. Ingest: No. Access tier: Not applicable. Refresh trigger: Not applicable.
Two exclusions deserve emphasis. Patient and prospect data has no business in a claims repository; the system needs evidence, not people. And competitor copy is the most common quiet contaminant, because once it is indexed the model learns the category's most aggressive phrasing and reproduces it as house style. Liability does not transfer to whoever said it first. FTC guidance notes that all parties who participate directly in marketing, or who control those practices, are responsible for presenting claims truthfully and checking the adequacy of support, and that its view of advertising extends to websites, social media, influencer marketing and press materials ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)).
Access should be tiered the way an editorial desk already works: drafters query and propose, editors assemble, only designated reviewers move a record to approved. Query logs should be retained so an auditor can reconstruct which records informed a given draft. Design grounded draft assistance that shows its work
Picture the actual desk. A content strategist is writing a clinician-facing page and needs a line about what a compound has been studied for. In an ungoverned setup, the strategist asks a chat tool and receives four fluent sentences with no provenance, then spends an hour reverse-engineering whether any of it is true. In a private AI claims review setup, the draft comes back differently: each substantive sentence carries the claim_id it was built from, the permitted context for that record, and a visible note where no approved record exists.
That third element is the one teams skip. The system must have a defined behavior for absence. When a writer requests a benefit statement with no approved claim behind it, the correct output is a gap notice naming what would be needed, not a confident sentence. Smoothing over a gap is the single most costly default in regulated drafting, because the resulting copy reads better than the copy that is actually supportable.
Practical controls that make this real:
* No unsourced substantive sentences. Claim-bearing language either resolves to an approved record or is rendered as a flagged gap.
* Verbatim mode for cleared wording. Approved claim text is inserted exactly, not regenerated, so the model cannot improve it into something stronger.
* Context checking at insert time. If the record permits clinician-facing use and the draft is a consumer landing page, the insert fails and tells the writer why.
* Implied claim review. FTC guidance evaluates the net impression of an ad as a whole, including product names, images, charts and layout, and holds advertisers responsible for reasonably implied claims ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)). A sentence-level citation check cannot see a page-level impression, so a human reads the assembled asset.
* Uncertainty preserved, not polished. Limitations recorded on the evidence object should travel into the draft rather than being dropped for readability.
Retrieval grounding reduces the frequency of invented detail. It does not remove the possibility, and no responsible workflow should be sold as though it does. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework is explicitly intended for voluntary use and is accompanied by a Generative AI Profile, released in July 2024, which helps organizations identify risks specific to generative systems and proposes management actions ([NIST](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework)). That framing is the right one: documented practices and oversight, not elimination of risk. Test for unsafe expansion with adversarial, permissioned cases
A regulated marketing retrieval workflow needs an evaluation suite in the same way a payment system needs tests. Build the cases from synthetic records, run them on a schedule, and record pass and fail per category rather than publishing a single score. Four categories matter most.
Invented benefit. Ask for a benefit no approved record supports and confirm the system returns a gap notice. Vary the phrasing, including flattering framings and questions that assume the benefit already exists.
Stale rule. Change a source record's status, then ask a question whose old answer is now wrong. The system should surface the newer record and mark the superseded one. This is where regulatory status is most dangerous, because status language is sticky. FDA's interim policy for compounding under section 503A sorts nominated bulk drug substances into three categories, and for category 2 substances the agency has identified significant safety risks and would consider taking action against a compounder under its general enforcement policies ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/bulk-drug-substances-used-compounding-under-section-503a-fdc-act)). Category placement can change, so any record repeating a status needs a retrieval date and a re-check trigger.
Category confusion. Test whether the system conflates distinct ideas: enforcement discretion is not approval, approval of a drug product is not permission to advertise a given claim, a certification is not channel eligibility, and channel eligibility is not account standing. FDA's own description of category 1 is that the agency does not intend to take action provided the conditions in the guidance are met ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/bulk-drug-substances-used-compounding-under-section-503a-fdc-act)), which is a statement about enforcement posture rather than a marketing permission. Separately, Google's healthcare and medicines policy does not support promotion of speculative or experimental medical treatments ([Google Ads](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031)), which is a platform rule that operates independently of any federal status. Teams working through adjacent certification questions can start with the guide to [LegitScript certification](/resources/legitscript-certification).
Cross-product and cross-population generalization. Feed a record approved for one formulation, route or audience and request copy for another. The expected behavior is refusal with an explanation, consistent with the relevance principles in FTC guidance ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)).
Report results as observed behavior on a defined test set, dated. A pass rate on your own synthetic cases is evidence that the controls function, not a certification, and it should never be presented to a client or a regulator as one. Note also that FTC staff guidance describes itself as business guidance that does not carry the force of law and does not create a safe harbor ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)), which is another way of saying your internal test suite is a management tool, not a defense.
Operate review and retirement as a standing editorial function
The workflow is unglamorous and that is the point. A weekly review block, a queue, and three people with distinct roles: the editorial owner who proposes and maintains records, the reviewer with regulatory or clinical qualification who approves, and the publisher who ships only approved-status records. Approvals are scoped. A claim cleared for a clinician-facing resource is not automatically cleared for a paid search landing page, and the record should say so in its permitted context field rather than relying on memory.
Retirement is the half that teams build last and need most. When a source changes, the linked claims move to suspended, not deleted, and every live asset referencing them appears in a dependency list. Someone owns closing that list. Deleting a record destroys the only artifact that can answer the question that arrives months later: what did we know, and who signed off, on the day that page went live. Version history makes a published sentence traceable to the evidence and the approval that existed at publication time.
Triggers worth encoding: a monitored source page changes, a product formulation or supplier changes, a platform policy updates, a reviewer leaves the organization, or a fixed re-review interval elapses. Pair that with a quarterly audit that samples published pages and walks each claim back to its record. Audit the output, not the intention.

FAQ
Q: Can a knowledge base approve a clinical claim?
A: No. It can retrieve evidence, show context, flag limitations and record decisions. Approval is a judgment that a specific wording is adequately supported for a specific use, and FTC guidance places that substantiation obligation on the advertiser before an ad runs, with health claims generally requiring competent and reliable scientific evidence ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)). The guidance also treats implied claims as the advertiser's responsibility, assessed by the net impression of the whole asset, which is a holistic reading a retrieval score cannot perform. Build the system so that approval requires a qualified human identity, and so that no automated path can set the approved status.
Q: What fields should each evidence record contain?
A: At minimum: source type, publisher, publication date, retrieval date, a stable location for the stored copy, storage and quotation rights, a plain-language statement of what the source establishes, and an explicit limitations field covering population, formulation, route, duration and outcome measures. FTC guidance directs advertisers to compare those parameters against the marketed product and the advertised benefit before relying on a study ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)), so the limitations field is load-bearing rather than decorative. Add a totality note recording contrary or inconsistent evidence in the same area, because the guidance also warns against relying on favorable studies while discounting research that does not support the effect.
Q: How should source changes affect approved copy?
A: Automatically, and conservatively. A monitored source change should suspend every linked claim, generate a dependency list of live assets, and route the records back to the reviewer who approved them. Suspension is not a quiet downgrade; it should block new insertions while the existing pages are assessed. This matters acutely for regulatory status language, since FDA's interim policy categories for compounding bulk substances can be updated, and category 2 placement reflects identified safety risks rather than a settled list ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/bulk-drug-substances-used-compounding-under-section-503a-fdc-act)). Store the retrieval date, re-check on a defined interval, and verify again immediately before publishing anything that repeats a status.
Q: Can patient records be added without a separate review?
A: They should not be added at all. A claims repository needs evidence about products and sources, not information about individuals, and identifiable health information introduces obligations that have nothing to do with drafting speed. Practitioner observations and customer experiences are a separate matter: they may be stored as reference material clearly labeled as non-substantiating, because FTC guidance treats anecdotal consumer experience and practitioner observation as insufficient to substantiate health product claims ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)). Testimonials used in advertising still require underlying scientific support for the implied claim that the product will work as described.
Q: How should the system be tested?
A: With adversarial, synthetic cases run on a schedule and scored by category: invented benefit, stale rule, status or category confusion, and cross-product or cross-population generalization. Record results with dates and keep failures visible rather than averaging them away. NIST's framework is voluntary and its generative profile is oriented toward identifying risks and proposing management actions, with the framework itself noted as under revision ([NIST](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework)), which is a reasonable model for internal practice: document what you test, what failed, and what changed as a result. Testing demonstrates that controls operate. It does not substitute for qualified legal, clinical or regulatory review of the published asset.
