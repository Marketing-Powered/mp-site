Clinical Review and Evidence Standards for Peptide Content
Most peptide content programs do not break down over writing quality. They break down because no one decided in advance who is qualified to rule on a clinical statement, which turns peptide content review into a cleanup operation instead of a control. This guide covers how to assign those judgments, document the evidence behind each claim, and keep evidence limits intact as pages get revised.

Separate education, service explanation and clinical claims
Three different kinds of sentences usually live on the same peptide page, and they carry three different evidentiary burdens.
The first is business or category education: what a compounding pharmacy is, how a telehealth intake works, what a chain of custody looks like, how a research supplier differs from a prescriber. The second is service explanation: what your business does, who is eligible to work with you, what a consultation includes, what happens after an order. The third is a clinical or efficacy claim: a statement about what a substance does inside the human body, who it helps, or how safe it is.
Teams conflate the three because the page reads as one voice. A regulator does not read it that way. The FTC evaluates the whole advertisement and the claims a reasonable consumer takes from it, and advertisers are responsible for implied claims as well as express ones, including those created by product names, images, charts and graphs. The agency's guidance instructs marketers to assess the net impression conveyed by all elements of an ad rather than focusing narrowly on individual phrases, and when an ad supports more than one reasonable interpretation, each interpretation has to be substantiated ([FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)).
Here is an illustrative example, written for this guide and not drawn from any client page. A service-explanation sentence reads: "Our clinicians review your labs and build a protocol around your goals." A later edit adds eight words: "Our clinicians review your labs and build a protocol around your goals, so patients see results faster than with diet alone." The first sentence describes a process. The second is a comparative efficacy claim that now requires substantiation of a specific outcome against a specific alternative. Nothing in the editing workflow flagged the change, because the sentence was already approved and the writer treated the addition as a style improvement.
The practical fix is to tag content at the sentence or block level before review, not after. Education and service blocks route to marketing and legal. Clinical-claim blocks route to a qualified clinical reviewer and do not publish without that review. The tagging is also what makes later audits fast, because you can pull every clinical-claim block across the site without rereading the site.
Assign each decision to the reviewer qualified to make it
Review responsibility in most peptide and wellness programs is informal. Someone asks the medical director in a hallway, or assumes that legal will catch anything serious. Informal review produces no record of who decided what, which is the asset you actually need when a claim is challenged.
A responsibility matrix fixes that by naming the decision type, the role that owns it, and the escalation path. The structure below is an illustrative model, not a legal standard, and role names should be mapped to your actual organization.
* Audience, positioning, structure, keyword intent. Owner: Marketing lead. Authority: Approve and publish. Escalates to: Executive sponsor.
* Clinical accuracy, mechanism statements, safety framing. Owner: Qualified clinical reviewer (licensed, relevant scope). Authority: Approve, amend or block. Escalates to: Medical director.
* Product status, intended use, regulatory exposure. Owner: Regulatory or legal counsel. Authority: Approve, amend or block. Escalates to: Counsel of record.
* Channel eligibility and ad policy fit. Owner: Paid media owner. Authority: Approve for a specific channel only. Escalates to: Legal if the fix changes a claim.
* Final publication. Owner: Named editor. Authority: Publish only with the three approvals above recorded. Escalates to: Executive sponsor.
Two boundaries matter more than the table. First, a marketing agency, including Marketing Powered, does not provide medical or legal sign-off. We build and operate the editorial workflow, prepare the evidence record, and route clinical and regulatory questions to the qualified reviewers your business designates. Asserting clinical conclusions is their job, and the workflow should make it impossible for a writer to bypass them.
Second, agency involvement does not insulate anyone. FTC guidance states that all parties who participate directly in marketing and promotion, or who have authority to control those practices, are obligated to make sure claims are truthful and to check the adequacy of the support, and the Commission has taken action against ad agencies, distributors, retailers and expert endorsers as well as product marketers ([FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)). That is a reason for an agency to insist on documented clinical review rather than treat it as client overhead.
Platform eligibility is a fourth decision, and it is separate from the first three. A claim can be lawful, substantiated and still ineligible for a given ad channel, and a channel can accept an ad without that acceptance saying anything about the claim's legal standing. Keep those judgments in different columns. Build an evidence hierarchy before the claim is written
The common failure is backwards sequencing. A draft is written, then someone searches for a citation that appears to support it. That produces claims attached to sources that do not actually test the claim.
Work the other way. Identify the evidence, judge what it supports, and write to that ceiling. The FTC's substantiation standard for health-related claims is competent and reliable scientific evidence, defined as tests, analyses, research or studies conducted and evaluated in an objective manner by experts in the relevant disease, condition or function, and generally accepted in the profession to yield accurate and reliable results. As a general matter, substantiation of health-related benefits needs to take the form of randomized, controlled human clinical testing ([FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)).
Several points in that guidance do real work in peptide content, where much of the available literature is preclinical:
* Animal and in vitro studies may provide useful background but are not sufficient to substantiate health-related claims without confirmation by human trials.
* Epidemiological studies can show association but do not prove a causal link.
* Anecdotal evidence, including consumer surveys and a practitioner's observations about patients, is never sufficient to substantiate claims about a health product's effects.
* Public health recommendations and advisories from medical organizations are not substantiation on their own.
* Relevance is a separate test from validity. Dosage, formulation, route of administration, outcome measures and study population all have to match the product and the audience you are marketing to.
Record those judgments where the writer can see them. A claim-evidence worksheet keeps one row per claim, not one row per article.
* Claim text. What it captures: The exact published sentence, not a paraphrase.
* Claim type. What it captures: Education, service explanation, structure or function, safety, efficacy.
* Source. What it captures: Citation and stable URL.
* Source type. What it captures: Human RCT, observational, animal, in vitro, review, regulatory document.
* Limitation. What it captures: Population, dose, duration, endpoint or formulation mismatch.
* Supported ceiling. What it captures: The strongest wording the evidence actually supports.
* Reviewer and date. What it captures: Named clinical or legal reviewer, with the review date.
* Re-review trigger. What it captures: Source change, policy change, offer change, or a set interval.
Note the "supported ceiling" field. It is the one that survives editing, because it tells a future writer how far the sentence can move before it needs new review. Vague hedging does not substitute for it. The FTC's guidance is explicit that qualifiers such as "may," "helps," "promising," "preliminary" and "pilot" are generally inadequate to convey the limits of weak science, and that consumers tend to read them as positive attributes. Review claims in context, not sentence by sentence
A page can pass a sentence-level review and still fail as a whole. The most concrete recent illustration in this category is the FDA warning letter issued to Gram Peptides on March 31, 2026, in which the agency reported reviewing the firm's website from January to March 2026 and concluded that products offered there were unapproved new drugs ([FDA Warning Letter, Gram Peptides](https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/warning-letters/gram-peptides-721806-03312026)).
Three details in that letter should change how review is scoped.
The first is that research-only labeling did not control the outcome. FDA stated that despite labeling marketing the products for "Research Use Only" and as not intended for human consumption, medical use or veterinary use, evidence obtained from the website established that the products were intended to be drugs for human use.
The second is where the evidence came from. The letter cites marketing copy on individual product pages, including statements about appetite suppression, insulin sensitivity and fat oxidation, and a bulleted "benefits" list covering metabolic regulation, weight management and improved lipid profiles. That is ordinary product-page body copy and subheads, the kind of content a marketing team writes and publishes without clinical review.
The third is adjacency. FDA treated the sale of bacteriostatic water for injection alongside peptide products requiring reconstitution as demonstrating intended combined use, which made that water a drug as well. Merchandising decisions, not just sentences, carried regulatory meaning.
Scope review to every surface that contributes to net impression: the H1 and subheads, meta title and description, image alt text and on-image text, product names, bullet lists, FAQ answers, schema, internal link anchor text, related-product modules, testimonials and the call to action. The FTC guidance reinforces this across several specific points. Disclaimers cannot contradict a claim; a DSHEA-style disclaimer does not cure an otherwise deceptive ad; disclosures made through hyperlinks are avoidable and therefore weak in interactive media; and mischaracterizing an FDA action, such as presenting enforcement discretion or a clearance as approval of efficacy, is itself deceptive.
Build the surface list into a repeatable pass rather than leaving it to reviewer memory. Our [healthcare marketing compliance checklist](/resources/healthcare-marketing-compliance-checklist) covers the general version of that pass, and the peptide version adds product-page copy, adjacency and research-use framing as named review items.
Control versions and expiration dates for every claim
Claims do not stay reviewed. The underlying source gets retracted or superseded, the regulatory picture shifts, a platform rewrites a policy, the offer changes, or a writer updates the page for freshness and moves a sentence past its supported ceiling.
Treat approval as dated rather than permanent. A version and expiration log sits next to the claim-evidence worksheet and answers one question: is this claim still standing on the thing that justified it?
* Page and claim ID. What it captures: Links the log entry to a specific worksheet row.
* Approved wording and date. What it captures: The text as approved, and by whom.
* Review interval. What it captures: A set cadence appropriate to the claim's risk.
* Source check date. What it captures: Last confirmation that the cited source is live and unchanged.
* Policy check date. What it captures: Last confirmation of advertising and channel rules relied on.
* Change event. What it captures: Edit, offer change, source change, policy change, enforcement development.
* Status. What it captures: Current, in re-review, amended, withdrawn.
Four triggers should force re-review regardless of the calendar. A claim's cited source changes, is withdrawn, or becomes unreachable. The offer or product changes, including price, eligibility, bundling or what ships with an order. An advertising or certification policy you rely on is revised. Or a relevant enforcement development lands, such as a warning letter addressing a comparable product or a comparable marketing pattern.
The regulatory documents themselves are living pages. FTC health-product guidance is published as staff business guidance that interprets advertising law and expressly does not carry the force of law or provide a safe harbor, so what is defensible also turns on the facts of a given case ([FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)). Warning letters are dated enforcement records that keep accumulating. Any program that cites either should recheck the sources on a fixed cadence and immediately before publishing an update.
Certification credentials run on their own clocks and belong in the same log, with renewal dates and monitoring obligations recorded rather than remembered. Measure editorial reliability, not publication volume
Content programs are usually graded on output. For regulated claims, output is the wrong denominator, because volume produced without review is a liability that compounds.
Track reliability instead:
* Correction rate: published claims later amended or withdrawn, as a share of claims published in the period.
* Claim coverage: the share of live clinical claims that have a completed worksheet row with a named reviewer.
* Source currency: the share of claims whose cited source was verified within the defined interval.
* Review turnaround: median hours or days from submission to clinical and legal decision, measured separately for each.
* Re-review backlog age: the oldest claim sitting past its expiration date, which is usually the most honest number in the set.
* Trigger response time: elapsed time from a policy or source change to completed re-review of affected pages.
These are process metrics. They describe how reliably the workflow runs, and they do not demonstrate marketing return. Keep them in a separate report from acquisition performance so no one reads a falling correction rate as evidence of revenue. Commercial measurement is a different discipline, covered in our approach to [healthcare paid media and attribution](/services/paid-media), where the useful chain runs from spend through qualified inquiry to a confirmed outcome rather than stopping at form fills.
One caution on baselines. Search volume, difficulty, cost per click and first-party conversion baselines for a new peptide content program are unavailable at launch, not zero. Set the reliability metrics first, gather acquisition baselines after launch, and avoid comparing either against numbers borrowed from a different category.
How this workflow fits an acquisition program
The reason to build governance before scale is economic. Two operating models make the difference visible.
In the first, content and media scale together. Pages ship on a calendar, campaigns point at them, and clinical review happens when something looks risky. The program's cost base grows while its claim inventory grows unreviewed. When a claim has to be pulled, the landing page, the ad copy derived from it, the internal links pointing at it and the conversion path all move at once, and the remediation bill lands on top of media that has already been spent.
In the second, the claim inventory is reviewed and logged first, and spend is gated to the pages that are substantiated and channel-eligible. Fewer pages carry traffic at the start. The pages that do are the ones you can defend, and the ones you can edit later without reopening every question. The trade-off is real: the second model launches slower and publishes less in the first quarter.

Questions from content and clinical teams
### Who should review clinical statements?
A licensed clinician whose scope of practice actually covers the subject matter, named in the workflow with the authority to block publication. "Someone with a clinical background" is not a role. The reviewer should see the claim in its published context, including headline, images and call to action, not a stripped text file, because net impression is assessed across all elements of an advertisement ([FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)). Regulatory status and intended-use questions are a separate judgment and belong with counsel, even when a clinician is comfortable with the science.
### Can an agency author provide medical approval?
No. An agency can draft, assemble the evidence record, identify where a sentence exceeds its support, and route the decision, but clinical and legal approval have to come from qualified reviewers your business designates. That division does not shift liability away from the agency either. FTC guidance states that everyone who participates directly in marketing and promotion, or who controls those practices, must check the adequacy of the support for claims, and lists ad agencies among the parties the Commission has acted against.
### Do metadata and images need review?
Yes. Implied claims can be created by images, product names, charts and graphs as readily as by body text, and the FTC's examples include imagery that conveys clinical proof without stating it. Review meta titles and descriptions, image alt text, on-image text, schema fields and link anchor text. Adjacency matters too. In the Gram Peptides letter, FDA treated bacteriostatic water offered for sale alongside peptide products requiring reconstitution as evidence that the water was intended for use in injection, which made it a drug ([FDA Warning Letter, Gram Peptides](https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/warning-letters/gram-peptides-721806-03312026)). Merchandising modules are review surfaces.
### How should conflicting evidence be handled?
Assess the totality, not the convenient subset. FTC guidance directs advertisers to consider all relevant well-conducted research rather than relying on the studies that support an effect while discounting those that do not, and warns that if a stronger body of surrounding evidence runs contrary to a claimed effect, even a qualified claim is likely to be deceptive. Where results conflict, document whether there is a sound methodological explanation, such as a difference in dose, administration route or population. If there is not, lower the supported ceiling or drop the claim. Record the reasoning in the worksheet so the next writer does not relitigate it.
### When should an article be revised?
On a trigger or on a cadence, whichever comes first. Revise when a cited source changes or disappears, when the offer or product changes, when an advertising or certification policy you depend on is updated, or when an enforcement development addresses a comparable product or marketing pattern. Also revise when an internal edit moves a sentence past its recorded supported ceiling, which is the most common and least noticed trigger. Every revision that touches a clinical claim re-enters review. Treat the previously approved version as evidence of what was approved, not as permission for what replaced it.
