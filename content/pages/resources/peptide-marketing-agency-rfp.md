How to Run a Peptide Marketing Agency RFP and Due Diligence
A pitch deck can be rewritten in an afternoon. A signed account-ownership clause, a redacted campaign artifact and a correct answer to a policy scenario cannot, which is why a peptide marketing agency RFP should score documents before it ever scores enthusiasm. This page gives you the request list, the scenario questions, a weighted scorecard and a worked comparison you can run against every vendor on your shortlist.

Define the business and the eligible scope before you ask
Most weak proposals are the buyer's fault. If three vendors receive three different descriptions of your business, you get three incomparable proposals and no way to tell competence from confidence. Write one accurate business description, send it to every vendor, and require that proposals respond to it directly.
The description should state, in plain language:
* Legal entity and ownership. Operating entity, any affiliated pharmacy, clinic or telehealth entity, and who controls each.
* Product lines exactly as labeled today. Research-use-only chemicals, compounded preparations dispensed through a partner pharmacy, dietary supplements, devices, or consultations that may result in a prescription. Do not describe what you intend to sell next quarter in the same breath as what ships today.
* Named substances you sell, reference or plan to reference in content. This single list changes what a competent vendor will agree to do.
* Current certification status. Certified, in application, denied, never applied.
* Current advertising account status. Active, limited, disapproved ads, suspended, appealed once, appealed twice.
* Root domains in scope. Each one, including storefronts and content subdomains, because certification scope and platform review both attach to domains.
* Markets and jurisdictions you actually ship or treat in.
* Payments status. Current processor, any prior merchant account termination.
Then separate four things that buyers routinely merge into one question: whether the business operates lawfully, what the product's regulatory status is, whether the advertising channel is eligible, and whether your account is currently in good standing. A business can be lawful and still be ineligible to advertise. A product can be widely sold and still be impermissible. An account can be reinstated and still have nothing it is permitted to promote.
Scope matters at the product level, not the industry level. LegitScript's Healthcare Certification covers activities such as selling or advertising prescription medications online, telemedicine that may result in a prescription, and card-not-present payments for prescription drugs, while [research-use-only chemical businesses, including peptides, are listed as generally not eligible](https://www.legitscript.com/certification/healthcare-certification/faq/). Certification fees are assessed per root domain, with an application fee of $975 and an annual certification fee of $2,150 due only on approval, [per the same FAQ](https://www.legitscript.com/certification/healthcare-certification/faq/). If a vendor's proposal assumes certification without asking which of those activities you perform, that is your first data point.
Be honest about the unresolved items as well. "We are not certified," "we have an open appeal," and "our packaging says research use only while our site describes benefits" are the three disclosures that most change a credible scope of work. Hiding them produces a proposal priced for a business you do not have.
Require evidence of delivery, not a specialization claim
"We specialize in peptides" is a sentence, not a credential. Treat it as the beginning of due diligence rather than the end of it, and ask for artifacts a vendor can produce before contract signature.
Ask for these, with client identities redacted:
* A campaign structure export or screenshot from a regulated account, showing naming conventions, match types, negative lists and conversion actions. You are testing whether structure reflects policy risk, not whether the account looks busy.
* A written policy review they produced for a client. A landing page claim review, a disapproval diagnosis, or a pre-launch checklist. The document tells you whether compliance is a workflow or a sales talking point.
* An appeal or remediation narrative they drafted, redacted. Read it for accuracy and tone. Appeals that read like legal threats or like boilerplate are both bad signs.
* A measurement specification. Conversion definitions, offline import method, CRM fields, call tracking configuration and how they handle sensitive-category constraints on audience features.
* Two references who will discuss process, not outcome claims. Ask the references what the vendor refused to do.
* Named staffing. Who builds, who writes, who reviews claims, and how many hours per month each person contributes. Pitch teams and delivery teams are frequently different people.
Reject "trust our experience" as a substitute for any of the above. Also reject pure outcome claims that cannot be audited. If a vendor offers a number, ask for the account context that produced it: spend level, date range, conversion definition and who owned the account. A claim that survives those four questions is worth something; a claim that evaporates is a disqualifier for every other claim in the proposal.
Verifiable specificity is the standard you are applying to yourself as well as to vendors. For context on what testable operator background looks like, Marketing Powered's founder is a former healthcare CMO who ran marketing for a treatment network that grew from 3 to 24 locations, managed $1.5 to $2M per month in Google Ads across 11+ behavioral and mental health accounts, and serves as a court-certified expert witness in advertising strategy. Those are claims with a shape you can interrogate: spend band, account count, vertical, role. Ask every vendor for claims in that shape, then test them. Our broader view of vetting criteria sits in the [healthcare marketing agency guide](/services/healthcare-marketing-agency).
One more evidence item is easy to forget. Ask what the vendor will not do, in writing, and whether that list appears in the contract. A vendor with no refusal list has not encountered enforcement yet, or has, and resolved it in ways you should ask about.
Test policy judgment with scenarios that have wrong answers
Capability questions invite rehearsed answers. Scenario questions do not. Send four short scenarios with the RFP and require written responses of 150 words or fewer each. Score the reasoning, not the confidence.
Scenario one. "Our search ads were disapproved for promoting an unapproved pharmaceutical. What do you change in the first week?" A competent answer addresses the destination and the claims on it, then resubmits through the same account. A disqualifying answer proposes a second account, a new domain running the same offer, or ad variations designed to slip past review. The [Google Ads circumventing systems policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15938075?hl=en) names bypassing enforcement by creating variations of ads, domains or content that were disapproved, and lists creating new websites or accounts to show ads similar to ones already disapproved as multiple account abuse.
Scenario two. "Our BPC-157 education article ranks well. Marketing wants to add a benefits section and a 'talk to our team' button. What do you advise?" A competent answer separates informational content from promotional content and flags efficacy framing. LegitScript's guidance describes promotional signals including efficacy or safety claims, dosing recommendations, describing specific treatment benefits, calling a substance clinically proven without clear disclosure, and [including a call to action inviting readers to ask about obtaining the product unless the content clearly discloses that it is not approved and not available](https://www.legitscript.com/healthcare/the-peptide-boom-why-so-many-popular-products-are-still-off-limits/).
Scenario three. "An advisory committee recommended several peptides for a bulk drug substances list. Can we start advertising them?" The correct answer is that a recommendation is not authorization. LegitScript's analysis states that [an advisory committee recommendation does not authorize compounding and does not change the law](https://www.legitscript.com/healthcare/the-peptide-boom-why-so-many-popular-products-are-still-off-limits/), and that formal rulemaking with a proposed rule and comment period would normally precede any final rule. A vendor who treats enforcement discretion or a committee vote as advertising permission will spend your budget on an account that does not survive review.
Scenario four. "If we obtain certification, are our ads approved?" Certification and channel eligibility are different gates. LegitScript states that certification provides eligibility to advertise on participating platforms subject to each platform's advertising policies, and that [certification may be required for certain advertising programs but does not by itself secure advertising approval](https://www.legitscript.com/certification/healthcare-certification/faq/). A vendor who collapses those two gates into one will also collapse your timeline expectations.
Score answers on three dimensions: whether they cite the governing rule, whether they distinguish product status from channel status, and whether they say "I do not know, here is how I would confirm" when that is the honest answer. The last one is undervalued by most buyers and is the strongest single predictor of a vendor who will not surprise you. If you want the general-market version of these tests, the [healthcare PPC agency selection guide](/resources/choose-healthcare-ppc-agency) covers the non-peptide baseline.
Inspect recovery proposals for evasion language
This is the section where money gets lost fastest. If your account is suspended, you are shopping under pressure, and the vendors who respond fastest are often the ones selling the tactics that caused the suspension in the first place.
Red-flag language to search for in every recovery proposal:
* "Backup," "aged," "warmed" or "replacement" accounts
* Running the offer under a different business name, a different entity or a nominee
* A "verification workaround," or an offer to supply documents on your behalf
* A landing page served to reviewers that differs from the page users reach
* A reinstatement timeline stated as a commitment, or pricing contingent on reinstatement
* Reassurance that enforcement is random and a fresh account resolves it
Each of these maps to a named prohibition. The circumventing systems policy prohibits cloaking, which it defines as showing different content to different people or to Google to hide things that might break the rules, prohibits creating new accounts after a suspension decision to re-enter the system, and prohibits submitting false information as part of verification programs, with the policy stating that [violations are considered egregious and that suspension occurs upon detection without prior warning](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15938075?hl=en). The same page states that [accounts are only reinstated in compelling circumstances](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15938075?hl=en), which is exactly why no vendor can commit to an outcome.
A defensible recovery proposal looks different. It starts with diagnosis: which policy, which asset, which claim. It separates the business-model problem from the asset problem, because a site that promotes an impermissible substance will be re-flagged no matter how clean the account is. It proposes remediation you can see: claims removed or rewritten, disclosures added, entity information corrected, verification completed with accurate and unmodified documents. It then proposes a single, honest appeal, and it tells you plainly that the decision belongs to the platform. Google's own best practices for multiple account situations include resolving existing suspensions through the appeal process and [contacting the agency that manages your account about the suspension](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15938075?hl=en), which is worth remembering if a previous vendor touched accounts you do not control.
Two contract terms protect you here. First, a warranty that the vendor will not create accounts, domains or identities to circumvent enforcement, with termination for cause attached. Second, disclosure of every account, manager account and domain the vendor will associate with your business. Pair the RFP with the [healthcare marketing compliance checklist](/resources/healthcare-marketing-compliance-checklist) so your internal reviewer is reading proposals against a fixed list rather than against the vendor's framing.
Compare governance, ownership and exit rights
Ownership questions feel administrative during selection and become existential at termination. Resolve them in the RFP, in writing, before price is discussed.
Assets the buyer should own outright: the Google Ads account and any Microsoft Ads account, the analytics property, the tag manager container, the CRM instance, the call tracking account, every domain and the DNS, the ad creative source files, the landing page source and hosting, and the conversion configuration documentation. Vendor manager accounts link to your accounts; they do not contain them.
Access terms to specify: your team retains admin on every asset throughout the engagement, the vendor's users are named individuals rather than shared logins, and any third-party tool that holds your data is disclosed as a subprocessor with its storage location named.
Approval workflow to require: who in your organization signs off on product claims, who signs off on landing pages, and what the turnaround is. Require a change log for claim-bearing pages. This is not bureaucracy for its own sake. LegitScript asks certified businesses to report significant changes including ownership, business operations, partnerships, major website updates, new services and expansion into new jurisdictions, [according to the Healthcare Certification FAQ](https://www.legitscript.com/certification/healthcare-certification/faq/). If your agency can publish a new service page without telling anyone, your certification record and your website can drift apart quietly. Background on program scope and process is in our [LegitScript certification guide](/resources/legitscript-certification).
Exit rights to put in the contract: a defined transfer window, return of all documentation and creative source files, removal of vendor admin access on a stated date, and continued access to historical campaign and conversion data. Add a clause stating that no account associated with your business will be created outside your ownership.
Data handling to confirm: whether any protected health information touches the vendor's systems, whether a business associate agreement is required and signed, which tools process customer data, and where processing happens. Marketing Powered is HIPAA-aware and LegitScript-aware, builds for Google's sensitive healthcare vertical where retargeting is not permitted, and runs owned, local AI infrastructure for HIPAA-aware inference. Our handling posture is documented in the [security overview](/company/security). Hold every vendor to the same level of specificity, and treat vague answers about data location as unanswered.
Score proposals consistently with visible assumptions
A rubric written after the pitches is a rationalization. Write it before, publish the weights to your internal reviewers, and score independently before anyone discusses.
The weighting below is an illustrative starting point. Adjust it to your situation: a suspended account raises policy judgment, a planned acquisition raises governance, a mature compliant catalog raises commercial fit.
* Evidence of delivery. Illustrative weight: 30%. What a 3 looks like: Redacted artifacts, named staffing, references who discuss process. What a 0 looks like: Logo wall, outcome claims with no account context.
* Policy judgment. Illustrative weight: 30%. What a 3 looks like: Scenario answers cite the governing rule and separate product status from channel status. What a 0 looks like: Confident answers with no rule cited, or evasion proposed.
* Governance and ownership. Illustrative weight: 25%. What a 3 looks like: Buyer owns all assets, named approval workflow, written exit terms. What a 0 looks like: Vendor-held accounts, no change log, silence on exit.
* Commercial fit and cost. Illustrative weight: 15%. What a 3 looks like: Scope matched to the business described, assumptions stated. What a 0 looks like: Fixed package priced before scope questions.
Score each criterion 0 to 3. A 3 means documented and verified, 2 means documented, 1 means asserted, 0 means absent or contradicted. Multiply by weight and total.
Apply disqualifiers outside the arithmetic. Any proposal that recommends replacement accounts, concealed identity, reviewer-specific content or falsified verification leaves the process regardless of score. Any proposal that states reinstatement or certification as a committed outcome leaves as well, because the authority to grant either belongs to the platform and the certifying body, not the vendor.
Finally, keep an assumptions column in the scorecard. When a vendor does not answer, record what you assumed and who made that call. Six weeks later, when performance questions start, the assumptions column is what tells you whether a shortfall came from the vendor's work or from a gap you accepted during selection. The same discipline applies after signing: define what you will measure, over what window, and against which conversion definition. Compare three answers to the same suspension question
Here is what scoring looks like in practice. The three responses below are illustrative composites written for this guide, not quotations from real vendors. The question asked: "Our Google Ads account was suspended for circumventing systems. What are your first 30 days?"
Response A. Promises the account back within two weeks, prices half the fee on reinstatement, and mentions that a parallel account can run in the meantime to keep volume up. Policy judgment scores 0 and the proposal is disqualified, because creating new accounts after a suspension decision to re-enter the system is prohibited by the [circumventing systems policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15938075?hl=en). The commercial terms are attractive precisely because the risk sits entirely with you.
Response B. Offers a general compliance audit, says it will "work with Google," provides no artifacts and asks for account access before scope is fixed. Evidence scores 1, policy judgment scores 1, governance is unaddressed. Not dishonest, not qualified. This is the response that consumes a quarter.
Response C. Asks for the disapproval notices and the suspension email before proposing anything. States that it will inventory claim-bearing pages against the product's current regulatory status, correct entity and verification information with accurate documents, remove or rewrite promotional claims, then file one honest appeal and stop. Says plainly that the decision belongs to the platform and that paid search may need to stay dark while organic, email, partnerships and direct channels carry demand. Attaches a redacted remediation document from another regulated account and names the two people doing the work. Evidence 3, policy judgment 3, governance 2 pending contract language.
Response C is the only one that priced the actual problem. Note what made it visible: a fixed question, written answers and a rubric applied before anyone spoke.
Questions buyers ask before they sign
### What proves peptide-industry expertise?
Documents and scenario answers, not a stated specialization. Expertise shows up as the ability to name which regulatory gate applies to each product you sell, to distinguish informational content from promotional content, and to produce redacted work from regulated accounts. No vendor holds a peptide-specific credential from Google or LegitScript, so treat any implication of certifying authority as a red flag. A vendor who correctly says that your research-use-only line sits outside Healthcare Certification eligibility has demonstrated more than one who says the word "peptides" forty times.
### What questions expose unsafe recovery tactics?
Three work reliably. Ask what they would do if the appeal is denied twice; evasion-minded vendors answer with a new account or a new domain. Ask who the account will be registered to and whose verification documents will be submitted; the only acceptable answer involves your real business identity and accurate, unmodified documents, since submitting false information in verification programs is prohibited and [can cost verified status and the account](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15938075?hl=en). Ask whether reviewers will see the same landing page as users; anything other than yes is cloaking.
### Should the client own advertising accounts?
Yes, and the RFP should say so before proposals arrive. You should hold the Google Ads account, analytics property, tag container, domains, CRM and call tracking, with the vendor granted named-user access and manager-account linkage. Vendor-owned accounts create three problems at once: you lose performance history at termination, you inherit whatever policy history that account carries, and you cannot verify what was submitted on your behalf. Ownership also makes your exit cheap, which is the point.
### Can an agency promise certification or reinstatement?
No. Certification is granted by the certifying body against its standards, and advertising approval is a separate platform decision; LegitScript states that certification provides eligibility to advertise on participating platforms [subject to each platform's advertising policies](https://www.legitscript.com/certification/healthcare-certification/faq/). Reinstatement sits with the platform, which states that [accounts are reinstated only in compelling circumstances](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15938075?hl=en). A vendor can improve your odds by fixing the underlying issue and filing an accurate appeal. A vendor who commits to the outcome is either uninformed or planning something you would not approve.
### How should competing proposals be scored?
Independently, against weights published before the pitches, with disqualifiers applied outside the arithmetic. Use evidence, policy judgment, governance and commercial fit as your four criteria, score 0 to 3 on documentation rather than assertion, and record every assumption you made where a vendor stayed silent. Then re-read the top two proposals against your own business description from section one. If a proposal would read the same for any peptide company, it was written for a category, not for you.
A final note on timing. Platform policies and certification standards are revised without notice, so re-read the primary sources yourself before you sign, and route anything touching product status, labeling or regulatory conclusions to qualified legal review rather than to your agency.
