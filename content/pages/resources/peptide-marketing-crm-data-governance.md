CRM and Data Governance for Peptide Marketing
A peptide business is usually three businesses sharing one CRM: a patient-care operation, an institutional procurement operation and a consumer commerce operation. Peptide marketing data governance starts by separating those three, because nearly every later decision about consent, access, retention and disclosure depends on which one a record came from. Most failures in this category are not caused by ignoring regulation; they are caused by never drawing that first line.

Map the business and data categories
Before anyone writes a policy, write an inventory. The useful inventory is not a list of software. It is a list of data categories, each with an origin, an audience, a sensitivity level and a named owner, mapped to the systems that currently hold it.
Three categories show up repeatedly in peptide operations, and they rarely belong in the same workflow.
Patient care data. Intake forms, eligibility questions, symptom descriptions, provider notes, lab results, prescription and fulfillment records, billing detail and appointment history. This is the category most likely to meet the definition of individually identifiable health information, and it is generated by clinical functions, not by marketing.
Institutional and laboratory procurement data. Supplier contacts, purchasing agreements, certificates of analysis, distributor terms, research-account registrations, quoting history and accounts payable records. These are business-to-business relationships between organizations. The people in this category are professional contacts acting in a commercial role, and the records describe purchasing behavior rather than personal health.
Consumer commerce data. Ecommerce orders, shipping addresses, email and SMS opt-ins, cart and browsing behavior, loyalty records, support tickets and ad platform audiences. This category is the one marketers are most used to operating freely, and it is also the one where seemingly ordinary signals start to carry health meaning.
That last point deserves emphasis. The FTC's guidance is explicit that health information covers more than diagnoses and treatments: it includes anything that conveys information or enables an inference about a consumer's health, and browsing or purchase information can do exactly that ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach)). A product page view, a reorder cadence or a support ticket can carry an inference even when no clinician was ever involved.
Illustrative example, not a client scenario: a single contact record holds a wholesale inquiry from a laboratory purchasing manager, a consumer order placed from the same company domain, and a telehealth intake submitted by that person's spouse. One record, three categories, three different sets of rules. If the CRM cannot represent that difference, no downstream control can enforce it.
The output of this step is a data-category and system map: every category, the systems that store it, the integrations that move it, and the person accountable for each. Teams that skip the map end up governing software instead of governing data, which is how peptide CRM data boundaries quietly disappear.
Determine applicable obligations
The common framing in this market is that anything health-adjacent is HIPAA data. That framing is both inaccurate and operationally useless, because it tells a team nothing about what to actually do.
Coverage depends on the organization and the use. The HIPAA Rules apply if you are a covered entity, meaning a health plan, a health care provider that conducts standard health care transactions electronically, or a health care clearinghouse, and parts of the Rules also apply to business associates that help a covered entity carry out its health care activities or provide services involving access to protected health information ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach)). A supplement brand selling direct to consumers may sit entirely outside that definition. A telehealth prescriber billing electronically may sit squarely inside it. A company running both is running both sets of facts at once.
Falling outside HIPAA is not the same as falling outside regulation. The FTC Act prohibits unfair or deceptive acts or practices, and its obligations apply to HIPAA-covered entities and business associates as well as to companies that collect, use or share health information and are not required to comply with HIPAA ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach)). The FTC has also pointed to its actions involving BetterHelp, GoodRx and Premom to make the point that disclosing consumers' health information for advertising without their affirmative express consent may be an unfair practice ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach)).
A third regime catches businesses that assume they are unregulated. The FTC's Health Breach Notification Rule applies to certain businesses not covered by HIPAA, specifically vendors of personal health records, PHR related entities and third party service providers, and a breach of security under that Rule includes an unauthorized disclosure by the company itself, not only a security incident ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach)). An app or connected service holding consumer health information can land here without ever employing a clinician.
So the honest answer to "which rules apply to us" is that it requires a coverage analysis performed by counsel or a qualified privacy specialist against your actual entity structure, transactions and data uses. This page cannot perform that analysis, and neither can a vendor questionnaire. Local storage, a consent banner or a signed business associate agreement does not by itself resolve every duty you may have.
What a marketing organization can do is prepare the analysis properly: document the entities, the data categories from the previous step, the uses attached to each, and the states and jurisdictions involved. Define permitted marketing uses
Once categories and coverage are documented, marketing permissions become answerable instead of debatable. The instrument that makes this work is a purpose and permission register: for every marketing use of personal or health-adjacent data, record four things. What the data is. The stated purpose. The authority or consent basis for that purpose. Who receives it, including every vendor in the path.
For businesses operating under HIPAA, the baseline is restrictive by design. The Privacy Rule gives individuals control over whether and how protected health information is used for marketing, and with limited exceptions it requires an individual's written authorization before a use or disclosure for marketing can occur ([HHS](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/marketing/index.html)). Marketing is defined as making a communication about a product or service that encourages recipients to purchase or use it, and there are carved-out exceptions, including communications describing the covered entity's own health-related products or services, communications made for treatment of the individual, and communications for case management or care coordination ([HHS](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/marketing/index.html)).
Two conditions matter most to anyone building audiences or partner programs. An arrangement in which a covered entity discloses protected health information to another entity, in exchange for direct or indirect remuneration, so that entity can promote its own product or service, is marketing with no exceptions, and covered entities may not sell patient or enrollee lists to third parties without authorization from each person on the list ([HHS](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/marketing/index.html)). Where marketing involves remuneration to the covered entity from a third party, the authorization itself must state that remuneration is involved ([HHS](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/marketing/index.html)).
The authorization is also a document with requirements, not a checkbox. It should be signed before the use or disclosure, written in plain language, and specific about who is authorized to make the disclosure, what information is involved, who receives it, when the permission expires and the purpose of the use ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach)).
Peptide marketing consent architecture is simply this register made operational. Consumer commerce contacts carry commercial opt-ins and the FTC's deception and unfairness standards. Institutional contacts carry contract terms and business communication rules. Patient records carry authorization requirements and the Privacy Rule's conditions. A record that moves between categories does not carry its old permission with it, and that is the rule most CRMs are configured to ignore. Set access and retention controls
Broad CRM access granted by default is the most common failure point in this market, and it is rarely a decision anyone remembers making. It happens because the first five employees needed to see everything, and the permission model never caught up with the org chart.
The fix is a role-access matrix built on the categories you already mapped. List each role, then state for each data category whether that role may view, edit, export or disclose. An illustrative pattern, not a prescription: a paid media specialist works with aggregated consumer commerce data and campaign performance, no patient records; an intake coordinator works with patient records for scheduling and care, no marketing exports; a sales representative works with institutional procurement contacts only; an administrator can change permissions but does not make routine use of records.
Exports deserve their own rule. Most data leaves a governed system as a spreadsheet, not through an integration. Decide who may export, what an approved export looks like, where it is allowed to go, and how long it may live. An unmanaged CSV on a laptop is outside every control you built.
Retention is the control people skip because deleting records feels like losing assets. The FTC frames it as a core obligation, directing businesses to understand their data flows and answer plainly what health information they collect, from what sources, how they use it, to whom they disclose it, for what purposes, and how long they are retaining it and why, supported by a written program, training and supervision, and policies that address data retention, purpose and use limitations ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach)). Data you no longer need is cost and exposure without upside.
Access control also answers the question of regulated marketing data access across teams and agencies. External partners should receive the narrowest category and the shortest duration consistent with the work, with access reviewed on a schedule rather than when someone leaves. Peptide clinic CRM privacy is mostly a permissions problem wearing a legal costume. Govern integrations and AI access
Every new connection is a new data path, and the review belongs before the connection, not after the first report looks strange. That applies to ad platforms, analytics, marketing automation, call tracking, customer data platforms, scheduling tools and any AI system you point at your records.
Tracking technologies are where peptide businesses get into trouble fastest. HHS guidance states that the HIPAA Rules apply when information that regulated entities collect through tracking technologies or disclose to tracking technology vendors includes protected health information, and regulated entities are not permitted to use tracking technologies in a manner that results in impermissible disclosures of PHI ([HHS](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html)). On the consent question, the guidance is direct: disclosures cannot rest solely on telling individuals in a privacy policy, notice or terms of use that they will occur, and website banners asking users to accept or reject cookies do not constitute a valid HIPAA authorization ([HHS](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html)). Vendor promises do not close the gap either: it is insufficient for a tracking technology vendor to agree to remove or de-identify PHI before saving it, because disclosure without authorization still requires a signed business associate agreement and an applicable Privacy Rule permission ([HHS](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html)).
Read that guidance with its current legal status in view. On June 20, 2024, the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas vacated a portion of the bulletin, specifically the part providing that HIPAA obligations are triggered where an online technology connects an individual's IP address with a visit to an unauthenticated public webpage addressing specific health conditions or health care providers ([HHS](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html)). The rest of the analysis stands, including the treatment of authenticated portals, appointment and symptom-checker tools, and vendor relationships. The practical reading is that the facts of each page and each data flow decide the answer.
A workable review gate asks six questions of any integration or AI tool: which data categories it touches, whether it is a business associate under the applicable analysis, what it stores and for how long, whether it uses your data to train or improve its own models, who at the vendor can access the data, and how the connection gets revoked. Treat an AI system as a vendor relationship, not as a feature, because that is what it is.
Platform rules add another layer. Marketing Powered is built for Google's sensitive healthcare vertical, where retargeting is not permitted, and is LegitScript-aware and HIPAA-aware in how campaigns and data flows are structured. Marketing Powered also operates owned, local AI infrastructure for HIPAA-aware inference, which gives governance a real option: keep sensitive processing inside a defined boundary rather than extending it to another external endpoint. Prepare audit and incident records
Governance that exists only in people's heads cannot be demonstrated, and demonstration is the entire point when something goes wrong. Three records carry most of the weight.
First, a decision log. Each entry records the question asked, the data categories involved, the decision reached, who approved it, the date and the basis. When a platform changes a policy or a regulator asks why a pixel was configured a certain way, this is the document that answers.
Second, an access history. Who viewed, exported or changed which records, retained for a defined period and reviewed on a schedule. Audit controls and system activity review are explicitly among the safeguards the FTC describes under the HIPAA Security Rule, alongside access controls that limit electronic PHI to authorized workforce members, encryption where reasonable and appropriate, and contingency plans ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach)).
Third, a named incident owner with a written response sequence: who gets called, who contains the issue, who evaluates notification duties, who communicates. Name a person and a backup, not a department. The stakes are concrete. For regulated entities, an impermissible disclosure of PHI to a tracking technology vendor where no Privacy Rule permission applies and no business associate agreement exists carries a presumption of a breach of unsecured PHI unless the entity can demonstrate a low probability that the PHI was compromised ([HHS](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/hipaa-online-tracking/index.html)).
One more record matters: what you say publicly about your own practices. The FTC advises businesses not to make false or misleading claims that they are "HIPAA Compliant," "HIPAA Secure," "HIPAA Certified" or the like, and to avoid burying material facts where consumers will not read them ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/collecting-using-or-sharing-consumer-health-information-look-hipaa-ftc-act-health-breach)). Marketing claims about privacy are themselves a governed data practice.
None of this removes the possibility of an incident. It changes what you can show about how the organization operated before, during and after one.
