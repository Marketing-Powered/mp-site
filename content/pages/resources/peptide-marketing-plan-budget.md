A 90-Day Peptide Marketing Plan and Budget Framework
Most peptide businesses are asked to approve a media budget before anyone has confirmed that the offer can be advertised at all. A defensible peptide marketing plan runs the other direction: eligibility and evidence first, systems second, controlled acquisition third, with the money modeled from your margins and capacity rather than borrowed benchmarks. What follows is a conditional 90-day framework, written for executives who have to defend the spend.

Define the commercial objective before anyone proposes a channel mix
Three very different businesses get lumped under "peptide marketing," and the plans they need share almost nothing except the word peptide.
The first is a clinic or telehealth practice selling consultations. The buying unit is a person, the conversion event is a booked and attended appointment, and the operational constraint is provider calendar availability. The people who determine whether marketing works here are not marketers. They are the intake coordinator who answers at 4:50 p.m., the provider who decides whether a prospective patient is appropriate for care, and the billing lead who knows which visits are actually collected. If your front desk cannot answer within a working day, the campaign is subsidizing a queue.
The second is a procurement relationship: a 503A compounding pharmacy building prescriber accounts, or an ingredient supplier selling to formulators. Nobody buys that from a landing page in one sitting. The setting is a sequence of credential reviews, sample documentation, pharmacy agreements and relationship management, often months long. Demand generation here looks like targeted outreach, documentation that survives a compliance officer reading it, and a CRM that tracks accounts rather than form fills.
The third is ecommerce for products that are lawfully sellable and advertisable in your jurisdictions. This is the objective most likely to collide with platform rules, because consumer-facing product promotion is exactly where advertising policy is most restrictive.
The budget consequences diverge immediately. Consultation demand is capacity-bound and local or state-bound. Procurement demand is relationship-bound and slow, which means most of the first quarter's spend is content, data and sales enablement rather than clicks. Ecommerce demand is policy-bound and inventory-bound. General allocation principles still apply, and our broader [marketing budget guide](/resources/marketing-budget) covers them, but allocation is the second question. The first is which of these three businesses you actually are, in writing, before a channel plan exists. Many peptide companies run two of the three at once and then wonder why a single blended cost-per-acquisition number tells them nothing useful.
Set the eligibility foundation before committing media budget
Eligibility is not a compliance footnote. It determines whether a channel exists for you at all, and it is decided by regulators, certifiers and platforms in that order, each of which can say no independently.
Start with the product and the facility. In the United States, HHS released an enforcement discretion policy describing conditions under which FDA intends to refrain from specified enforcement action for 503A compounding involving six peptide ingredients: BPC-157 acetate, TB-500 acetate, Epitalon acetate, Semax free base, KPV acetate and MOTS-C acetate, as summarized by [LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/healthcare/hhs-peptide-compounding-policy-what-healthcare-businesses-need-to-know/). That same summary is explicit that enforcement discretion is not FDA approval, that the policy applies to qualifying 503A pharmacies and physician compounders rather than 503B outsourcing facilities, and that it sets out ten conditions covering ingredient identity, route of administration, FDA-registered sourcing with a valid certificate of analysis, reporting, labeling, single active ingredient formulation, oral bioavailability documentation and delivery device limits.
Then certification. LegitScript states that it updated its certification review approach effective November 2, 2026 to reflect the policy's conditional allowances, and that offering a covered peptide should not by itself block certification when the offering meets the policy's conditions and all other applicable standards, while peptides and formulations outside that scope remain a barrier to certification.
Then the platform, which is a separate decision again. Google's policy states plainly that some healthcare content cannot be advertised at all, while other content can only be advertised in certain locations by advertisers who have applied and been approved ([Google Ads healthcare and medicines policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031)). For United States online pharmacies and telemedicine providers, that same policy requires LegitScript certification or NABP accreditation, and requires the advertiser to be certified with Google as well. Separately, Google's unapproved substances rules prohibit promotion of certain products regardless of any claims of legality, including non-government approved products marketed as safe or effective for preventing, curing or treating a disease, and products that have been subject to government or regulatory action or warning ([unapproved pharmaceuticals and supplements list](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/2423645)).
LegitScript also notes that its review updates do not change the independent requirements of advertising platforms or payment providers, that certification does not assure access to those services, and that platform implementation may take time and may not align with its own timeline.
The practical gate, then, is a dependency map, not an opinion:
* Lawful operation. Question answered: Is the product lawfully compounded, sourced and dispensed?. Who decides: Regulators and your counsel.
* Product and facility status. Question answered: Which ingredient, form, route and facility type?. Who decides: FDA policy conditions, your pharmacy partner.
* Certification. Question answered: Does the offering clear certification review?. Who decides: LegitScript or NABP.
* Channel eligibility. Question answered: Will the platform accept this advertiser and this claim?. Who decides: Google, Meta, other platforms.
* Account status. Question answered: Is the advertising account in good standing?. Who decides: Platform enforcement.
Each row can fail while the row above passes. Executive ownership of that map is exactly the kind of work a [fractional CMO engagement](/services/fractional-cmo-agency) is meant to hold, because it sits above any single channel manager.
Build the operating prerequisites that make spending defensible
Once eligibility is mapped, the question becomes whether the business can operate an ad account without creating new risk every week. Four prerequisites decide that.
Claim review. Someone with clinical and regulatory standing has to approve marketing language before it publishes, including ad copy, landing pages, email, SMS and the social content a founder posts on a Friday night. LegitScript's guidance for businesses preparing for certification and platform requirements includes reviewing labels, marketing and patient communications so they do not suggest that enforcement discretion means FDA approval. That is a workflow, not a document. Name the reviewer, define turnaround expectations, and keep a version history that shows which approved variant was live on which date. When a platform reviews your destination, the record is the defense.
Ownership. Decide who signs off, who can pause spend, and who speaks to a certifier or platform support. Ambiguity here is what turns a routine disapproval into a two-week stall.
Tracking and attribution. The measurement goal is to connect spend to an outcome the business actually banks: an attended consultation, a dispensed and collected order, a prescriber account that writes. The founder's background is relevant to how this gets built. The founder ran marketing for a treatment network that grew from 3 to 24 locations and managed $1.5 to $2M per month in Google Ads across 11+ behavioral and mental health accounts, in Google's sensitive healthcare vertical, where retargeting is not permitted. That environment forces the discipline this market needs: attribution tracked to admission, not just lead, and campaign decisions made without the remarketing crutch available in unregulated categories. Build call tracking, CRM stages and offline conversion import before launch, because retrofitting attribution after spend begins means the first month of data is a story rather than evidence.
Handoff. Clinical and legal review, marketing execution and sales or intake must pass work between them with defined inputs and outputs. The common failure is a marketing team that writes, waits, edits, waits again, and burns thirty days of the quarter in a review queue nobody staffed.
Data handling belongs in this phase too. Decide which systems may hold identifiable information, which may not, and what flows between them. Model spend and capacity from your own inputs
There is no credible published peptide benchmark for cost per acquisition, conversion rate or sales cycle that would survive scrutiny across compounding pharmacies, telehealth practices, med spas and supplement brands. Anyone quoting one is quoting a different business than yours. So the budget comes from your numbers.
Five inputs do the work:
* Contribution margin per unit or visit. What it governs: The ceiling on what an acquisition can cost.
* Repeat and retention behavior. What it governs: Whether you can pay above first-order margin.
* Sales cycle length. What it governs: How long before spend shows up as revenue.
* Service or fulfillment capacity. What it governs: The maximum useful volume per week.
* Existing first-party data. What it governs: How fast measurement becomes trustworthy.
The arithmetic is deliberately plain. If contribution margin per new patient over a defined window is M, and leadership sets a payback period of P months, then the acceptable acquisition cost is whatever fraction of M you are willing to commit inside P. Multiply that by the number of new customers your capacity can actually serve each month, and you have a spending ceiling that is defensible in a board meeting. Working capital, not optimism, sets the floor.
Here is one hypothetical, with numbers chosen for arithmetic clarity and not drawn from market data. Assume a clinic with 60 available new-patient slots per month, contribution margin of $400 per new patient across the first 90 days, and a decision to spend no more than half of that margin on acquisition. The ceiling is $200 per acquired patient and $12,000 per month at full capacity. Now assume the sales cycle runs 45 days from first inquiry to attended visit. Spend in month one shows up as revenue mostly in month two, so the cash plan must carry roughly six weeks of spend before matching revenue appears. Change any input, and the plan changes: if capacity is 20 slots rather than 60, the same $12,000 is overspending into a queue.
Two more scenarios are worth modeling in your own numbers rather than reading here. One is a procurement motion where the unit is a prescriber account with a long cycle and high lifetime value, which justifies more content and sales enablement and far less click volume. The other is an ecommerce motion where margin is thin and the binding constraint is policy risk, which argues for a smaller media commitment held in reserve against a disapproval cycle.
Build a remediation line into the budget as well. If documentation, site changes, certification work or review capacity are needed, that is real cost, and it should be funded explicitly instead of quietly consuming media budget. Cross-reference the [healthcare marketing compliance checklist](/resources/healthcare-marketing-compliance-checklist) when scoping that line, and treat every figure above as modeling logic, not a projection.
Sequence the 90 days as gated phases, not promises
Treat the quarter as three planning phases. Each phase ends in a decision, and no phase commits to an approval date, a reinstatement, a cost per acquisition or a revenue figure, because none of those are within a marketing team's control.
Days 1 to 30: eligibility, documentation and infrastructure. Confirm ingredient, form, route, facility type and compounding arrangement with your pharmacy partner and counsel. Assemble the documentation that certification review will ask for, including supply chain and pharmacy relationship information. Audit every live page and ad asset against current platform policy. Build tracking, CRM stages and the claim review workflow. Decide the single commercial objective for the quarter. If certification is part of the path, scope cost and timing with the [LegitScript certification resource](/resources/legitscript-certification) and with LegitScript directly, since review timelines belong to the certifier, not to an agency.
Days 31 to 60: controlled, limited-scope launch with monitoring. Launch narrow. One objective, one geography set, tightly governed copy, and a budget sized so that a disapproval is an inconvenience rather than a crisis. Google's policy describes ads that meet location and certification requirements as labeled "Eligible (limited)", which means serving is constrained by design, so plan for restricted reach rather than treating it as a malfunction. Watch disapprovals daily in the first two weeks. Record what was approved, what was rejected and the exact asset involved, because that log becomes your policy knowledge base.
Days 61 to 90: evaluation and the scale decision. Compare what the data can support against what leadership assumed. Did capacity absorb the volume? Did attributed outcomes reach the stage where revenue is recognized, or did they stall mid-funnel? Is the policy environment the same as it was on day one? Then choose: scale, hold, reallocate across objectives, or stop.
A word on risk, since it shapes sequencing. Google treats unauthorized pharmacy violations as egregious, states that accounts will be suspended upon detection without prior warning, and says accounts are only reinstated in compelling circumstances ([healthcare and medicines policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031)). That asymmetry is the whole argument for phase one. Enforcement evasion tactics are not a strategy and are not something we will build. Neither is a promise that a suspended account comes back.
Run executive reviews that compare verified facts and uncertainty
The monthly review is where this framework either holds or quietly decays into a reporting ritual. Keep it to four people and four questions.
Who should be in the room: the executive sponsor who controls budget, the person accountable for clinical or regulatory review, the person accountable for intake or fulfillment capacity, and whoever owns media execution. Agency participation is useful only if the operator in the room can speak to policy, measurement and economics together rather than reading dashboard screenshots.
What gets reviewed, in this order:
* Verified versus uncertain. Separate what the data supports from what people believe. An inquiry count is verified. A claim that those inquiries will convert at a given rate is an estimate until the cycle completes. Label them differently in the deck.
* Policy and certification changes since the last review. Platform rules and compounding policy are moving. LegitScript itself advises businesses to monitor updates to FDA's enforcement discretion policy and to be prepared to adjust offerings if it is revised or withdrawn, and to independently review the current policies of advertising platforms, payment processors, acquirers and card brands. Assign a named owner to recheck primary sources, not a general intention to stay informed.
* Spend against capacity. If attended visits or fulfilled orders are capped by staffing, the correct action is often to hold spend and fix throughput. Spending past capacity manufactures a backlog and a reputation problem at the same time.
* The next investment decision. Continue, pause, reallocate or expand scope, with the reasoning written down. The founder's work as a court-certified expert witness in advertising strategy shapes this habit: decisions that are documented at the time hold up later, and decisions that are reconstructed afterward rarely do.
Score the quarter on inputs you control as well as outputs you do not. Review turnaround time, asset approval rate, tracking completeness and capacity utilization are all leading indicators, and they move before revenue does.

Questions executives ask about peptide marketing plans
Should paid media start in the first month?
Usually not at scale. The first month is for resolving whether the offer, product classification and certification status permit advertising on the channels under discussion, and for building tracking that makes month two interpretable. If eligibility is already settled and infrastructure exists, a tightly scoped test can start earlier. What rarely works is committing a full monthly media budget before the dependency map is complete, because a disapproval or a certification gap then costs both the spend and the time.
What inputs determine the budget?
Your contribution margin per unit or visit, repeat behavior, sales cycle length, service or fulfillment capacity, available first-party data, and the working capital you can carry through the cycle. The budget also needs a separate line for documentation, review capacity and site remediation. Published industry averages for this category are not reliable enough to plan against, which is why the model in the spend section uses your figures rather than ours. The general principles are in our [marketing budget resource](/resources/marketing-budget).
Can a 90-day plan promise approvals or revenue?
No. Certification decisions belong to the certifier, advertising eligibility belongs to the platform, and both can change. LegitScript's own guidance notes that platform implementation of policy changes may take time and may not align with its timeline, and that certification does not assure access to advertising or payment services. A 90-day plan sequences the work and sets decision points. Anyone offering a date-certain approval or a revenue figure for this market is describing something they do not control.
How should a suspended account change the roadmap?
It moves the first phase from preparation to diagnosis. The questions are what triggered the action, whether the underlying offer or claim is eligible at all, and whether the business model can support acquisition through compliant channels if that account never returns. Google describes certain violations as egregious and says reinstatement happens only in compelling circumstances, so a roadmap that assumes recovery is a roadmap built on a wish. Plan a parallel path that does not depend on the suspended asset, and do not pursue replacement accounts or any other evasion approach. That path makes the situation worse and we will not build it.
What should leadership review each month?
Four things: what is verified versus estimated, what changed in platform policy and certification requirements since the last review, whether spend is matched to real service capacity, and what the next investment decision is. Keep the scorecard short enough that people read it and specific enough that a decision follows. If a review produces no decision, shorten the deck and lengthen the discussion.
