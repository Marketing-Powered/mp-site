Compromised Peptide Websites and Advertising Recovery
A confirmed security finding and an advertising policy disapproval are two different problems, and closing one does not close the other. Most operators researching peptide website compromised ads need two things at once: a documented technical remediation path, and a separate, honest read on whether the offer itself can be advertised after the site is clean. This guide sequences both so you stop paying twice for the same outcome.

Confirm what the notice actually says
The first mistake is reading a disapproval as a single verdict. A Google Ads account can surface several different problems using similar-looking language, and the correct response changes completely depending on which one you have. A compromised site finding points at your code and hosting. An unapproved substance finding points at your product. A certification or geography restriction points at your business status. Treating all three as "the ads got rejected" is how teams spend a month fixing the wrong layer.
Start by reading the policy label, not the summary line. Google's compromised sites policy defines a compromised site as a site or destination whose code has been manipulated to act in ways that benefit a third party without the knowledge of the site or destination's owner or operator, often in a way that harms the site's users, and lists examples including sites injecting scripts or code that transmits user data without consent, such as credit card skimmers, installing malware on end user devices, launching pop-up ads or redirecting users to other websites, and operating a website using a content management system with known security vulnerabilities where it has been exploited ([Google Ads compromised sites policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15938376?hl=en)). That definition is useful because it is narrow. If nothing in your situation matches it, you may be looking at a different policy entirely.
Inside the account, Google instructs advertisers to go to Ads within the Campaigns menu, add a filter for "Policy Details: Compromised site," and continue if ads are disapproved for that reason, and notes that reviewing the disapproval reason shows where the content is loaded from, since Google Ads sometimes shows compromised domains in your Google Ads account when it is possible to identify them ([Google Ads compromised sites policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15938376?hl=en)). Record those filtered results exactly: the ad IDs, the final URLs cited, the policy label, the date, and any third-party domain named in the disapproval detail. Screenshot the Policy Manager view before anything changes.
Timing matters here too, and it is worth knowing where you stand before you panic. For this policy area, Google states that violations will not lead to immediate account suspension without prior warning and that it will issue a warning at least 7 days before suspending an account ([Google Ads compromised sites policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15938376?hl=en)). That is a different posture from the healthcare rules that carry suspension on detection, which is covered further down. Confirm which of the two you are in before you decide how aggressively to move, because the response sequence is not the same.
One more distinction to settle on day one: a Google Ads policy decision and a Google Search security flag are separate systems with separate queues. Search Console reports security issues in categories including hacked content, malware and unwanted software, and social engineering ([Search Console security issues report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9044101)). You may have one, the other, or both. Write down which surfaces are affected before you brief a developer, because that list defines the scope of the cleanup and the scope of the evidence you will need later.
Preserve the evidence before anyone touches a file
The instinct after a security notice is to delete the bad code immediately. Resist it for the length of one working session. Cleanup destroys the record of what happened, and that record is what makes your later review request credible instead of speculative. You cannot describe a cause you did not document.
Assign one accountable technical owner before work starts. Not a committee, and not the marketing team by default. That person owns the timeline, the access list and the final write-up. Everyone else routes findings through them, which prevents the common failure where a contractor patches a file at the same hour another contractor restores a backup over it.
Capture these artifacts first. The original notice language, copied in full, including the policy name and the detail text. The exact affected URLs. Server and application access logs covering a window that starts well before the first detection date. A file-level diff or list of modified files with timestamps. The current user list for the content management system, the hosting account, the DNS provider, the tag manager and any deployment pipeline. A copy of the compromised state, stored offline, so the cleaned site is not your only artifact.
Search Console helps anchor the timeline. Google notes that the issue details show the date that the issue was first detected on your site, along with a brief description and a link to learn more, and that the sample list of affected URLs is not necessarily complete, but just a sample of pages on your site affected by the selected issue ([Search Console security issues report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9044101)). Treat the sample as a starting point, never as the full blast radius. Teams that clean only the sample URLs usually get flagged again.
Then close the door. Rotate credentials for every system on that access list, revoke stale API tokens and integration keys, remove dormant administrator accounts, and reset session tokens. Do it in a single coordinated pass so the attacker does not simply reuse a credential you missed. Record who rotated what and when, because an advertising reviewer or an insurer may ask you to prove containment preceded cleanup rather than followed it.
For regulated sellers there is a second reason to preserve evidence: a compromise that touched forms, checkout, scripts or analytics may carry notification obligations under contracts, payment rules or privacy law. That question belongs to qualified counsel, not to a marketing vendor. Keep the forensic record intact so counsel can make that call on facts instead of recollection. Contain the incident and close the underlying cause
Surface cleanup is the most expensive shortcut in this category. Removing an injected script makes the symptom disappear and leaves the entry point open, which produces reinfection, a second disapproval, and a much harder conversation with a reviewer who has already seen you claim the problem was solved.
Google is direct about who should do this work, advising advertisers to contact a web developer or security specialist for a thorough security assessment of the website, which can help identify and eliminate any malware, malicious code, or security vulnerabilities, and to update website software, making sure the content management system, themes and plugins are updated to the latest versions ([Google Ads compromised sites policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15938376?hl=en)). Search Console frames the skill requirement the same way, noting that fixing a malware issue on your site requires the ability to read and understand code, and possibly web server configurations for your site ([Search Console security issues report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9044101)).
A defensible remediation pass answers four questions in writing:
* What was changed. The specific files, database records, templates, scheduled tasks or server configuration entries that were altered, with timestamps.
* How entry happened. The vulnerable component, the exposed credential, the unpatched version, or the compromised third-party integration. If the cause cannot be determined, say so plainly and describe the compensating controls applied instead. An honest "root cause not conclusively identified, following controls applied" is stronger than an invented explanation.
* What was removed or restored. Whether the site was cleaned in place or rebuilt from a known-good state, and how you verified the restore point predates the compromise.
* What was hardened. Patch level, credential rotation, administrative access reduction, file permission corrections, integrity monitoring, and any removal of unused plugins or themes that enlarged the attack surface.
Scope the fix site-wide rather than page-by-page. Google's guidance for security issues is explicit that you should fix the issue throughout your site, because fixing the issue on just some pages will not earn a partial return to search results, and that if the report lists multiple security issues, you should fix all of them ([Search Console security issues report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9044101)). The same logic applies to advertising destinations. A clean landing page attached to a still-compromised domain is not a clean destination.
Keep the engagement defensive. The job is removal of unauthorized content and closure of the cause, not making a flagged page look different to a crawler. Nothing in this process should involve serving different content to reviewers, spinning up replacement accounts or obscuring the destination. Those actions create a far more serious enforcement problem than the one you started with. Verify the destination end to end
Verification is a separate step from cleanup, performed after the developer says the work is done, and it should be adversarial rather than confirmatory. You are not trying to prove the site is clean. You are trying to find the piece that was missed.
Work the full destination path the way a reviewer or a user would. Start at the ad's final URL, then follow every redirect hop, including tracking templates, affiliate parameters and any URL shortener in the chain. Redirects are a common hiding place because they execute before anyone looks at the page. Check mobile and desktop user agents separately, and check behavior for a first-time visitor with no cookies, since injected code frequently targets exactly that profile.
Then inventory everything the page loads that you did not write. Third-party scripts, chat widgets, review embeds, pixels and tag manager containers, form handlers, payment elements, content delivery network rules, plugins and theme files, and any edge or worker scripts. For each one, confirm it is still needed, confirm the account that owns it is under your control, and confirm its credentials were rotated. Tag containers deserve particular attention because a tag can be published by an account you removed from the site months ago.
Use the independent checks Google points to rather than relying on your own browser. Advertisers are told to use Google Search Console to run a Security Issues Report to identify malicious software and other security issues on the site, and to set up email notifications in Search Console to proactively monitor for these issues, and to check the status of the website or final URL of disapproved ads in Google's External Safe Browsing Site Status Checker ([Google Ads compromised sites policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15938376?hl=en)). Search Console also warns that Safe Browsing displays warnings based on browsing context, so you may or may not be able to reproduce the warnings, and the Security Issues report should be relied on as the source of truth for whether issues exist or have been fixed ([Search Console security issues report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9044101)). That single sentence resolves most internal arguments about whether a problem is really gone.
Verification output should be dated evidence, not a verbal all-clear: a clean scan result with a timestamp, a Search Console report state, a Safe Browsing status check, a log review summary covering the period after remediation, and a short note on monitoring now in place. Store them together.
Finally, verify the content a human reviewer will read, not only the code. A compromise sometimes leaves behind injected pages, spam directories or altered copy that create a second, non-security policy problem on a regulated site. Claims, product descriptions, intake forms and disclosures should be re-checked against your own standards at the same time, and the [Healthcare Marketing Compliance Checklist](/resources/healthcare-marketing-compliance-checklist) is a reasonable structure for that pass.
Prepare a review handoff a reviewer can act on
A review request is not an appeal letter and it is not an argument. It is a short factual record that lets someone else confirm, quickly, that the condition which caused the flag no longer exists. The structure Google describes for reconsideration requests transfers well: a good request explains the exact quality issue on your site, describes the steps you have taken to fix the issue, and documents the outcome of your efforts ([Search Console security issues report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9044101)).
Build the packet in that order. Cause, in one or two sentences, named specifically. Corrective actions, as a list with dates. Verification outcome, with the evidence described above. Prevention, meaning what now stops recurrence. Keep it factual and keep it short. Redact credentials, personal data and anything that identifies individuals; a reviewer needs the shape of the incident, not your logs.
Know which door you are knocking on. Where a site has been disallowed in Safe Browsing, Google instructs owners to fix the malware issue and file an appeal through Google Search Console to have the domain removed from the Safe Browsing threat list, after which site or landing pages should automatically be re-enabled to serve ads, with support contacted if ads are still disapproved after completing this process ([Google Ads compromised sites policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15938376?hl=en)). Inside Google Ads, the mechanics differ: editing the ad resubmits the ad and its destination for review, and where changes were made only to the landing page, those ads are appealed using the "Made changes to comply with policy" button, while a decision believed to be in error is disputed from the Policy Manager using the "Dispute" button ([Google Ads compromised sites policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15938376?hl=en)). Picking the wrong button sends the wrong signal about what changed.
Then wait properly. Google asks advertisers to resubmit ads for review after cleaning the website and allow the system up to 72 hours to recrawl and re-evaluate the landing page ([Google Ads compromised sites policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15938376?hl=en)). Search security review runs on its own clock, where most reconsideration reviews can take several days or weeks and you should not resubmit a request before receiving a decision on an outstanding one, since submitting while the issue is unfixed can cause longer turnaround or lead to being marked a repeat offender ([Search Console security issues report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9044101)). Repeated submissions are the second-most common self-inflicted delay in this process, after shallow cleanup.
Evidence discipline is a learned habit, not a formality. Marketing Powered's founder is a court-certified expert witness in advertising strategy, and the same standard applies to a review packet: state only what the record supports, label estimates as estimates, and never assert a cause you cannot show. Keep business eligibility as a separate workstream
Here is the part that decides whether any of the preceding work pays off. A clean, secure, fully verified website does not create advertising eligibility for an offer that policy does not permit. These are independent gates, and the security gate is often the easier one.
Google's healthcare framework ties eligibility to the offer, to approval status and to geography: some types of healthcare-related content cannot be advertised at all, while others can only be advertised in certain locations by advertisers who have applied and been approved to advertise those products or services, and for most healthcare policies, if the campaign targets allowed locations and the domain is properly certified, the ad is labeled "Eligible (limited)" and can run in allowed locations ([Google Ads healthcare and medicines policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031)). Under the unapproved substances rules, Google does not allow the promotion of certain products regardless of any claims of legality, including non-government approved products that are marketed in a way that implies they are safe or effective for use in preventing, curing or treating a particular disease or ailment, products that imply they are as effective as prescription drugs or controlled substances, and products that have been subject to any government or regulatory action or warning ([Google Ads healthcare and medicines policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031)). The supporting list is published as a non-exhaustive set of examples of the types of content monitored under the unapproved substances policy ([Google's unapproved pharmaceuticals and supplements list](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/2423645)), so absence of a specific product name from that list is not a finding of eligibility.
Read those criteria carefully, because they turn on marketing presentation and regulatory status rather than on disclaimers. Rewriting a page is not a workaround; it changes what you are offering, or it changes nothing. Adjacent rules can bite harder still. Google states that offering prescription drugs without a prescription is not allowed and targeting locations where you are not licensed is not allowed, treats such violations as egregious, with accounts suspended upon detection and without prior warning ([Google Ads healthcare and medicines policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031)). That is a materially different risk profile than a compromised site flag.
The following comparison is illustrative, meant to structure your own assessment rather than to predict a decision in any specific account:
* What is being judged. Security workstream: Code, hosting and destination integrity. Eligibility workstream: The product, claims, certification and target locations.
* Who does the work. Security workstream: Developer or security specialist. Eligibility workstream: Counsel, regulatory advisor, certification body, marketing lead.
* Evidence produced. Security workstream: Remediation report, clean scans, access and log review. Eligibility workstream: Product status assessment, licensure, certification status, claim review.
* What a successful outcome means. Security workstream: The destination is no longer compromised. Eligibility workstream: The offer is permitted where you intend to run.
* What it does not resolve. Security workstream: Any product or claim restriction. Eligibility workstream: Any open security finding.
Sequence accordingly. Resolve and document the compromise, then request review, and run the eligibility assessment in parallel rather than after, so you learn early whether paid search is a viable channel for this offer at all. If the answer is that it is not, that is valuable information, and it redirects budget toward channels and owned assets that can actually carry demand instead of funding repeated appeals.
