Private AI Vendor Evaluation Checklist: The Evidence to Request
Most private AI proposals compress five separate claims into one adjective. A useful private AI vendor evaluation pulls that adjective apart and asks for evidence behind each piece: network path, data location, identity and access, model ownership, and exit rights. This guide turns sales language into specific documents you can request, read and test before you sign anything.

Translate claims into evidence requests
"Private," "secure," "owned" and "compliant" are not architecture. Each one can describe a different layer of a system, and a vendor can be accurate about one while silent on the other three. A deployment can be private on the network path and still run inference on a shared managed service. A system can be hosted on hardware the vendor owns and still store your documents in a third-party index. A proposal can reference a recognized risk framework without describing a single control that was configured, tested or logged.
The first move in a private AI procurement checklist is to stop evaluating adjectives and start evaluating artifacts. For every claim in the proposal, ask what the word means in this specific configuration, then ask which document proves it. If no document exists, that is a finding, not a failure of the question.
A practical way to structure the request is to map each adjective to the thing it does not establish on its own. This table is illustrative. It shows how to decompose claims, not what any particular vendor offers.
* Private. What it may describe: Network path, tenancy, or physical location. What it does not establish: Who operates the model-serving layer. Evidence to request: Network diagram naming every endpoint and provider.
* Secure. What it may describe: Encryption, access policy, or hardening posture. What it does not establish: Whether controls were configured for your tenant. Evidence to request: Access control configuration and log samples.
* Owned. What it may describe: Hardware, code, configuration, or model weights. What it does not establish: Which of those four you keep at exit. Evidence to request: Ownership and licensing schedule by asset.
* Compliant. What it may describe: A framework the vendor consulted. What it does not establish: A tested, assessed or attested system. Evidence to request: Assessment scope, date, and who performed it.
Frameworks help you organize the questions. The National Institute of Standards and Technology describes its AI Risk Management Framework as voluntary guidance for incorporating trustworthiness into the design, development, use and evaluation of AI systems, with a generative AI profile published as an additional risk resource ([NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework)). Voluntary guidance is a structure for inquiry. Alignment with it is a claim that itself requires evidence: which functions were applied, by whom, to which system, and with what output.
Write the evidence requests into the RFP rather than the kickoff call. Vendors answer precisely when the answer becomes part of the contract record, and vaguely when it stays in a slide. Ask for the artifact, the date it was produced, and the name of the person accountable for keeping it current.
Inspect data and provider boundaries
The single most useful document in secure AI vendor assessment is a complete data flow map. Not a marketing diagram with three boxes, but a map that follows a representative record from the moment it enters the system to the moment it is deleted. Where does the input originate? Which services receive it in transit? Where is it embedded, indexed, cached, logged or retained? Which human roles can read it at each stage? Which named third parties touch it?
Ask for named providers, not categories. "Cloud provider" and "model API" are categories. A subprocessor list gives you entity names, the function each performs, the regions involved, and the retention behavior of each. If a vector database, an embedding service, an evaluation tool, an observability platform or a transcription service is part of the pipeline, each belongs on that list. Organizations in regulated settings should also confirm which agreements govern each relationship and whether the vendor can change a subprocessor without notifying you.
Private network access is often presented as the end of the conversation. It is better understood as one layer. AWS documents private network access to Amazon Bedrock through VPC interface endpoints, which keeps traffic between your virtual private cloud and the service off the public internet ([AWS Bedrock VPC interface endpoints](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/vpc-interface-endpoints.html)). That is a meaningful control, and it is a statement about the route, not about who owns the model-serving infrastructure at the other end of it. A buyer who hears "private" and understands "we operate the models" has merged two separate facts.
Responsibility is the second half of the boundary question. AWS states that under its shared responsibility model the customer remains responsible for their content and for their security configuration, and its data protection documentation points to permissions, encryption and activity logging as customer-side work ([AWS Bedrock data protection](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/data-protection.html)). In a vendor engagement, that customer-side work does not disappear. It moves to someone. Your evaluation needs to establish whether it moved to the vendor under contract, stayed with your IT team by default, or was never assigned at all. The third outcome is common and is the one that surfaces during an incident.
Three follow-up requests are worth making every time: a written statement of whether your data, prompts or outputs are used for model training or product improvement under any circumstance; a retention schedule covering logs, caches and intermediate artifacts rather than only the primary datastore; and a description of what happens to data held by each subprocessor when the engagement ends.

Review identity and actions
Storage boundaries tell you where data sits. Identity controls tell you who can retrieve it, and action controls tell you what the system is allowed to do once it has it. Both are routinely under-specified in private AI proposals because they require configuration work rather than architecture claims.
Start with retrieval. If the system answers questions over your documents, ask whether retrieval respects existing document permissions or whether the index is effectively flat. Microsoft's documentation for Azure AI Search describes restricting retrieval through document-level permissions tied to user identity, so that a query returns only what the requesting user is entitled to see, and it notes that several native permission capabilities are in preview ([Microsoft Learn: document-level access control](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/search/search-document-level-access-overview)). Preview status matters for a production decision. Ask the vendor to confirm, in writing, which features in their design are generally available, which are preview, and what the fallback is if a preview capability changes.
Then ask about administrators. Who holds the highest privilege in the environment, how many such accounts exist, whether vendor staff hold standing access or request it per task, and how that access is logged and reviewed. A short responsibility matrix removes most of the ambiguity. The example below is illustrative and should be rewritten against the actual proposal.
* Grant or revoke end-user access. Vendor: Executes. Buyer IT: Approves. Joint approval: Quarterly review.
* Change model, prompt or retrieval configuration. Vendor: Proposes. Buyer IT: Approves. Joint approval: Logged change record.
* Add or replace a subprocessor. Vendor: Proposes. Buyer IT: Approves. Joint approval: Written notice before change.
* Access production data for support. Vendor: Requests. Buyer IT: Approves. Joint approval: Time-bound, logged.
* Export or delete client data. Vendor: Executes. Buyer IT: Requests. Joint approval: Confirmation record.
Action controls matter because language-model systems can be influenced by the content they process. OWASP describes both direct and indirect prompt injection in language-model applications, where crafted input alters model behavior, with potential effects including unwanted information disclosure and unauthorized use of connected functions (OWASP GenAI: LLM01 Prompt Injection). The practical consequence for a buyer is that any integration which lets the system send email, write to a CRM, trigger a workflow or query another system needs its own authorization boundary. Ask which actions require human confirmation, which run unattended, what the system is permitted to do with untrusted content such as inbound email or scraped pages, and how an unauthorized action would be detected after the fact.
Vendors who have operated these systems can answer these questions in specifics. Our own [published security practices](/company/security) describe how we document access and operating responsibilities, and the same level of detail is reasonable to request from anyone bidding.
Evaluate reliability and quality
A demo is a curated performance. An acceptance test is a measurement on your material, with criteria written before the test runs. The difference decides whether you learn anything about the system you are buying.
Build a representative test set from your own corpus and your own task. Include the easy cases, the ambiguous cases, the cases involving documents that contradict each other, the cases where the correct answer is that the system does not know, and the cases that involve restricted material the system should refuse to surface to the requesting user. Fifty to a hundred realistic items is usually enough to expose a weak configuration. Keep a portion of the set private from the vendor so you can distinguish a system that generalizes from one that was tuned to the examples it was shown.
Define pass criteria in advance and in business terms. Accuracy on factual retrieval. Correct refusal behavior. Citation of source documents where required. Latency under concurrent load at your expected volume. Behavior when an upstream service is unavailable. Cost per task at projected usage, including retries. A score without a pre-agreed threshold is a demonstration, not a test.
The following acceptance schedule is illustrative. Adjust the measures and thresholds to the workload you are actually buying.
* Retrieval accuracy. Method: Blind set of buyer documents and questions. Pass condition: Agreed threshold, scored by named reviewers. Who runs it: Buyer, vendor observes.
* Permission integrity. Method: Queries run as users with differing entitlements. Pass condition: No retrieval outside entitlement. Who runs it: Joint.
* Refusal and uncertainty. Method: Unanswerable and out-of-scope prompts. Pass condition: System declines rather than fabricates. Who runs it: Buyer.
* Adversarial input. Method: Injection attempts in documents and inbound content. Pass condition: No unauthorized action or disclosure. Who runs it: Vendor, evidence shared.
* Load and failover. Method: Peak concurrency, dependency outage simulated. Pass condition: Latency and degradation within agreed limits. Who runs it: Vendor, witnessed.
Ask for known failure modes in writing. Every working system has them, and a vendor who names theirs is easier to trust than one who implies there are none. Ask what happens when a source document is outdated, when two documents disagree, when a user asks something the corpus cannot answer, and how often such cases are reviewed by a person.
Change management is the part buyers forget. Models get updated, prompts get edited, retrieval settings get tuned, and a system that passed acceptance in March can behave differently in September. Request the vendor's change process: how changes are tested against a regression set before production, who approves them, how they are recorded, and how you are notified. Ask whether you can pin a model version and for how long. These questions also clarify where the system actually runs, which is the subject of our page on [owned AI systems and deployment boundaries](/ai-edge/owned-ai-systems).
Review ownership and exit
Ownership is four questions, not one, and each has a different answer in most contracts.
Your data. Confirm in writing that you own your source data, your prompts, your outputs and any derived artifacts such as embeddings, fine-tuning datasets and evaluation results. Derived artifacts are where ambiguity usually lives. An index built from your records is still built from your records. Ask for the export format, the method, the timeline and who bears the cost.
Code and configuration. Custom integrations, prompt libraries, retrieval logic and workflow definitions may be licensed to you, assigned to you, or retained as vendor property. All three are legitimate commercial positions. Only one of them leaves you able to continue operating without the vendor. Ask which applies to each component, and ask for the answer as a schedule rather than a sentence.
Model licenses and dependencies. Open-weight models are released under a range of terms, and open-source software licensed under OSI-approved licenses is a separate category from open-weight model releases. If the system depends on a commercial API, you are holding a dependency rather than an asset, and you should know the notice period, pricing mechanics and deprecation policy attached to it. Ask what happens to your system if a model is retired by its publisher.
Credentials and accounts. Determine whose name is on the cloud accounts, the API keys, the domains and the monitoring tools. If the vendor's tenant holds everything, the exit is a migration project regardless of what the data ownership clause says. The cleaner pattern is buyer-owned accounts with vendor access granted under role-based permissions that can be revoked in an afternoon.
Write the exit into the agreement while both parties are still enthusiastic. A sample exit schedule covers the trigger events, the notice period, the export deliverables and their formats, a transition support window at a stated rate, the deletion obligation for the vendor and each subprocessor, and the evidence of deletion you will receive. Deletion certificates should name the systems covered, including backups and logs, with a stated timeline for backup expiry.
Hardware ownership deserves one clarification, because it is frequently blurred in proposals. Owning servers changes where processing happens and who controls the physical environment. It does not by itself transfer ownership of your data, and it does not settle the licensing of the software running on it. Those are separate contractual facts, which is why we keep custody and processing boundaries documented separately in our material on [data custody and processing boundaries](/ai-edge/data-sovereignty).
Record gaps before contracting
The output of private AI due diligence is a written register, not a verdict. Two proposals rarely differ on whether they have gaps. They differ on which gaps, how material those gaps are, and whether the vendor will close them in writing before the signature.
Sort every finding into three categories.
Must-have. Blocking. The engagement does not proceed without a documented resolution. Typical entries include an unnamed subprocessor handling regulated data, no retrieval permission model where entitlements differ between users, no export path for your own data, or no answer on training use of your content.
Conditional. Acceptable with a compensating control that is named, assigned and dated. A preview feature can be acceptable if there is a documented fallback. Vendor standing access can be acceptable if it is logged, time-bound and reviewed. Write the control into the agreement rather than the meeting notes.
Unresolved. The vendor has not answered yet, or the answer was not specific enough to evaluate. Keep a date and an owner against each item. Unresolved items that stay unresolved through two rounds are usually telling you something.
* Must-have. Decision rule: No contract until closed. Required artifact: Written vendor commitment in the agreement. Timing: Before signature.
* Conditional. Decision rule: Proceed with named control. Required artifact: Control description, owner, review date. Timing: Before go-live.
* Unresolved. Decision rule: Hold item open with owner. Required artifact: Dated response from vendor. Timing: Second round of review.
One caution about badges. A framework reference, a trust page, a logo wall or a questionnaire response is not a tested configuration of your tenant. Where a vendor cites an audit or assessment, ask for the scope statement, the period covered, the systems in scope, who performed it, and whether the system you are buying is inside that scope. Many assessments cover a corporate environment while the product you are evaluating sits outside it. Private deployment, by itself, does not establish security or legal compliance. Configuration, testing and documented responsibilities do, and they have to be verified for your specific environment.

Questions buyers ask during private AI due diligence
### Which documents should an AI provider supply before signing?
At minimum: an architecture and data flow diagram naming every service and provider in the path; a subprocessor list with function, region and retention; a responsibility matrix showing which party performs and which party approves each operating task; an access control description covering administrator roles and end-user entitlements; a written statement on training use of your data; an acceptance test plan with pass criteria; a change management process; and an exit schedule covering export formats, transition support and deletion evidence. If a vendor cannot produce these, ask when they will exist and who will own them, then record the answer as an open item rather than accepting a verbal assurance.
### What is the difference between owning hardware and owning the solution?
Hardware ownership determines where computation happens and who controls the physical and network environment. Solution ownership is a separate bundle: rights to your data and derived artifacts, rights to custom code and configuration, license terms for models and software, and control of credentials and accounts. A vendor can own the servers while you own the data, or you can own the servers while the orchestration layer remains vendor property. Both arrangements can work. Problems start when a proposal uses hardware ownership as shorthand for all four, so ask for the four answers separately.
### How should security badges or compliance claims be verified?
Treat a badge as a pointer to a document, then read the document. Ask for the scope statement and the period covered, confirm the product you are buying is inside that scope, and identify who performed the assessment. Separate a framework the vendor consulted from an assessment someone performed. Voluntary guidance such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework is a structure for managing risk rather than a certification ([NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework)), so "aligned to" claims should be followed by which functions were applied and what they produced. Then test the control yourself where you can, because a configuration test on your tenant outranks a document.
### How should a small team adapt this approach?
Reduce the scope, not the standard. A small team can run a compressed version in two sessions: one to collect the data flow map, subprocessor list and responsibility matrix, and one to run a thirty-item acceptance test on real material with pass criteria written beforehand. Keep the exit schedule in full, because exit terms are the hardest thing to negotiate after the relationship is established and the cheapest thing to add before it starts. If internal expertise is thin, limit the first engagement to a single workload with low data sensitivity and use it to test the vendor's operating behavior before expanding scope.
### What evidence would change the recommended approach?
Several things. If a vendor provides a tested, documented retrieval permission model with evidence from your own entitlement structure, the weight placed on storage location falls, because the practical risk moves to identity. If the workload involves no regulated or confidential data and no connected actions, much of the identity and action review can be scaled back. If a vendor's own accounts hold all credentials but the contract includes a tested, time-boxed export and transition process with evidence from a prior migration, the credential finding can move from must-have to conditional. The method is designed to be adjusted by evidence. What should not change is requiring the evidence before the signature rather than after.
