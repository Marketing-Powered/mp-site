Competitive Intelligence Services With Sources and Strategic Context
Most competitive decisions are made from a slide nobody can trace: a screenshot with no date, a revenue figure with no method, a deck recycled from a market that has since moved. Competitive intelligence services are only worth buying when every claim carries a source, a retrieval date, and a visible line between what was observed and what someone inferred. That discipline is the point of this work.

Define the competitive set around the real buyer decision
A competitive set is not the list of names your sales team repeats in meetings. It is the set of options a buyer actually weighs, which usually includes direct rivals, substitutes, in-house alternatives and the decision to do nothing for another two quarters. For a multi-location healthcare group, that may mean a national telehealth brand, a regional hospital service line and a primary care referral pattern sitting in the same consideration set.
Inclusion criteria get written down before research starts: who qualifies, on what evidence, and why anyone obvious was excluded. Written criteria are what let a leadership team argue with the scope instead of arguing with the conclusions later.
Specify the comparison dimensions before collecting anything
Comparison without fixed axes becomes a highlight reel of whatever was easiest to find. The dimensions set in advance typically cover offer and scope, buyer fit and segment, channel presence, proof and credibility signals, pricing structure where it is published, and operating model, including locations, staffing model and intake or sales process.
Fixing the axes first makes competitor benchmarking comparable across entrants and keeps the research from drifting toward whichever competitor has the loudest marketing. It also exposes gaps honestly: if a rival's pricing is not public, the matrix says so rather than filling the cell with a guess. More on how research is structured sits in our [research methods and resources](/resources/research).
Collect evidence from public, licensed or authorized sources
Collection is limited to public, licensed or authorized sources, each recorded with a URL or document reference and the date it was retrieved. Ad transparency libraries, filings, job postings, published pricing, review platforms, conference materials and authorized interviews all qualify. Gated competitor systems, misrepresented identity and credential sharing do not. The Strategic and Competitive Intelligence Professionals association maintains published ethics guidance for the field, which is the standard this work follows: [SCIP ethics](https://scip.world/ethics/).
Dates matter as much as sources, because a competitor's landing page from eight months ago supports a different argument than one captured last week. The same evidence logic applies downstream to how inquiries are judged, covered in [measuring lead quality](/resources/lead-quality).
Separate what you observed from what you believe it means
Three things get labeled separately in every deliverable. Observation: a competitor added a second intake number and began running video creative in two metros on a given date. Interpretation: this is consistent with a market test, with stated confidence and at least one alternative explanation. Unknown: whether spend increased, since spend is modeled, not measured, unless a licensed source reports it.
Competitor claims are recorded as claims. A company asserting it is the largest provider in a state is evidence of positioning, not evidence of share. Keeping those categories apart is what makes a competitive landscape analysis survive a board question. Published work follows the same convention in our [published research hub](/research).
Translate findings into defensible choices
Intelligence that stops at a findings deck transfers no risk. The output of competitive analysis consulting should be a short set of options: where the position is genuinely defensible, where a rival's move deserves a response and where it deserves deliberate patience, what pricing or packaging change the evidence supports, and which questions are still too thin to act on.
Each option carries the evidence behind it and the condition that would change the recommendation. Flagging the weak areas is part of the deliverable, not a failure of it. Related methodology appears in our [published studies and their evidence](/research/studies).
Keep the picture current with refresh triggers
A one-time snapshot is not monitoring, and presenting it as monitoring is how teams end up responding to a competitor move six months after the market absorbed it. Refresh triggers are defined with the scope: a pricing change, a new entrant or acquisition, a channel shift, a regulatory change affecting the category, or an internal trigger such as a planned launch or budget cycle.
Each refresh re-dates the evidence rather than appending to an undated file. What this is not, and how a scope gets built
Buyers often conflate three different products. A keyword-gap report is useful for content planning and tells you little about positioning. Scraped or gated data is an ethics and legal exposure, not an advantage. An unverified revenue estimate presented as fact is the single most common reason a competitive deck collapses under questioning.
Established intelligence firms separate these functions deliberately; Fuld & Company distinguishes competitor analysis, pricing intelligence and sales enablement intelligence as different services ([Fuld & Company](https://www.fuld.com/services/competitive-market-intelligence/)), and Teknicks treats market sizing, competition, trends, win-loss and market-entry research as distinct research types ([Teknicks](https://www.teknicks.com/services/research/market-research/)). Scoping starts the same way here: one decision, one question, defined before any collection begins.

FAQ
Q: Will the research include competitors beyond the obvious names?
A: Yes, when the buyer decision supports it. Substitutes, adjacent entrants and in-house alternatives are included or excluded against written criteria, and the exclusions are documented alongside the inclusions so the scope can be challenged before conclusions are drawn.
Q: Which sources and collection methods are permitted?
A: Public, licensed and authorized sources only, with recorded retrieval dates. Unauthorized access to competitor systems, misrepresentation of identity and credential sharing are excluded, consistent with published industry ethics guidance. Where a source is licensed, the license terms govern how the material can be reused internally.
Q: How do you avoid treating competitor claims as verified facts?
A: Claims are logged as claims, with the source and date. Anything modeled, such as spend or share, is labeled as an estimate with its method and confidence stated. Where evidence conflicts, both readings appear rather than one being quietly dropped.
Q: What information and access are needed to scope this work?
A: The decision you are trying to make, your current view of the competitive set, any pricing or packaging constraints, and the internal audience who must accept the conclusion. No protected health information or personal data is required or requested for competitor research.
Q: What will our organization own and be able to export at the end?
A: Scope, ownership, retention, hosting and access are documented in writing before work begins, and the evidence matrix with its source links is built to be exported and read without specialist software. Those terms are confirmed in scoping rather than assumed. Bring one decision you need to defend, and we will work out whether the evidence exists to settle it.
