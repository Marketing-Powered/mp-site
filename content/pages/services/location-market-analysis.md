Location Market Analysis Before Opening the Next Site
Most expansion decisions get made with whatever market data was easiest to pull: a demographics export, a competitor count from a map search, a regional report written for somebody else's business. A location market analysis replaces that with one comparison method applied identically to every candidate site, so the number in the lease approval deck can be traced back to a dated source or labeled as an assumption.

Define the actual catchment before counting anything
Every figure downstream depends on the boundary you draw, so the boundary is the first decision, not an afterthought. An outpatient clinic that draws from a 20 minute drive is a different geography than a mobile service with a dispatch radius, a destination specialty practice that draws statewide, or a professional services firm whose real catchment is an industry concentration rather than a neighborhood. Pick the boundary type that matches how customers actually reach the service: drive time, service radius, delivery zone, or referral territory. Then hold it constant. Trade area analysis built on ZIP codes deserves particular care, since ZIP Code Tabulation Areas approximate postal service areas and exist to present statistical data, not to describe how far anyone will travel ([Census ZIP Code Tabulation Areas](https://hub.arcgis.com/datasets/2303df6b82b94c41b14f272f5975c18c_0)).
Build comparable evidence across every candidate site
Comparability fails on dates more often than on data quality. American Community Survey 1 year estimates are published only for geographies of 65,000 people or more, while 5 year estimates cover all geographies down to the block group ([U.S. Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2020/acs-5-year.html)). Pull a 1 year figure for a metro site and a 5 year figure for a suburban one and the comparison is already broken. The same discipline applies to business-side inputs such as County Business Patterns, which reports establishments, employment and payroll annually down to the ZIP level ([U.S. Census Bureau, County Business Patterns](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/2017-county-business-patterns)). Record the vintage, the geography and the source for every cell, and add your own first-party geography: where existing customers, referrals and inquiries already come from. That last layer is usually the most predictive and the most overlooked. Map competition and the substitutes buyers actually use
Counting competitors inside a circle is not competitive analysis. What matters is observable footprint: how many locations a competitor operates inside your catchment, which services they publish, their hours and access, their local search presence, their review volume and recency, and whether they appear to be adding or consolidating sites. Substitutes count too. A regional hospital outpatient department, a telehealth provider, a national franchise and an in-house team can all absorb the demand you are modeling. Record what is visible and verifiable, and mark capacity estimates as estimates, because staffing levels and utilization are rarely public. Local search visibility is an observable competitive signal and a later execution lever, covered in [Multi Location SEO Services](/services/multi-location-seo-services).
Test operational feasibility, not just market attractiveness
A market can look strong and still be unworkable. Site selection market research should account for the labor pool for the specific roles you need, licensure and credentialing timelines where applicable, the commute radius your staff will accept, delivery and supply logistics, parking and transit access, hours the site can realistically operate, and your organization's own capacity to open and support another location without degrading the existing ones. Regulatory and platform constraints belong here as well, since some services face advertising restrictions that shape how demand can be reached at all. This is where a site visit and a hiring conversation outperform a spreadsheet. A location feasibility study that ignores staffing has simply moved the risk to opening day. Methodology and published work sit in the [published research hub](/research).
Separate observed evidence from assumed reachable demand
Public data describes a place, not an appetite for your offer. Census tools support research into residents, businesses and local economies, but their vintages and geographic boundaries do not measure demand for a specific service ([Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/topics/business-economy/small-business/about/powerful-data.html)). Market sizing is commonly framed around entry, investment, planning and capacity decisions, and still requires independently sourced calculation rather than one headline total ([Douglas Insights](https://www.douglasinsights.com/consulting/market-sizing/)). So the scorecard carries two visible classes of cell: observed values with a source and a date, and modeled values with a stated assumption, a range and a sensitivity test. If the ranking flips when you move one assumption by 20 percent, the comparison is telling you to go collect more evidence. Documented methods are described in [research methods and resources](/resources/research).
Make the decision usable for the people approving it
The output is a decision document, not a data dump. It should show the weighted comparison, the tradeoffs each site carries, the gaps that evidence could not close, and the validation steps worth taking before signing anything. Growth research is most useful when it feeds an investment choice rather than producing disconnected geographic summaries ([Fuld](https://www.fuld.com/services/business-strategy-and-foresight/)). Your organization keeps the inventory, the scorecard and the assumption log, so the next expansion starts from a method rather than a blank page. FAQ
Q: How do you define a trade area for different businesses?
A: By how customers actually reach the service. Walk-in and appointment-based locations usually justify a drive-time boundary. Mobile and delivery businesses use a dispatch or route radius constrained by service time. Referral-driven and professional services often need a territory defined by referral sources or industry concentration rather than residential geography. The boundary is documented once and applied to every candidate so local market analysis stays comparable.
Q: Can demographic data prove demand for our service?
A: No. It can establish who lives and works inside a boundary and which businesses operate there, which is why Census tools are a sound starting point ([Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/topics/business-economy/small-business/about/powerful-data.html)). Demand for a specific offer is inferred, and the inference should be labeled, bounded and tested. First-party evidence from existing locations, inquiry geography and referral patterns is the strongest available correction.
Q: How do you compare several locations fairly?
A: Same boundary logic, same data vintages, same categories, same weights, applied before anyone sees the results. Weights are set with your leadership team up front so the scoring reflects your economics rather than being tuned after the fact to confirm a preferred site.
Q: What information and access are needed to scope this work?
A: The candidate list and the rationale behind it, your current location footprint and performance context, customer or patient geography at whatever aggregation your policies allow, staffing and capacity constraints, and the decision timeline and approval process. No identifiable personal or health information is needed for this analysis, and data handling, retention and access boundaries are agreed before any transfer.
Q: What will our organization own and be able to export at the end?
