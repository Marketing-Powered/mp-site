Location market analysis before opening the next site
Most expansion decisions rely on whatever market data is easiest to pull: a demographics export, a map count of nearby competitors, or a regional report written for another business. A location market analysis replaces those snapshots with one comparison method applied identically to every candidate site. Every figure in the lease approval deck can then be traced back to a dated source or clearly labeled as an assumption.

Define the actual catchment before counting anything
Every figure downstream depends on the boundary you draw, so boundary selection comes first rather than as an afterthought. An outpatient clinic drawing patients from a 20-minute drive operates inside a different geography than a mobile service with a dispatch radius, a destination specialty practice drawing statewide, or a professional services office anchored to an industry corridor.
Pick the boundary type that matches how customers, patients, or referring providers actually reach the service: drive time, service radius, transit corridor, or referral territory. Document that rule once and hold it constant across every candidate market.
Trade area analysis built solely on ZIP codes creates distortion because U.S. Census Bureau ZIP Code Tabulation Areas approximate postal routes for statistical reporting rather than measuring travel behavior. Comparing an urban medical corridor against a suburban commuter market requires boundaries grounded in real access patterns.
Build comparable evidence across every candidate site
Comparability fails on dates more often than on data quality. U.S. Census Bureau American Community Survey 1-year estimates cover geographies of 65,000 people or more, while 5-year estimates cover smaller geographies down to the block group.
Pulling a 1-year figure for a metro candidate and a 5-year figure for a suburban town breaks the comparison before scoring starts. The same vintage discipline applies to business-side indicators such as U.S. Census Bureau County Business Patterns, which reports establishments, employment, and annual payroll down to the ZIP level.
Record the vintage, geography, and source for every cell, then layer in first-party geography showing where existing customers, referrals, and inquiries already originate. That internal layer is often the most predictive and the most overlooked. For multi-site operators already running local campaigns, that evidence connects directly to [paid media](/services/paid-media/) planning measured by conversion value per cost, where higher is better.
Map competition and the substitutes buyers actually use
Counting pins inside a circle is not competitive analysis. What matters is observable footprint: how many locations each competitor runs inside the catchment, published service lines, operating hours, appointment access, local search visibility, review volume and recency, and whether competitors are adding or consolidating sites.
Substitutes absorb demand just as directly as named peers. A regional hospital outpatient department, a telehealth provider, a national multi-site chain, or an in-house team can all capture the same buyer before they ever compare local options.
Record what is visible and verifiable, and label capacity estimates clearly as estimates because staffing rosters and utilization rates are rarely public. Local organic presence also acts as an observable competitive signal today and a launch lever later, tying site selection directly to [technical SEO and site performance](/services/web-development/technical-seo-and-performance/) priorities.
Test operational feasibility, not just market attractiveness
A market can look strong on paper and still be unworkable once doors open. Site selection market research should evaluate the local labor pool for required clinical or specialist roles, licensure and credentialing timelines, realistic staff commute distances, parking and transit access, supply logistics, and practical operating hours.
Internal readiness belongs on the same scorecard. Executive teams need a candid view of whether current managers, intake staff, and clinical leaders can open and support another facility without degrading performance at existing locations.
Regulatory and platform constraints belong here too, because restricted categories shape acquisition economics from day one. In Google's sensitive healthcare vertical, retargeting is not permitted, and LegitScript-aware and HIPAA-aware compliance requirements affect how local demand can be reached. On-site walkthroughs and local hiring conversations routinely uncover operational friction that demographic tables miss, preventing organizations from moving staffing risk to opening day.
Separate observed evidence from assumed reachable demand
Public data describes a place, not an appetite for a specific service. Census datasets help teams study residents, employers, and local economies, yet population counts and geographic boundaries do not measure active demand for a particular clinical or professional offer.
Market sizing for entry, capital allocation, and capacity decisions still requires independently sourced calculations rather than one top-down headline total. To prevent false precision, every location scorecard separates two visible classes of cells.
Observed cells display an exact value, primary source, and publication date. Modeled cells display a stated assumption, a reasonable range, and a sensitivity test. If shifting one demand or conversion assumption by 20 percent flips the top-ranked site, the comparison is signaling that leadership needs more field evidence before signing a lease.
Make the decision usable for the people approving it
Finance, operations, and clinical leaders need a decision document, not a raw spreadsheet dump. An executive review sheet should present the weighted comparison, the tradeoffs each site carries, the gaps that public evidence could not close, and the specific field checks worth completing before signing anything.
Growth research earns its keep when it feeds a capital allocation vote instead of producing disconnected geographic summaries. Retaining the data inventory, weighted scorecard, and assumption log inside your organization gives leadership a repeatable method for the next expansion rather than starting from a blank page.

FAQ
Q: How do you define the geographic boundary for a candidate site?
A: The boundary is set by how customers or patients actually reach the service. Walk-in and appointment-based locations usually justify a drive-time boundary, mobile and dispatch businesses use a route radius constrained by service time, and referral-driven or professional practices often map territories by referral sources or industry concentration. That boundary is documented once and applied identically across every candidate so local market comparisons remain consistent.
Q: Can public demographic data measure actual demand for our services?
A: No. Public data establishes who lives and works inside a boundary and which businesses operate there, making U.S. Census Bureau datasets a reliable starting point rather than a demand forecast. Demand for a specific offer is inferred, so every inference is labeled, bounded with a range, and tested. First-party evidence from existing locations, inquiry geography, and referral patterns is the strongest available correction.
Q: How do you keep site comparisons fair across different markets?
A: Every candidate uses the same boundary logic, the same data vintages, the same scoring categories, and the same weights before anyone sees the final results. Weights are set with your leadership team up front so the scorecard reflects your actual economics rather than being tuned after the fact to confirm a preferred site.
Q: What information is needed from our team to evaluate candidate locations?
A: The analysis draws on the candidate list and site rationale, current location footprint and performance context, customer or patient geography at whatever aggregation your privacy policies allow, staffing and capacity constraints, and your approval timeline. No identifiable personal or health information is needed, and data handling, retention, and access boundaries are agreed before any transfer. Marketing Powered maintains HIPAA-aware workflows and owned, local AI infrastructure for HIPAA-aware inference when analytical work involves sensitive operational contexts.
Q: How is the analysis organized for executive review and future sites?
A: The work focuses on leaving your leadership team with transparent, editable records: the dated geographic data inventory, the weighted comparison scorecard, the documented assumptions with sensitivity ranges, and the named research gaps.
