Peptide Marketing Claims Review Across Copy, Imagery and Customer Journeys
Peptide brands publish claims faster than they can document the evidence behind them, and the record of who approved what usually does not exist. A peptide marketing claims review fixes the traceability problem first: every express and implied claim in a defined asset set, mapped to its evidence, its gaps and a named approver.

Define the asset set before the review begins
A claims review is only useful when its boundary is written down. Scoping starts with an inventory: which site pages, paid search and paid social ads, product detail copy, email sequences, video scripts, creator and affiliate briefs, and sales or practitioner-facing decks are included, and which are explicitly out of scope for this pass. The FTC treats advertising broadly, including statements on packaging and labeling, promotional materials, internet and digital content, social media and influencer marketing, press interviews, trade shows, and promotion made indirectly through healthcare practitioners [FTC health products compliance guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance). Surfaces excluded from scope do not become safe. They stay unreviewed, and the scope document says so plainly.
Extract express and implied claims, not just the wording
Most teams review sentences. Regulators review impressions. The FTC advises that marketers should not focus narrowly on individual phrases but should assess the net impression conveyed by all elements of the ad, including text, product name, charts, graphs and other images, and that when an ad lends itself to more than one reasonable interpretation, the advertiser is responsible for substantiating each interpretation [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance). That is why extraction covers before-and-after imagery, study graphics, body-composition visuals, testimonial framing, product naming and adjacent category cues. Each extracted claim is logged as express or implied, with the asset, timestamp and the specific element that conveys it. Teams running variant-heavy creative should align this work with [creative testing](/services/creative-testing) so new winners enter review rather than bypass it.
Match every claim to the evidence that actually supports it
Extraction without evidence mapping is just a list. Each claim is matched to the specific support offered for it, then annotated for fit: species and population studied, dose and formulation, duration, endpoint, and whether the research examined the finished product or a different preparation. The FTC notes that a marketer should confirm underlying research is relevant to the advertiser's product, for example, that the dosage and formulation are comparable, and should examine research that contradicts as well as supports the position [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance). Where the match is partial or absent, the matrix records the gap and flags the claim for qualified review rather than resolving it internally. Governance patterns for that handoff are covered in [healthcare content marketing and clinical review](/resources/healthcare-content-marketing).
Prioritize corrections by exposure, not by editing difficulty
Not every gap carries the same weight. Findings sort into working tiers: unsupported promises that imply treatment, prevention or disease outcomes; claims that may establish drug intended use; missing material context such as safety or limitation disclosures; stale references, expired studies and outdated regulatory language; and wording that is defensible but imprecise. Priority also factors live spend, so a claim running in a high-budget ad group outranks the same claim sitting on an archived blog post. Sequencing matters because before disseminating an ad, advertisers must have adequate substantiation for all objective product claims conveyed, expressly or by implication [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance). The [healthcare marketing compliance checklist](/resources/healthcare-marketing-compliance-checklist) supports the remediation pass.
Build an approval record that names a decision owner
An approval that lives in a Slack thread is not an approval. Each reviewed asset gets a record: claim set, supporting evidence reference, the correction applied, the named individual who approved it, their role, the version and date, and the publication limits attached. Limits matter because the same sentence can be acceptable on an educational page and unacceptable in a conversion ad. For advertisers who also face platform and certification gates, this record is what you hand to counsel, to a reviewer, or into a [LegitScript certification](/resources/legitscript-certification) process, instead of reassembling history from memory.
If the asset list is ready and you want the scope defined in writing, [book a scoping call](/get-started/book-a-call).
Maintain the approved library as creative changes weekly
Approvals expire because evidence, product formulation and policy all move. Maintenance defines the shelf life of each approved claim, the change-request route for new creative, and the triggers for re-review: reformulation, new study publication, a supplier or label change, a platform policy update, an enforcement action in the category, or a creator going off-script. In practice the library sits next to the ad account, so paid teams pull approved language rather than writing fresh copy at launch. Brands running continuous acquisition should connect it to their [healthcare paid media](/services/paid-media) workflow, where new ad variants are the most common source of unreviewed claims.
Where this review stops and someone else decides
This is an evidence-mapping and documentation service. It does not replace medical, legal or regulatory review, and it does not determine product status. Intended use is the deciding question under FDA rules: claims stated in labeling, advertising, on the internet or in other promotional materials can establish a product as a drug because the intended use is to treat or prevent disease or otherwise affect the structure or functions of the human body, and consumer perception and well-known therapeutic ingredients can also establish intended use [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetics-laws-regulations/it-cosmetic-drug-or-both-or-it-soap). FAQ
Q: Do you review implied claims in images and video?
A: Yes. Imagery, before-and-after sequences, charts, product names and on-screen text are extracted as claims in their own right, because the FTC assesses the net impression conveyed by all elements of the ad [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance). Video is reviewed by segment so a visual claim is tied to the timecode where it appears.
Q: Does ingredient research support every finished-product claim?
A: Often it does not. The relevant test is whether the research is applicable to the advertised product, including comparable dosage and formulation, and whether contradicting research exists [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance). The matrix records that distinction instead of letting an ingredient study stand in for finished-product support.
Q: Who makes the medical or legal determination?
A: Your qualified reviewer or counsel does. The review produces the structured input they need: the claim, where it appears, the evidence offered, and the open question. Deciding product status, permitted language and regulatory exposure stays with licensed professionals in your organization.
Q: Can a disclaimer cure an unsupported promise?
A: Not reliably. The FTC states that a disclosure should not be contradicted or mitigated by, or inconsistent with, anything else in the ad, that hyperlinked disclosures in interactive media are avoidable, and that if a significant minority of consumers take a misleading claim from an ad despite a disclosure, the disclosure isn't effective [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance). Fixing the claim usually beats adding fine print.
Q: What does the review deliver?
A: A defined asset inventory, a claims-to-evidence matrix separating express from implied claims, a prioritized correction list, and a versioned approval log with named owners and expiration triggers.
