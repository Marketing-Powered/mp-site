Reputation Management for Peptide Businesses
Peptide clinics and consumer brands are judged on review activity like any local business, but the category carries extra weight. One public reply can expose private care details or turn a customer anecdote into an unsupported health claim, and one poorly designed review request can create a federal problem. Peptide reputation management works when it is run as an operating system for feedback rather than a campaign to lift a number.

Audit the feedback you already have
The first pass is inventory, not strategy. Every existing review, survey reply, support ticket, social comment and direct message gets sorted into three buckets: service experience, product or efficacy claims, and privacy-sensitive detail. Someone who understands the clinical or product side reads the second and third buckets, because those are where liability sits. Prior public replies are read with the same discipline, since a reply posted two years ago is still live. The output is a single list with themes, counts, unresolved items and any response already published that needs review. That list, not a competitor rating comparison, drives every later decision in peptide clinic review management.
Design review collection that stays sentiment neutral
Collection design is where most peptide brand customer feedback programs break. Google does not allow merchants to offer incentives for a review or for the revision or removal of a negative one, to discourage or prohibit negative reviews, to selectively solicit positive reviews, to pressure users to post while on the premises, to request specific review content, or to set staff review quotas ([Google Maps user generated content policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114?hl=en)). The FTC rule, effective October 21, 2024, separately reaches compensation or incentives conditioned on reviews expressing a particular sentiment, and authorizes civil penalties for knowing violations ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers)). Asking only customers you believe are happy is not specifically barred by that rule, but the FTC notes it can still violate the FTC Act. The practical answer is one neutral request, sent to everyone, with no scripting. General method is covered in the [reputation management agency guide](/services/reputation-management-agency).
Set response standards before anyone replies publicly
A public reply is a disclosure decision, not a customer service task. The standing rule for clinics is simple: do not confirm that the reviewer is or was a patient, do not reference diagnosis, treatment, products dispensed or visit history, and do not repeat or amplify a health claim the business cannot substantiate. HHS Office for Civil Rights reached a $30,000 settlement with a New Jersey psychiatric practice in June 2023 over protected health information disclosed in responses to negative online reviews ([HHS OCR](https://www.hhs.gov/about/news/2023/06/05/hhs-office-civil-rights-reaches-agreement-health-care-provider-new-jersey-disclosed-phi-response-negative-online-reviews.html)). A usable peptide review response strategy gives staff three or four approved reply patterns, a named offline contact path, and an explicit list of words never used in public. Adjacent response discipline is covered in [reputation management for doctors](/resources/reputation-management-for-doctors).
Route complaints to the owner who can resolve them
Responding and resolving are different jobs with different owners. Clinical complaints and reported adverse experiences go to the clinical lead on a documented timeline. Service, billing and scheduling issues go to operations. Product quality issues such as lot, labeling, shipping or storage concerns go to the quality owner. Anything touching records, identity or privacy goes to the designated privacy contact. The public reply stays generic while the internal investigation runs. The FTC confirms the rule does not stop a business from contacting a negative reviewer to resolve the reported issue, but it does prohibit unfounded legal threats and intimidation used to get a review removed ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers)). Every item is logged with owner, date and resolution state, using the structure in the [healthcare marketing compliance checklist](/resources/healthcare-marketing-compliance-checklist).
Govern testimonial reuse before feedback becomes marketing
Feedback changes legal character the moment it moves into advertising. The FTC is direct about this: when consumer reviews are featured in advertising or marketing materials they become testimonials, and the exemption for mere review hosting no longer applies ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers)). So reuse requires written permission from the person, a claim review that strips outcome language an anecdote cannot support, and a record of both. For clinics, the HIPAA Privacy Rule generally requires an individual's written authorization before protected health information is used for marketing, subject to limited defined exceptions ([HHS](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/marketing/index.html)). Certification status is a separate track from feedback governance; the requirements are outlined in the [LegitScript certification overview](/resources/legitscript-certification).
Report what improved, not a rating forecast
Reporting covers what the business actually controls: request volume sent to all customers, response rate, time to first reply, the share of published replies that pass a standards audit, themes resolved, themes that keep returning, and the inventory of permissioned testimonials with their documentation. No rating projections appear in it, because ratings are the output of real experiences and platform moderation. FAQ
Q: Can you promise a higher star rating?
A: No. Ratings follow genuine customer experience and platform moderation, and Google removes incentivized or biased reviews. What is controllable is request consistency, response speed and quality, and whether real issues get resolved.
Q: Can negative reviews be removed on demand?
A: No. Removal is a platform decision based on its own policies. The FTC rule prohibits unfounded legal threats and intimidation aimed at getting a review removed, and notes that paying consumers to change or remove truthful negative reviews may be an unfair or deceptive practice ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumer-reviews-testimonials-rule-questions-answers)).
Q: How should clinics respond without confirming patient status?
A: Use a neutral reply that references general policies and an offline contact path, with no acknowledgment that the reviewer received care and no clinical detail. OCR has stated that disclosing patient information in response to negative reviews is not permitted ([HHS OCR](https://www.hhs.gov/about/news/2023/06/05/hhs-office-civil-rights-reaches-agreement-health-care-provider-new-jersey-disclosed-phi-response-negative-online-reviews.html)).
Q: Can customer feedback be reused in ads?
A: Only with documented permission and a claim review first, since featuring a review in advertising converts it into a testimonial under the FTC rule. For covered entities, marketing use of protected health information generally requires written authorization ([HHS](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/marketing/index.html)).
