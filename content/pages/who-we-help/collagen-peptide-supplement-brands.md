Marketing for Collagen Peptide Supplement Brands
Collagen peptide marketing is a consumer packaged goods problem governed by dietary supplement law and ecommerce channel policy, not a healthcare intake problem. Growth depends on settling three questions before spend scales: what the product legally is, what the evidence supports, and which channels will carry it.

Verify the product category before you buy any media
The word collagen is not proof of status. Before a brand is treated as a lawful dietary supplement inside a media plan, someone sits down with the paperwork: the manufacturer specification, the certificate of analysis, the ingredient deck including carriers and flavorings, and the claims printed on the panel. Congress defined the term in the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994. Dietary ingredients include vitamins and minerals, herbs and other botanicals, amino acids, dietary substances that are part of the food supply, and concentrates or extracts of those, and a new dietary ingredient is one not marketed in the United States before October 15, 1994 ([FDA supplement Q&A](https://www.fda.gov/food/information-consumers-using-dietary-supplements/questions-and-answers-dietary-supplements)). The definition generally excludes ingredients approved as new drugs, licensed as biologics or authorized for clinical investigation under an IND that has gone into effect, unless previously marketed as a supplement or food ([FDA supplement Q&A](https://www.fda.gov/food/information-consumers-using-dietary-supplements/questions-and-answers-dietary-supplements)). That exclusion is one reason status is confirmed ingredient by ingredient rather than assumed from the product name. Certain new dietary ingredients also require notification to FDA at least 75 days before introduction into interstate commerce ([FDA supplement Q&A](https://www.fda.gov/food/information-consumers-using-dietary-supplements/questions-and-answers-dietary-supplements)). Qualified counsel reaches these conclusions. Marketing reads the output.
* Product status. Consumer dietary supplement: Meets the dietary ingredient definition and is labeled a supplement. Prescription drug: Regulated as a drug, dispensed under prescription. Research-only compound: Unresolved until counsel confirms it.
* Claim standard. Consumer dietary supplement: Substantiated structure and function claims with the required disclaimer. Prescription drug: Approved labeling. Research-only compound: No consumer benefit claims.
* Channel question. Consumer dietary supplement: Product eligibility plus claim review before listing. Prescription drug: Pharmacy certification pathways. Research-only compound: Assume ineligible.
* Economics. Consumer dietary supplement: Repeat purchase and contribution margin. Prescription drug: Outside consumer ecommerce. Research-only compound: Outside consumer ecommerce.

Build positioning the formulation can actually carry
Positioning fails when the hero claim outruns the evidence file for that formulation. Advertising for supplements is regulated by the Federal Trade Commission, while FDA is generally responsible for safety, quality and labeling ([FDA supplement Q&A](https://www.fda.gov/food/information-consumers-using-dietary-supplements/questions-and-answers-dietary-supplements)). Health benefit and safety claims require competent and reliable scientific evidence, and as a general matter that means randomized, controlled human clinical testing ([FTC health products compliance guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)). Hedging does not rescue a thin claim. FTC guidance treats vague qualifiers as inadequate, noting it is not enough to say a product "may" have the claimed benefit or "helps" achieve it, and that consumers read modifiers such as promising or preliminary as positive attributes rather than as limits on the science ([FTC health products compliance guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)). A firm making a structure and function claim must have substantiation that it is truthful and not misleading, and the label carries the required FDA disclaimer ([FDA supplement Q&A](https://www.fda.gov/food/information-consumers-using-dietary-supplements/questions-and-answers-dietary-supplements)). Write the claim ladder first, then test message and layout against it through [conversion rate optimization](/services/conversion-rate-optimization) instead of reaching for stronger language when conversion stalls.
Review each channel on its own eligibility terms
Channel permission is a separate gate from legal status. Under Google's healthcare and medicines policy, some products cannot be listed regardless of any claims of legality, including:
* items on its non-exhaustive list of unapproved pharmaceuticals and supplements
* herbal and dietary supplements with active pharmaceutical or dangerous ingredients
* products that imply they are as effective as prescription drugs or controlled substances
* non-government approved products marketed as safe or effective for preventing, curing or treating a disease
* products that have been subject to any government or regulatory action or warning
That list comes from the [Google Merchant Center healthcare and medicines policy](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/6150151?hl=en). Certification is a different question. Pharmacy programs such as LegitScript, NABP VIPPS and the Pharmacy Verified Websites Program apply to online pharmacies listing prescription drugs, and in the United States no certification is required for listing over-the-counter medication ([Google Merchant Center](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/6150151?hl=en)). Geography matters as well. Google does not allow promotion of dietetic products or supplements in Mexico, or of online dietary supplement sales in Russia ([Google Merchant Center](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/6150151?hl=en)). Each platform publishes its own advertising policies, so scope [paid media](/services/paid-media) per channel rather than running one blanket supplement playbook. This work is United States focused.
Make the product page explain composition and purchase terms
The product page is where a collagen ecommerce strategy earns the order or buys a refund. Four blocks carry the weight: what is in the tub, what the evidence shows and does not show, what the customer agrees to pay, and how reviews were collected. Label content is already specified. A Supplement Facts panel lists serving size and servings per container and declares each dietary ingredient with amounts, an "other ingredients" list covers what is not in the panel, and labels must give a domestic address or phone number for reporting serious adverse events ([FDA supplement Q&A](https://www.fda.gov/food/information-consumers-using-dietary-supplements/questions-and-answers-dietary-supplements)). Mirror that detail on the page rather than hiding it behind a lightbox, and put subscription terms, billing cadence and the cancellation path in the offer block instead of footer policy text. Review displays sit inside an explicit federal rule that prohibits fake or false reviews and testimonials, incentives conditioned on a particular sentiment, undisclosed insider reviews, misrepresenting a company-controlled site as independent, and suppressing negative reviews through unfounded legal threats ([FTC final rule on consumer reviews](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials)). The habits in the [healthcare marketing compliance checklist](/resources/healthcare-marketing-compliance-checklist) transfer here, with the claim rules swapped for the supplement standard.
Measure repeat value and contribution margin, not order count
Treat this as a working assumption rather than a measured fact: repeat purchase usually decides whether acquisition pays back, so first-order ROAS is a weak control variable. Reporting that can govern budget separates new customer orders from repeat orders, follows each cohort through refunds, chargebacks, churn and discount depth, and reports contribution margin after cost of goods, fulfillment, fees and media. Build it on first-party order and subscription data joined to channel, not on platform-reported conversions alone. The founder's background is relevant to that discipline: a former healthcare CMO who managed $1.5 to $2M per month in Google Ads across 11+ behavioral and mental health accounts, with attribution tracked to admission rather than to lead. The same principle applies here. Judge acquisition by what the customer is worth after the first order, not by the checkout event. Channel eligibility belongs in the model too, because a disapproval or suspension can remove a revenue line. Model that downside explicitly. Scope the engagement by who owns each decision
Write the scope as a list of owners, not a list of deliverables. Four questions decide whether it works: who signs off on claim language, who maintains the product feed, who owns analytics and the definition of a qualified order, and who holds the evidence file for each formulation. The defensible split is that regulatory and legal conclusions stay with the brand and its qualified counsel, the evidence file stays with the formulator, and the agency owns creative, media, catalog hygiene, measurement and the discipline of refusing to publish a claim the file cannot support. Before work starts, a serious collagen supplement marketing agency asks to see the product and ingredient review, the formulation-specific claims evidence, a contribution-margin worksheet, and the approved product pages. Ask any agency how it reviews a claim before launch, how it handles a disapproval, and what it will refuse to run.
