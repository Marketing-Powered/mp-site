Built for Hospitals and Health Systems
A hospital marketing plan has to hold several things at once: distinct service lines with different demand and capacity, locations with their own access points, and internal teams with their own review responsibilities. Our operating model is built for that shape, which is why the homepage describes us as [built for hospitals and health systems](/#hospitals).
We are direct about what that means. We do not publish hospital client names, engagement results, or logos on this page. Where an approved case exists, it appears on our [client results](/results/case-studies) page with its scope, dates, baseline, and limitations. Until then, this page explains the approach so your team can evaluate fit.
Our founder's prior executive experience was in behavioral health, leading marketing for a multi-state operator. That experience shapes how we treat intake, scheduling, and admissions as part of the marketing operation. It is prior experience, not agency hospital work, and we label it that way wherever it appears.
Service-Line Priorities Come First
A health system does not have one marketing problem. Orthopedics, cardiology, primary care, urgent care, women's health, oncology, and behavioral health each have their own demand patterns, referral paths, capacity constraints, and payer considerations. A plan that treats them as one campaign hides the differences that matter.
We start by agreeing which service lines come first and why. The criteria are practical.
* Capacity to accept new patients within a reasonable scheduling window. Generating demand for a service line that cannot schedule it creates frustration, not acquisition.
* Strategic priority set by your leadership, including growth targets, new locations, and programs that need volume to sustain.
* A measurable access path. The service line needs a way for a patient to act, such as online scheduling, a call center queue, or a referral form, that we can measure.
* Competitive intensity in each market, which affects cost and the content needed to earn consideration.
Each prioritized service line gets its own plan: audience, messaging reviewed by the people responsible for clinical accuracy, channels, landing experience, and conversion definition. The plans share standards so leadership can compare them, but they are not copies of each other.
Local Access Points, Not Just a Brand
Patients search locally. They look for a service near them, check whether a location offers it, and want hours, directions, and a way to schedule. Health system marketing works when every location is accurate and findable, not when the system brand alone is strong.
That means location pages that state which services are offered at each site, accurate business listings for each location and, where appropriate, each department, consistent names, addresses, and phone numbers, and scheduling paths that lead to the right queue. Provider directories need the same care, because a listing that points to a provider who is not accepting patients wastes the inquiry.
We design [multi-location marketing](/problems/scale-multi-location-campaigns) around shared standards and local market needs, so a system can compare performance without forcing every site into the same campaign. Our [website development](/services/web-development) work preserves useful existing URLs and builds location structure that supports both patients and search engines, and our [technical SEO](/services/web-development/technical-seo-and-performance) work covers the structured data and site architecture that make location information legible.
Multi-Site Reporting Your Leadership Can Compare
Reporting across a health system fails when each site or service line defines success differently. We agree conversion definitions before the first campaign runs, and we use the same definitions everywhere.
A conversion might be a scheduled appointment, a completed first visit, an admission, or a referral received, depending on what your systems can share with marketing. We report conversion value per cost, where higher is better, alongside the counts, so leadership can compare a high-volume service line with a low-volume, high-value one on the same basis.
We distinguish measured outcomes from estimates. Where a step happens offline or in a system we cannot connect to, we label the gap rather than present a modeled number as an observed one. Our [analytics and attribution](/services/analytics-and-attribution) work defines identifier handling, deduplication, and the limits of the data, and our [intake attribution](/services/call-tracking-and-intake-attribution) work covers what happens after a call reaches your access center.
We propose weekly operating checks and a monthly executive review, with the cadence defined in your engagement. The monthly review covers spend, acquisition quality, conversion by service line and location, and the next decisions.
Review Roles and Governance We Plan Around
Hospitals and health systems review marketing vendors carefully, and they should. We plan around that review rather than treating it as an obstacle.
During scoping we document who approves what. Marketing leadership typically owns strategy and budget. Privacy and compliance review data handling and tracking. IT and security review integrations and access. Clinical or service-line leaders review claims about services and outcomes. Legal reviews terms and public statements. Procurement manages the vendor process.
We expect to complete security questionnaires, vendor risk reviews, and data-handling documentation, and we prepare those materials from our own operating records. Whether a business associate agreement is required for a given scope is a decision for your privacy office and counsel, and we work within that determination. These are requirements we expect to meet, not credentials we claim to hold.
Data handling and governance belong in the planning discussion because they shape which information can move between systems. We work within your review process, with responsibilities and deployment boundaries defined before implementation.
Integration Discovery Before Integration
We do not claim electronic health record integrations. Where a connection to scheduling, call center, or CRM systems is possible and useful, we define it in discovery with your IT owner, and we document the direction data moves, the fields involved, and the approvals required.
Discovery covers the website content system, scheduling tools, the call center platform, any CRM or marketing automation platform, analytics, and call tracking. For each, we ask what connections exist today, what data can be shared with marketing, and who approves changes. The result is an integration map that shows which steps can be automated and which stay manual.
Our [workflow integrations](/services/web-development/integrations-and-tracking) work connects the systems you already operate; it does not replace them. Where automation supports intake, reporting, or follow-up, our [customizable AI](/ai-edge/customizable-ai) work scopes it with a human approval step and an acceptance test before anything goes live.
Data Handling and Deployment Boundaries
Marketing data is not clinical data, and we keep the boundary visible. The data a marketing workflow needs, such as inquiry source, service line of interest, location, and scheduling outcome, is defined per engagement and approved by your reviewers. Clinical records stay in your clinical systems.
Supported workloads run on infrastructure we own and operate, which gives us direct oversight of data handling during processing. That does not mean every integration runs offline or that every external service disappears; ad platforms, analytics, call tracking, and your own systems remain part of delivery. Our [owned infrastructure](/ai-edge/owned-ai-systems) and [local processing](/ai-edge/local-processing) pages describe what runs where, and our [data sovereignty](/ai-edge/data-sovereignty) page explains how storage, processing, access, and outside providers are made explicit for your review.
Our [AI governance](/ai-edge/ai-governance) approach combines access controls, testing before deployment, human review at defined decision points, and clear accountability. Those controls support responsible execution without promising that technology alone resolves every security or compliance obligation.
Specialties Within the System
Behavioral health and mental health service lines carry additional sensitivity: platform advertising policies for these categories, families researching under stress, and intake processes that determine whether an inquiry becomes care. Our founder's operator experience informs how we approach [behavioral health marketing](/who-we-help/behavioral-health) and [mental health](/who-we-help/mental-health) services within a larger system, with the constraints surrounding sensitive services considered from the start.
The same operating disciplines apply to other service lines. The specialty depth does not define the whole engagement; it means the sensitive parts are handled by people who have run them.
How We Would Start
The first step is a focused conversation about where growth is getting stuck and what your leadership team needs to see. From there we propose a scoped review: service-line selection, an access-point audit across locations, a measurement baseline, and an integration discovery with your IT owner.
We recommend starting with a small number of service lines and locations, agreeing the standards, and expanding once the reporting holds up. That sequence respects your review process and gives leadership comparable results before the program grows.
Bring the business problem. We will start there.
