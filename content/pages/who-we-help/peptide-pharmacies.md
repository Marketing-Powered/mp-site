Marketing for Pharmacies With Eligible Peptide Offerings
Demand for peptide therapies can outpace a pharmacy's own documentation of what it may lawfully prepare and dispense. Peptide pharmacy marketing works when it is built on a current, product-by-product eligibility register, and it breaks when it is built on a category assumption. Every ad account, landing page and partner description inherits the accuracy of that register.

Define the pharmacy offering before you fund demand
Start with a working session that puts the right documents on one table: state licenses for every jurisdiction you ship into, facility classification, nonresident pharmacy registrations, current certification scope, supplier certificates of analysis, and the formulary as it exists today. The people in that room are usually the pharmacist in charge, the compliance officer, the business development lead and outside regulatory counsel. Marketing attends to listen and to record decisions, not to pitch campaigns.
The output is an eligibility register that sorts every offering into three buckets rather than one.
* Approved products. What it covers: FDA-approved drugs the pharmacy dispenses on a valid prescription. Marketing posture: Factual product and service information.
* Conditionally compounded. What it covers: Preparations with a documented basis for the substance, form and route, confirmed by qualified review. Marketing posture: Scope-matched description only, no approval language.
* Unsupported. What it covers: Anything without a current documented basis, including research-use-only material. Marketing posture: Not promoted, not referenced in acquisition assets.
That table is an illustrative structure for organizing a register, not legal advice or a statement about any specific pharmacy. Each row in a real register names the product, the facility classification it sits under, the supporting source, the reviewer and the review date. Anything without a named reviewer and a date stays out of promotion until qualified review says otherwise. Keep 503A and 503B distinct in every claim
HHS has issued an interim enforcement policy describing circumstances in which it does not intend to take enforcement action against a state-licensed pharmacy, federal facility or licensed physician compounding with six peptide bulk drug substances listed in the policy appendix: BPC-157, Epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, Semax and TB-500 ([HHS enforcement discretion policy](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/hhs-503a-peptide-enforcement-discretion-policy.pdf)). The policy applies while the 503A bulks list is developed through rulemaking, lists ten circumstances that must all be present, and states that compounded drugs are not FDA-approved ([HHS enforcement discretion policy](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/hhs-503a-peptide-enforcement-discretion-policy.pdf)). HHS also states it may revisit the interim policy, in whole or in part, if a safety signal or product quality issue arises ([HHS enforcement discretion policy](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/hhs-503a-peptide-enforcement-discretion-policy.pdf)). FDA makes the same underlying point about compounding generally: compounded drugs are not FDA approved, so the agency does not review their safety, effectiveness or quality before marketing ([FDA human drug compounding](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/guidance-compliance-regulatory-information/human-drug-compounding)).
The policy addresses section 503A compounding and should not be read as 503B permission. LegitScript's analysis states that 503B outsourcing facilities are not covered and that the policy does not create a new allowance for those facilities to compound the six referenced peptides ([LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/healthcare/hhs-peptide-compounding-policy-what-healthcare-businesses-need-to-know/)). If your organization runs both a 503A pharmacy and a 503B arm, separate the register, the domains and the claim library before a campaign goes live. Align certification and channel scope to each reviewer
Lawful operation, product status, certification, channel eligibility and account status are five separate questions. A pharmacy can be correctly licensed and still be ineligible to advertise a specific product on a specific platform, because each reviewer applies its own criteria to the entity, the domain and the offer.
LegitScript updated its certification review approach effective November 2, 2026 to reflect the policy's conditional allowances, and its review considers the specific ingredient, formulation, route of administration, compounding facility and supply chain compliance ([LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/healthcare/hhs-peptide-compounding-policy-what-healthcare-businesses-need-to-know/)). LegitScript also states that its updates do not change the independent requirements of advertising platforms or payment providers, and advises businesses to confirm each platform's current requirements and effective dates directly ([LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/healthcare/hhs-peptide-compounding-policy-what-healthcare-businesses-need-to-know/)).
Practical alignment looks like this: one legal entity mapped to one reviewed domain, a product list that matches the reviewed scope, a landing page set that promotes nothing outside that list, and a named owner for every platform and processor account. Support patient and prescriber discovery with factual access
Most pharmacy search demand is logistical rather than clinical. Patients want to know which states you ship to, whether you accept a prescription from their clinician, how transfers work, what turnaround looks like, how temperature-sensitive preparations are shipped, and who answers the phone. Prescribers want the same answers in a different format: product categories, sourcing and testing practice stated plainly, how to send a prescription, and who to call with a question.
Build those pages on verifiable facts: licensure by state, facility classification, pharmacist leadership, hours, contact routes, and product categories described at the category level. Keep dosing, reconstitution and administration guidance out of public marketing. That is clinical communication between a prescriber and a patient.
The expensive gap is often intake rather than traffic. Consider a plausible scenario: calls ring out during posted hours, the prescriber form requires an account before anyone sees it, and a transfer request produces no confirmation step. In an ad platform report, all three look identical to a targeting problem, because they appear as spend with no recorded return. Control claims across partner and marketplace pages
Your largest claim risk often sits on a website you do not own. The HHS policy matters not only to compounders but to businesses that facilitate prescribing, sale or dispensing through them, including telehealth providers ([LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/healthcare/hhs-peptide-compounding-policy-what-healthcare-businesses-need-to-know/)). Telehealth platforms, prescriber groups, affiliate marketplaces and wellness clinics describe the pharmacy relationship in their own words, and those words can expand the offer beyond what the register supports. Partner descriptions can create exposure for the pharmacy as well as the partner.
The practical control is an approved partner-description library: short, pre-cleared descriptions of the pharmacy, its licensure footprint, its facility classification and its product categories, written so a partner can use them without editing. Pair it with contract language that requires the approved descriptions, prohibits implying approval status for compounded preparations, and sets a review cadence whenever the register changes. Add a scheduled check of partner pages so business development finds a drifting claim before a platform reviewer does. A library reduces drift; it does not substitute for qualified review of each arrangement. Measure fulfillment, not form fills
Lead counts say little about a pharmacy's economics. The meaningful sequence is inquiry, qualified inquiry, prescription received, prescription verified, first fill, refill, and partner-referred volume by referring entity. Compare that chain against spend by channel and campaign, and campaign rankings often change.
Treat this as a working hypothesis rather than a measured statistic: the cheapest source of inquiries can be the most expensive source of filled prescriptions, because part of the demand it captures sits outside what the register says the pharmacy can serve. A cost-per-lead report cannot show that. A report built on cost per verified prescription, plus the share of inquiries the register allows you to accept, can.

FAQ
Q: Does the 2026 policy apply to every compounding facility?
A: No. It describes circumstances in which HHS does not intend to act against a state-licensed pharmacy, federal facility or licensed physician compounding with the six listed peptide bulk drug substances, when all ten circumstances are present ([HHS enforcement discretion policy](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/hhs-503a-peptide-enforcement-discretion-policy.pdf)). LegitScript states that 503B outsourcing facilities are not covered ([LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/healthcare/hhs-peptide-compounding-policy-what-healthcare-businesses-need-to-know/)). Confirm your classification with qualified counsel before publishing any claim.
Q: Can a compounded product be described as FDA-approved?
A: No. Compounded drugs are not FDA approved, which means FDA does not review their safety, effectiveness or quality before marketing ([FDA human drug compounding](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/guidance-compliance-regulatory-information/human-drug-compounding)). The HHS policy is an interim enforcement position issued while the 503A bulks list is developed through rulemaking, not an approval ([HHS enforcement discretion policy](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/hhs-503a-peptide-enforcement-discretion-policy.pdf)).
Q: Does certification cover all pharmacy products?
A: No. LegitScript reviews the specific ingredient, formulation, route of administration, compounding facility and supply chain compliance, and offerings outside the policy that are not otherwise eligible remain a barrier to certification ([LegitScript](https://www.legitscript.com/healthcare/hhs-peptide-compounding-policy-what-healthcare-businesses-need-to-know/)). Confirm scope with the certification body before adding a product line, domain or entity.
Q: How should partner websites describe pharmacy relationships?
A: Factually and within scope: pharmacy name, licensure footprint, facility classification and product categories, using text from an approved library. Partners should not characterize approval status, clinical outcomes or availability of offerings the register does not support, and should update their pages when the register changes.
Q: What evidence is needed before promoting an offering?
A: Current licensure and facility classification, a product-by-product eligibility register with named sources and review dates, documentation of current certification scope, and the approved partner-description library. Because the HHS position is interim and HHS states it may revisit the policy ([HHS enforcement discretion policy](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/hhs-503a-peptide-enforcement-discretion-policy.pdf)), recheck primary sources on a set schedule and immediately before any launch, and obtain qualified regulatory review of each compounding arrangement.
